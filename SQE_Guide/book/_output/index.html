SQE1 FLK1 — a study text

Published

4 September 2026

How to use this text

This is a study text for SQE1 FLK1, written against the SRA’s SQE1 Assessment Specification applicable to assessments from 1 September 2026.

What this text is for

SQE1 is not an essay exam. It is 360 single-best-answer multiple choice questions across two papers — 180 in FLK1, 180 in FLK2 — at roughly 1.7 minutes each, and it tests whether you can apply a rule to a client’s facts rather than whether you can recite it. This text is written to that end. Every section states the rule, explains why it exists, works an example where the rule is mechanical, names the discrimination the examiner is most likely to build a question around, and then makes you do questions in the exam’s own format.

Four conventions run throughout.

Exam trap

The wrong answer that looks right. These mark the places where a plausible-sounding option is offered and most candidates take it.

Verify before relying on this

A point that turns on law which has moved recently. Check it against a current primary source before you commit it to memory, and check it against your sitting’s cut-off date.

Worked example

A rule applied to facts, step by step, where the mechanics matter more than the statement.

Check yourself

Every substantive section closes with SQE-style single best answer questions. Answers and full explanations sit inside a collapsed block immediately below. Do not open it until you have committed to an answer — recognising the right answer when you see it is not the skill being assessed.

How the questions in this text are built

Each question follows the SRA’s own format:

  • A short factual scenario, usually naming a client and a solicitor.
  • A single question stem.
  • Five options, A to E. Exactly one is correct.
  • No negative marking, so never leave one blank.

The wrong options are not filler. Each is built on a specific error a real candidate makes: the right rule applied to the wrong party, the correct answer for a different vehicle, the pre-reform position, or the commercially attractive answer that the law does not support. The explanations say which error each option encodes, because knowing why an option is wrong is what transfers to the next question.

The examinable law cut-off

This matters more than it first appears. The assessment specification states the rule:

The cut off date for the law upon which candidates are examined in the SQE will be four calendar months prior to the date of the first assessment in an assessment window. Candidates will be tested on the law as it stands at that date. They will not be tested on the development of the law. For the avoidance of doubt, changes in the law which are implemented on the calendar date four calendar months prior to the first SQE assessment in an assessment window may be examined.

Three things follow.

Work it out yourself, from your own window. SQE1 runs in January and July. Take the first day of your assessment window — not the day you personally sit — and count back four calendar months. A window opening on, say, 11 January gives a cut-off of 11 September the previous year. The cut-off is the same for everyone in the window.

Changes on the cut-off date itself count. The specification says so expressly. Anything implemented after it does not, however prominent.

Learning the newest reform can lose you marks. This is genuinely counter-intuitive, and it is why the corporate transparency, insolvency and business tax reforms currently in progress are dangerous revision territory. Where this text discusses a reform that is still commencing it says so explicitly and states which position is the examinable one.

Confirm your own cut-off

Check the first day of your assessment window on the SQE website and apply the four-month rule to it. Do not rely on a date quoted in any textbook, this one included — including the illustrative one above.

What the specification says about tax figures

The specification is explicit, and candidates consistently over-prepare here:

Where candidates are required to apply the monetary value of any exemptions and reliefs, particular rates or thresholds, these will be provided.

Read that as permission to stop memorising the Budget. What you must know cold is the mechanics — which tax applies, to whom, on what, in what order the computation runs, and which relief is available on which facts. The numbers will be handed to you; knowing that a disposal qualifies for business asset disposal relief will not be. 6  Taxation of the business and its stakeholders is organised on that basis: mechanics in the body, figures in clearly marked boxes you can check against your sitting.

What the specification says about case names

Also explicit, and also over-prepared:

On occasion in legal practice a case name or statutory provision is … the term normally used to describe a legal principle or an area of law, or a rule or procedural step (e.g. Rylands v Fletcher, CPR Part 36, Section 25 notice). In such circumstances, candidates are required to know and be able to use such case names … In all other circumstances candidates are not required to recall specific case names, or cite statutory or regulatory authorities.

So Salomon and Prest earn their place — they are the names practitioners use for the principles. Cases are given throughout this text because they make a rule memorable and show you its limits, not because you will be asked to name them. Learn the proposition; the citation is scaffolding. Statutory section numbers are given for the same reason, and for a handful of provisions — s.994, s.214, s.168 — the number really is the working shorthand.

Ethics is not a separate subject

Professional conduct is examined pervasively across both papers, not in a chapter of its own. An otherwise straightforward company law question can turn on the SRA Principles or the Code of Conduct. This text raises conduct where it arises, because that is how it will reach you.

Two things decide a surprising number of questions:

  1. The ordering of the Principles. Where two or more Principles conflict, those which safeguard the wider public interest — the rule of law, and the proper administration of justice — take precedence over an individual client’s interests.
  2. Identifying the client. In business scenarios the company is a person distinct from its directors and shareholders. A great many conduct questions are really asking whether you noticed who the solicitor actually acts for.

The specification also flags that scenarios may be drawn from SRA warning notices, naming those on non-disclosure agreements and strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs).

Coverage and honest limits

This text covers Business Law and Practice — the largest single subject in FLK1 — to the depth the specification requires. It does not cover the other FLK1 subjects, and it excludes what the specification excludes: the Listing, Prospectus, Disclosure Guidance and Transparency Rules, and any other FCA, London Stock Exchange, market rules or codes.

It is written to be right. Where the law has moved between drafting and your sitting, or where a provision is commencing in stages, the text flags it rather than smoothing it over. Statute references are to the legislation as amended; case citations are given in full on first use in each chapter, and collected in 8  Authorities and numbers.
