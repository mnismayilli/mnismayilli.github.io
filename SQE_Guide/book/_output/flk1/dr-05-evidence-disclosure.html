13  Proving the case

Assessment objective 6: the evidence needed and disclosure steps required in commencing, responding to, progressing or defending a claim.

13.1 The framework of civil evidence

13.1.1 Relevance and admissibility

Evidence is admissible if it is relevant to a fact in issue — that is, if it makes the existence of that fact more or less probable — and is not excluded by a rule. Civil evidence is far more permissive than criminal evidence: hearsay is admissible, and the court’s principal tools are weight and case management rather than exclusion.

CPR 32.1 gives the court a broad power to control evidence: it may give directions as to the issues on which it requires evidence, the nature of the evidence it requires, and the way in which it is to be placed before the court; it may exclude evidence that would otherwise be admissible; and it may limit cross-examination.

13.1.2 Burden and standard of proof

  • The legal burden rests on the party who asserts — normally the claimant on the elements of the claim, and the defendant on any positive defence (contributory negligence, limitation, frustration, illegality).
  • The standard is the balance of probabilities: more likely than not. There is no sliding scale for serious allegations, though the inherent improbability of an allegation is a matter the court may take into account in deciding whether it has been proved.
  • The evidential burden may shift during a trial, and some rules assist a claimant — res ipsa loquitur in negligence, and the presumption in s.11 Civil Evidence Act 1968 that a person convicted of an offence committed it.

13.1.3 Hearsay

Hearsay is a statement made otherwise than by a person giving oral evidence in the proceedings, which is tendered as evidence of the matters stated.

Section 1 Civil Evidence Act 1995: hearsay is not to be excluded in civil proceedings on the ground that it is hearsay. The question is one of weight, not admissibility.

Three consequences follow:

  • Notice (s.2 CEA 1995 and CPR 33.2). A party who intends to rely on hearsay must give notice. Where the hearsay is contained in a witness statement of a person not being called, the party must inform the other parties when serving the statement and give the reason the witness will not be called. Failure to give notice does not make the evidence inadmissible, but the court may take it into account in weighing the evidence and in costs.
  • Calling the maker (s.3 CEA 1995 and CPR 33.4). Where the maker of the hearsay statement is not called, another party may apply for permission to call that person for cross-examination on the statement.
  • Weight (s.4 CEA 1995). The court considers whether it would have been reasonable and practicable to call the maker; whether the statement was made contemporaneously; whether it involves multiple hearsay; whether any person had a motive to conceal or misrepresent; whether the original statement was edited or made in collaboration for a particular purpose; and whether the circumstances suggest an attempt to prevent proper evaluation of its weight.
Exam trap

The instinct carried over from criminal law — that hearsay is inadmissible unless an exception applies — is exactly wrong in civil proceedings. An option stating that a witness statement from an absent witness is “inadmissible because it is hearsay” is wrong. The correct analysis is that it is admissible, that notice should be given, that the other party may apply to call the maker for cross-examination, and that the court will attach less weight to it because it has not been tested.

13.2 Witness evidence

13.2.1 Witness statements

A witness statement is a signed statement containing the evidence which that person would be allowed to give orally (CPR 32.4). At trial it stands as the witness’s evidence in chief unless the court orders otherwise (CPR 32.5), and the witness is then tendered for cross-examination.

Requirements under Practice Direction 32:

  • It must be in the witness’s own words and, so far as practicable, in their own language, expressed in the first person.
  • It must state the source of any matters of information or belief.
  • It must not contain argument, opinion (unless the witness is an expert or the opinion is one a lay witness may give, such as speed or identity), or extensive commentary on documents.
  • It must be verified by a statement of truth, with the contempt of court warning.

Consequences of failure to serve. Under CPR 32.10, if a witness statement is not served within the time specified by the court, the witness may not be called to give oral evidence unless the court gives permission — which requires relief from sanctions.

PD 57AC — trial witness statements in the Business and Property Courts

For trials in the Business and Property Courts, Practice Direction 57AC imposes additional requirements: the statement must set out only matters of fact of which the witness has personal knowledge that are relevant to the issues; it must identify by list the documents the witness has referred to or been referred to for the purpose of providing the evidence; the witness must sign a confirmation of compliance; and the party’s legal representative must endorse a certificate of compliance. Breach can lead to the statement being struck out, withdrawn or redrafted, and to adverse costs orders.

13.2.2 Affidavits

An affidavit is a sworn statement, made on oath or affirmation before a person authorised to administer it. Affidavits are more formal and more expensive than witness statements, and are required only where a rule, practice direction or court order says so — most importantly for applications for freezing injunctions and search orders, and in contempt proceedings. A party may choose to use an affidavit instead of a witness statement, but will not recover the extra cost unless the court orders otherwise.

13.2.3 Compelling attendance

A witness summons (CPR 34) requires a witness to attend court to give evidence or to produce documents. It is issued by the court, and is binding if served at least seven days before the date on which the witness is required to attend — the court may permit shorter service. The party serving it must offer or pay a sum reasonably sufficient to cover the witness’s travel expenses and compensation for loss of time. Failure to comply is a contempt of court.

13.3 Expert evidence

13.3.1 The controls

Opinion evidence is generally inadmissible; s.3 Civil Evidence Act 1972 makes an expert’s opinion on a relevant matter admissible where the expert is qualified to give it.

Part 35 exists to restrain the cost and partisanship of expert evidence, and three rules do the work:

  • CPR 35.1: expert evidence shall be restricted to that which is reasonably required to resolve the proceedings.
  • CPR 35.4: no party may call an expert or put in an expert’s report without the court’s permission. When applying, the party must identify the field, the issues the evidence will address, where practicable the expert’s name, and an estimate of the costs.
  • CPR 35.3 — the overriding duty: it is the duty of an expert to help the court on matters within their expertise, and that duty overrides any obligation to the person from whom the expert has received instructions or by whom they are paid.
Exam trap

Permission under CPR 35.4 is the single most examined point in this topic. A party who instructs an expert without first obtaining permission has spent money it may never recover, and cannot rely on the report. An option saying “the party may adduce the report because it obtained it before proceedings” is wrong. Note the further distinction: a shadow or advisory expert, instructed to advise the party rather than to give evidence, needs no permission and is protected by litigation privilege — but cannot be relied on at trial.

13.3.2 The report

Practice Direction 35 prescribes the contents: the expert’s qualifications; the literature and material relied on; a statement of who carried out any test or experiment; the substance of all material instructions, written and oral, on the basis of which the report was written; a summary of the range of opinion where there is one, and reasons for the expert’s own opinion; a summary of conclusions; and a statement that the expert understands and has complied with their duty to the court. It must be verified by a statement of truth in the prescribed form.

Exam trap

The substance of the instructions stated in the report is not privileged, and CPR 35.10(4) provides that the court will not order disclosure of any specific document or permit cross-examination about the instructions unless it is satisfied there are reasonable grounds to consider the statement of instructions to be inaccurate or incomplete. So instructions are partially exposed, but the threshold for going behind the summary is high.

13.3.3 Written questions and discussions

CPR 35.6 — written questions. A party may put written questions to the other side’s expert (or to a single joint expert). Unless the court orders or the other party agrees otherwise, the questions may be put once only, must be put within 28 days of service of the report, and must be for the purpose of clarification of the report. The answers form part of the report. If the expert does not answer, the court may direct that the instructing party may not rely on the evidence, or may not recover the expert’s fees.

CPR 35.12 — discussions between experts. The court may direct a discussion between experts to identify and discuss the issues, and where possible to reach agreement. The court may specify the issues. The experts must prepare a statement for the court showing the issues on which they agree and those on which they disagree, with a summary of the reasons.

Two rules about that discussion are reliably tested:

  • The content of the discussion is not to be referred to at trial unless the parties agree.
  • Where the experts reach agreement, that agreement does not bind the parties unless they expressly agree to be bound by it.

13.3.4 Single joint experts

CPR 35.7: where two or more parties wish to submit expert evidence on a particular issue, the court may direct that it be given by a single joint expert. If the parties cannot agree who, the court may select from a list prepared by the parties, or direct the manner of selection.

CPR 35.8: each instructing party may give instructions to the expert, copying them to the other parties. Each instructing party is jointly and severally liable for the expert’s fees.

Single joint experts are the norm on the fast track and on quantum issues, and are less common where a central issue in a high-value dispute turns on contested expert opinion.

Concurrent evidence (“hot-tubbing”) is permitted under PD 35: the court may direct that experts give their evidence together, with the judge putting questions and the experts commenting on each other’s answers.

13.4 Disclosure and inspection

13.4.1 The concepts

Disclosure is stating that a document exists or has existed (CPR 31.2). Inspection is the separate right to look at it and take a copy (CPR 31.3). A party may disclose a document but withhold inspection — on grounds of privilege, or because inspection would be disproportionate, or because the party no longer has it.

A document is “anything in which information of any description is recorded” (CPR 31.4) — which plainly includes emails, text messages, metadata, databases and voicemails.

A party discloses documents which are or have been in its control (CPR 31.8): documents which it has or has had physical possession of, or has or has had a right to possession of, or has or has had a right to inspect or take copies of.

13.4.2 Standard disclosure

CPR 31.6: standard disclosure requires a party to disclose only:

  1. the documents on which it relies;
  2. the documents which adversely affect its own case, adversely affect another party’s case, or support another party’s case; and
  3. the documents which it is required to disclose by a relevant practice direction.
Exam trap

Standard disclosure is not a duty to disclose “everything relevant”. It is deliberately narrower — and, crucially, it is not confined to helpful documents. A party must disclose documents that damage its own case. That is the whole point, and it is the source of most of the questions: the client who says “I’m not producing that email, it’s fatal” must be told that they have no choice.

The search (CPR 31.7). A party must make a reasonable search. What is reasonable depends on the number of documents involved, the nature and complexity of the proceedings, the ease and expense of retrieval, and the significance of any document likely to be located. A party who has not searched a category or class of document on the ground that to do so would be unreasonable must say so in the disclosure statement.

The list and the statement (CPR 31.10). Disclosure is made by serving a list of documents in form N265, in three parts: documents in the party’s control which it does not object to the other side inspecting; documents in its control which it objects to producing (privileged documents, described by class); and documents which are no longer in its control, with an explanation of what became of them. The list includes a disclosure statement setting out the extent of the search, certifying that the party understands the duty to disclose, and stating that to the best of their knowledge they have carried it out.

The continuing duty (CPR 31.11). The duty of disclosure continues until the proceedings are concluded. A party who later comes into possession of a disclosable document must notify the other parties immediately.

13.4.3 Orders for disclosure

  • Specific disclosure (CPR 31.12): an order requiring a party to disclose specified documents or classes of documents, to carry out a search to the extent stated, or to disclose any documents located as a result. It is used where the applicant believes standard disclosure has been inadequate, and must be supported by evidence.
  • Pre-action disclosure (s.33 Senior Courts Act 1981, s.52 County Courts Act 1984, CPR 31.16). The court may order disclosure before proceedings where the applicant and respondent are likely to be parties to subsequent proceedings, the documents fall within standard disclosure, and disclosure is desirable in order to dispose fairly of the anticipated proceedings, to assist the dispute to be resolved without proceedings, or to save costs. The applicant normally pays the costs.
  • Non-party disclosure (s.34 Senior Courts Act 1981, CPR 31.17). Available only after proceedings have started. The documents must be likely to support the applicant’s case or adversely affect the case of one of the other parties, and disclosure must be necessary to dispose fairly of the claim or to save costs. Again, the applicant normally pays.
  • A Norwich Pharmacal order is a distinct jurisdiction, compelling a person mixed up in wrongdoing (however innocently) to disclose information identifying the wrongdoer, so that the applicant can bring a claim.

13.4.4 Electronic disclosure

Practice Direction 31B applies to electronic documents. The parties must discuss the scope of electronic disclosure before the first case management conference — the categories of documents, the date ranges, the custodians, the search terms and the format of exchange — and may be required to complete an Electronic Documents Questionnaire. Keyword searching, de-duplication and technology-assisted review are expressly contemplated. The overriding consideration is proportionality.

PD 57AD — Disclosure in the Business and Property Courts

Since 1 October 2022, disclosure in the Business and Property Courts is governed by Practice Direction 57AD, which replaced the disclosure pilot in PD 51U. It is a different regime from CPR Part 31, and the differences are structural:

  • Initial Disclosure: key documents relied on and necessary for the other party to understand the case must be served with the statements of case, subject to limits.

  • Extended Disclosure requires a party to identify Issues for Disclosure and to request one of five Models:

    Model Effect
    A Disclosure confined to known adverse documents
    B Limited disclosure — key documents plus known adverse documents
    C Request-led search-based disclosure of particular documents or narrow classes
    D Narrow search-based disclosure, with or without narrative documents — the closest to standard disclosure
    E Wide search-based disclosure, including documents that may lead to a train of inquiry

  • A Disclosure Review Document is completed jointly, and there is a free-standing duty to disclose known adverse documents regardless of the model ordered.

Outside the Business and Property Courts — which is where most claims are — CPR Part 31 and standard disclosure still apply. Read the question carefully to see which court you are in.

13.5 Privilege

Privilege is a right to withhold inspection of a document that must still be disclosed (listed by class in part 2 of the list). It belongs to the client, not the solicitor, and only the client can waive it.

13.5.1 Legal advice privilege

Confidential communications between a lawyer and client, made for the dominant purpose of giving or receiving legal advice. It applies whether or not litigation is contemplated.

  • Three Rivers District Council v Bank of England (No 5) [2003] EWCA Civ 474 gave “client” a narrow meaning: only those employees specifically tasked with seeking and receiving legal advice on the corporation’s behalf. Communications between the lawyer and other employees are not covered.
  • Three Rivers District Council v Bank of England (No 6) [2004] UKHL 48 gave “legal advice” a wide meaning: it extends beyond advice on legal rights and liabilities to advice as to what should prudently and sensibly be done in the relevant legal context — including presentational advice before an inquiry.

13.5.2 Litigation privilege

Confidential communications between a lawyer and client, or between either of them and a third party, where:

  • litigation is in progress or in reasonable contemplation; and

  • the communication was made for the dominant purpose of that litigation — conducting it, obtaining evidence or advice about it.

  • Waugh v British Railways Board [1980] AC 521 established the dominant purpose test: an internal report prepared for two purposes of equal weight — railway safety and litigation — was not privileged.

  • Serious Fraud Office v Eurasian Natural Resources Corporation Ltd [2018] EWCA Civ 2006 held that a criminal investigation may make litigation reasonably in contemplation, and that documents created to obtain legal advice about how to avoid or settle contemplated litigation fall within the privilege.

Exam trap

Litigation privilege covers third party communications — an expert’s report, a witness proof, an investigator’s findings — where legal advice privilege does not. That is why a shadow expert instructed to advise rather than to give evidence is protected, and why an accident report prepared for the dominant purpose of litigation is protected while one prepared to improve safety is not.

13.5.3 Without prejudice communications

Communications made in a genuine attempt to settle an existing dispute cannot be put before the court. The rule rests on public policy and on the implied agreement of the parties.

  • It is the substance that matters, not the label. Writing “without prejudice” on an open assertion of claim does not protect it, and omitting the label from a genuine settlement offer does not destroy the protection.
  • Rush & Tompkins Ltd v Greater London Council [1989] AC 1280: the protection extends to communications with other parties to the same litigation, and survives the settlement of the dispute as against a party who was not involved in the negotiations.
  • Unilever plc v Procter & Gamble Co [2000] 1 WLR 2436 lists the exceptions: whether a settlement was concluded and on what terms; rectification of a settlement; estoppel; unambiguous impropriety (a threat or perjury under cover of the privilege); explaining delay; and an offer expressly made “without prejudice save as to costs”.
  • A “without prejudice save as to costs” (Calderbank) offer is protected until liability and quantum are decided, and may then be shown to the court on the question of costs.

13.5.4 Other privileges, and waiver

  • Privilege against self-incrimination: a party need not produce a document that would tend to expose them to criminal proceedings or a penalty.
  • Common interest privilege: a privileged document may be shared with a party having a common interest in the subject matter without loss of privilege.
  • Public interest immunity: documents may be withheld where disclosure would damage the public interest.

Waiver may be express or implied. Deploying part of a privileged document in the proceedings — for example, relying on part of a legal opinion to explain why a party acted as it did — will generally waive privilege in the whole of that document, and possibly in the transaction to which it relates. This is the rule against cherry-picking, and it is why partial deployment is dangerous. Privilege is not waived by an inadvertent disclosure where the mistake was obvious; CPR 31.20 provides that where a party inadvertently allows a privileged document to be inspected, the party who has inspected it may use it only with the permission of the court.

Exam trap

Privilege belongs to the client. A solicitor cannot waive it, and cannot disclose a privileged document because they think it would be fairer to do so. Equally, a solicitor who receives an obviously privileged document sent to them by mistake must stop reading it, notify the other side, and return or destroy it — and must not use it without the court’s permission.

13.6 The solicitor’s position

Advising on the duty of disclosure. The client must be told, in writing and at the outset, that disclosure requires production of documents that harm their case, that the duty is continuing, and that documents must be preserved. A solicitor who allows a client to destroy or withhold disclosable documents risks being party to a contempt and breaches the duty not to mislead the court.

Document preservation. As soon as litigation is contemplated, the client should be told to suspend routine document destruction — including automatic email deletion policies.

Not misleading the court. A solicitor must not make a statement to the court knowing it to be untrue, must not put forward evidence they know to be false, and must draw the court’s attention to relevant cases and statutory provisions likely to have a material effect on the outcome, even where they are against the client’s case.

Where the client’s account changes. If a client admits that a served witness statement is untrue, the solicitor cannot continue to rely on it. The client must be advised to correct it; if they refuse, the solicitor must cease to act, without disclosing the reason.

Witnesses. A solicitor must not coach a witness or rehearse evidence, and must not interfere with the evidence of any witness. Taking a proof of evidence is proper; suggesting what the witness should say is not.

13.7 Check yourself

Q1. A claimant wishes to rely at trial on a signed statement from a witness who has since emigrated and cannot be called. The defendant objects that the statement is hearsay.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The statement is inadmissible, because the witness cannot be cross-examined.

B. The statement is admissible: hearsay is not excluded in civil proceedings on the ground that it is hearsay. The claimant should give notice under CPR 33.2; the defendant may apply for permission to call the maker for cross-examination; and the court will assess weight under s.4 of the Civil Evidence Act 1995.

C. The statement is admissible only if the court gives permission under CPR 32.1.

D. The statement is inadmissible unless the claimant obtains a witness summons.

E. The statement is admissible only if it was made contemporaneously with the events described.

Q2. A company’s in-house legal team asks employees across the business for factual information about a transaction, in order to advise the board. Litigation is not yet in prospect. The employees’ responses are later sought on disclosure.

Are the responses protected by legal advice privilege?

A. Yes, because they were created for the purpose of obtaining legal advice.

B. Yes, because all communications within a company are privileged once lawyers are involved.

C. No, on the Three Rivers (No 5) approach: for legal advice privilege the “client” is confined to those employees tasked with seeking and receiving legal advice on the company’s behalf, and communications with other employees are not covered.

D. No, because legal advice privilege does not apply to in-house lawyers.

E. Yes, because litigation privilege applies whenever a company takes legal advice.

Q3. A party wishes to rely on the evidence of a consulting engineer. It obtained a detailed report before proceedings were issued and has now filed a directions questionnaire.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The party may rely on the report at trial, because it was obtained before proceedings began.

B. The party may rely on the report provided it serves it on the other side.

C. No party may call an expert or put in an expert’s report without the court’s permission (CPR 35.4); the party must apply, identifying the field, the issues to be addressed, the expert’s name where practicable, and an estimate of costs.

D. The report is inadmissible because it is opinion evidence.

E. Permission is required only where the party wishes to call the expert to give oral evidence.

Q4. During disclosure, a defendant’s solicitor identifies an internal email in which the defendant’s managing director admits that the goods supplied were substandard. The defendant instructs the solicitor not to include it in the list of documents.

What should the solicitor do?

A. Follow the client’s instructions, because privilege belongs to the client.

B. Include the email in part 2 of the list as a privileged document.

C. Advise the client that the email falls within standard disclosure under CPR 31.6(b) because it adversely affects the defendant’s own case, that it must be disclosed, and that if the client refuses the solicitor must cease to act.

D. Disclose the email without telling the client, because the duty to the court prevails.

E. Destroy the email, because it was created before proceedings were issued.

Q5. Two experts hold a discussion directed by the court under CPR 35.12 and reach agreement on the cost of remedial works. One party’s solicitor tells the client that the claim is now settled at that figure.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The advice is correct: an agreement between experts binds the parties.

B. The advice is incorrect: where experts reach agreement in a discussion, that agreement does not bind the parties unless they expressly agree to be bound by it.

C. The advice is correct, because the court directed the discussion.

D. The advice is incorrect, because expert discussions have no legal effect of any kind and the statement cannot be shown to the court.

E. The advice is correct, unless a party applies to set the agreement aside within 21 days.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. Section 1 Civil Evidence Act 1995 abolished the rule against hearsay in civil proceedings. The consequences are procedural and evidential rather than exclusionary: notice under s.2 and CPR 33.2, the right of the other party to apply under s.3 and CPR 33.4 to call the maker for cross-examination, and the s.4 weight factors — of which “whether it would have been reasonable and practicable to produce the maker” is the first, and emigration is a good answer to it.

  • A imports the criminal law instinct, which is the trap.
  • C confuses the court’s general power to control evidence with an admissibility requirement.
  • D misuses a witness summons, which compels attendance and cannot reach a person abroad.
  • E turns a weight factor under s.4 into an admissibility condition.

Q2 — C. Three Rivers (No 5). For the purposes of legal advice privilege the “client” is confined to those employees authorised to seek and receive legal advice for the corporation. Information gathered from other employees is not privileged on that basis. Litigation privilege would cover it — but only if litigation were in reasonable contemplation, which the facts exclude.

  • A and B apply an organisation-wide conception of the client that Three Rivers (No 5) rejected.
  • D is wrong: in-house lawyers attract legal advice privilege when acting in a legal capacity.
  • E invokes litigation privilege where there is no litigation in reasonable contemplation.

Q3 — C. CPR 35.4(1): no party may call an expert or put in evidence an expert’s report without the court’s permission. The application is normally made in the directions questionnaire or at the CMC, and CPR 35.4(2) prescribes what must be identified.

  • A and B overlook the permission requirement entirely, which is the most commonly missed point in Part 35.
  • D is wrong: s.3 Civil Evidence Act 1972 makes qualified expert opinion admissible.
  • E is the sophisticated distractor: permission is required to put in the report as well as to call the expert.

Q4 — C. CPR 31.6(b) requires disclosure of documents which adversely affect the disclosing party’s own case. That is exactly what this email does. The client has no choice, and the solicitor cannot serve a list they know to be incomplete without misleading the court. If the client will not comply, the solicitor must cease to act — without disclosing the reason, because it is confidential.

  • A confuses privilege, which the client owns, with the duty of disclosure, which the client cannot waive away.
  • B is a misuse of part 2 of the list. The email is not privileged; an internal commercial email is not a lawyer-client communication and no litigation privilege attaches to a document created in the ordinary course of business.
  • D breaches the duty of confidentiality and the duty to act on instructions; the answer is to advise, not to act unilaterally.
  • E would be destruction of evidence, a contempt, and potentially a criminal offence.

Q5 — B. CPR 35.12(5): where experts reach agreement in a discussion, that agreement does not bind the parties unless the parties expressly agree to be bound by it. The parties remain free to argue for a different figure, though doing so in the face of an agreed expert position carries obvious forensic and costs risk.

  • A, C and E all give the experts’ agreement a binding effect it does not have.
  • D overstates the point in the other direction: the content of the discussion may not be referred to at trial without the parties’ agreement (CPR 35.12(4)), but the joint statement of agreement and disagreement is produced precisely so that the court can see it.
