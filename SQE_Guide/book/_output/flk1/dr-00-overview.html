8  Dispute Resolution: what is examined

8.1 Assessment objectives

Candidates are required to apply relevant core legal principles and rules appropriately and effectively, at the level of a competent newly qualified solicitor in practice, to realistic client-based and ethical problems and situations in the following areas:

  1. Analysis of merits of claim or defence.
  2. Arbitration, mediation and litigation as an appropriate mechanism to resolve a dispute.
  3. Pre-action considerations and steps.
  4. Commencing, responding to or progressing a claim.
  5. Case management and any interim applications relevant to a claim.
  6. The evidence needed and disclosure steps required in commencing, responding to, progressing or defending a claim.
  7. Preparation of a case for a trial, the trial and any post trial steps.
  8. Procedures and processes relevant to costs involved in dispute resolution.

Candidates must demonstrate their ability to act honestly and with integrity and in accordance with the Statement of Solicitor Competence, the SRA Principles and the Code of Conduct.

Questions may draw on any combination of the subject areas within FLK1.

8.2 The examinable content, in the SRA’s own words

Different options for dispute resolution — the characteristics of arbitration, mediation and litigation which make them an appropriate mechanism to resolve a dispute.

Resolving a dispute through a civil claim — preliminary considerations: limitation, pre-action protocols; parties and causes of action; calculating limitation periods for claims in contract and tort; Practice Direction – Pre-action Conduct; principles and purpose of pre-action protocols governing particular claims and consequences for failure to follow their terms; applicable law — mechanisms to determine which country’s laws apply to a contractual or tortious claim issued in the courts of England and Wales; jurisdiction — mechanisms to determine jurisdiction over an international contractual or tortious claim.

Where to start proceedings — allocation of business between the High Court and the County Court; jurisdiction of the specialist courts.

Issuing and serving proceedings — issuing a claim form; adding, removing or substituting parties; service of a claim form within the jurisdiction; procedure for service outside the jurisdiction (with or without the court’s permission) and mechanisms for effecting valid service in another jurisdiction; deemed dates of service and time limits for serving proceedings; service by an alternative method.

Responding to a claim — admitting the claim; acknowledging service and filing a defence and/or counterclaim; disputing the court’s jurisdiction; entering and setting aside judgment in default; discontinuance and settlement; time limits for responding to a claim.

Statements of case — purpose, structure and content of a claim form, particulars of claim, or defence relating to a claim in contract or tort; purpose, structure and content of a reply, Part 20 claim, or defence to Part 20 claim; requests for further information; amendments.

Interim applications — procedure for making an application; purpose, procedure and evidence required for summary judgment, interim payments and interim injunctions.

Case management — the overriding objective; track allocation; case management directions for cases proceeding on the fast or multi-tracks; non-compliance with orders, sanctions and relief; costs and case management conferences.

Evidence — relevance, hearsay and admissibility; the burden and standard of proof; expert evidence (opinion evidence, duties of experts, single joint experts, discussion between experts); witness evidence (witness statements, affidavits).

Disclosure and inspection — standard disclosure; orders for disclosure; specific disclosure; pre-action and non-party disclosure; electronic disclosure; privilege and without prejudice communications; waiver of privilege.

Trial — summoning witnesses; preparations for trial (purpose of pre-trial checklists and hearings; purpose of trial bundles); trial procedure; the nature and effect of judgment.

Costs — costs management and budgeting; inter-partes costs orders (interim and final); non-party costs; qualified one-way costs shifting; Part 36 and other offers; security for costs; fixed and assessed costs.

Appeals — permission; destination of appeals; grounds for appeals.

Enforcement of money judgments — oral examination; methods of enforcement; procedure and mechanisms for effecting valid enforcement in another jurisdiction.

Exam trap

Notice what the specification does not say. There is no mention of the small claims track procedure in detail, of family or criminal procedure, or of judicial review. And notice what it does say that candidates routinely skip: applicable law and jurisdiction, non-party costs, enforcement abroad, and destination of appeals. Those four are small topics with a high question-per-page ratio, precisely because they are neglected.

8.4 The shape of a civil claim

Almost every Dispute Resolution question sits at one point on this timeline. Locating the question on it is usually half the work.

Stage What happens
Analysis Cause of action, parties, limitation, evidence, merits, funding, the defendant’s ability to pay
Pre-action Letter of claim, response, disclosure, ADR — under the relevant protocol or the Practice Direction
Issue Claim form issued; limitation stops
Service Claim form served within 4 months (6 outside the jurisdiction); particulars of claim
Response Acknowledgment of service within 14 days; defence within 14 or 28 days; counterclaim; or default judgment
Allocation Directions questionnaire; allocation to the small claims, fast, intermediate or multi-track
Case management Directions; costs budgets on the multi-track; CMC or CCMC; interim applications
Disclosure Lists of documents; inspection; privilege
Evidence Exchange of witness statements; expert reports; experts’ discussions
Pre-trial Pre-trial checklists; pre-trial review; trial bundle
Trial Openings, evidence, submissions, judgment, costs
After Appeal (21 days); enforcement

8.5 Six ideas that decide most questions

Limitation stops on issue, not service. The claim is brought when the claim form is received by the court, not when it is sealed and not when it reaches the defendant.

The court’s permission is the hinge. Service out, adding a party after limitation, amending after limitation, relief from sanctions, expert evidence, an appeal, distributing to unsecured creditors — each turns on whether permission is needed and on what test.

Every deadline has a consequence. Miss the deadline for filing a defence and the claimant may enter default judgment. Miss the deadline for a costs budget and you are treated as having filed one comprising only the court fees. Miss the deadline for service of the claim form and, in most cases, the claim is over.

Track allocation drives everything downstream. It decides the directions, the extent of expert evidence, the trial length, whether costs are budgeted, and whether costs are fixed or assessed.

Costs are a weapon, not an afterthought. Part 36, budgets, indemnity costs and QOCS are examined as tactical instruments, because that is how they are used.

Conduct is embedded. The duty not to mislead the court, the limits on what a solicitor may do with a client’s untruthful instructions, and the rules on undertakings surface constantly in litigation scenarios.

8.6 Check yourself

Q1. Which of the following is not part of the examinable content for Dispute Resolution?

A. The mechanisms for determining which country’s law applies to a tortious claim issued in the courts of England and Wales.

B. The grounds on which permission to appeal may be granted.

C. The procedure for applying for judicial review of a decision of a public body.

D. Qualified one-way costs shifting.

E. Pre-action and non-party disclosure.

Q2. A solicitor is asked at what point in the life of a claim the limitation clock is stopped.

Which of the following is correct?

A. When the letter of claim is sent under the relevant pre-action protocol.

B. When the claim form is received by the court.

C. When the claim form is sealed and issued by the court.

D. When the claim form is served on the defendant.

E. When the particulars of claim are served.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Judicial review is not within the Dispute Resolution content list. Constitutional and administrative law appears elsewhere in FLK1, but the procedural machinery of judicial review is not examined here. Each of the other four is named expressly in the specification.

Q2 — B. For limitation purposes a claim is brought when the claim form is received by the court office, not when it is issued. Practice Direction 7A makes this explicit, and it matters because a claim form delivered on the last day of the limitation period but not sealed until several days later is still in time.

  • A confuses the protocol stage with the commencement of proceedings; a letter of claim does not stop time.
  • C is the near-miss and the answer most candidates give. Issue is when proceedings start for the purposes of CPR 7.2; receipt is what stops the limitation clock.
  • D and E are later still — service can lawfully occur four months after issue.
