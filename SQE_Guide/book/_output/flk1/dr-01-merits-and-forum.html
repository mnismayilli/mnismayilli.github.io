9  Analysing the dispute and choosing the forum

Assessment objectives 1 and 2: analysis of merits of claim or defence; arbitration, mediation and litigation as an appropriate mechanism to resolve a dispute.

9.1 Analysing the merits

Before a solicitor advises anyone to sue, six questions have to be answered. A question that asks “what should the solicitor advise?” is usually testing whether you worked through them in order.

9.1.1 1. Is there a cause of action?

Identify the legal wrong and its elements, then check that the facts supply each one.

Cause of action Elements to establish
Breach of contract A contract existed (offer, acceptance, consideration, intention, certainty); its terms; breach of a term; causation; loss not too remote
Negligence Duty of care; breach; causation (factual and legal); loss of a recognised kind, not too remote
Other torts The elements of the specific tort — nuisance, Rylands v Fletcher, occupiers’ liability, product liability
Debt A liquidated sum due under a contract; no need to prove loss, and no duty to mitigate
Exam trap

A claim for a debt is not a claim for damages. The claimant need not prove loss, need not mitigate, and remoteness is irrelevant. That difference decides whether a defendant’s “you could have sold the goods elsewhere” argument goes anywhere, and it decides whether the claim is for a specified or an unspecified sum on the claim form.

9.1.2 2. Who are the correct parties?

Getting this wrong is expensive, because correcting it after limitation has expired requires the court’s permission on a restrictive test (Chapter 11).

Defendant Named as
Individual Full name
Sole trader “Name trading as Business Name”
Partnership The firm name, where the partners carried on business in that name when the cause of action accrued
Company Its full registered name, with “Limited” or “plc”
LLP Its full registered name
Deceased person’s estate The personal representatives
Child or protected party Through a litigation friend

Also consider: is there more than one potential defendant? Are they jointly and severally liable, so that the claimant may sue any of them for the whole? Is there a claim for a contribution under the Civil Liability (Contribution) Act 1978 — for which the limitation period is two years from the judgment or settlement fixing the contributor’s liability?

9.1.3 3. Is the claim in time?

Limitation is dealt with fully in Chapter 10. It comes second in the analysis only because you cannot date the accrual of a cause of action until you have identified it.

9.1.4 4. Can it be proved?

The burden is on the party who asserts, and the standard is the balance of probabilities. Ask what documents exist, who the witnesses are, whether expert evidence will be needed and whether the court will permit it, and what the other side is likely to disclose.

9.1.5 5. Is the defendant worth suing?

A judgment is worthless against a defendant with no assets. Before proceedings, check whether the defendant is a company still on the register and filing accounts, whether it is subject to insolvency proceedings, whether it owns land, and whether there is insurance or a guarantor. Advising a client to litigate against a shell company without raising this is a competence failure, not a tactical one.

9.1.6 6. What will it cost, and who pays?

  • The costs risk. The general rule is that the unsuccessful party pays the successful party’s costs (CPR 44.2(2)(a)), and a claimant who loses will face a bill for the other side’s costs as well as its own.
  • Funding options: private retainer; conditional fee agreement (CFA), with a success fee that is not recoverable from the losing party; damages-based agreement (DBA); before- or after-the-event insurance; third party litigation funding; legal expenses insurance; trade union funding.
  • Recovery is never complete. On the standard basis a successful party typically recovers around 60–70% of what it has actually spent, and on the fast and intermediate tracks recovery is fixed by the regime in CPR Part 45 regardless of what was spent.
Third party funding after PACCAR

In R (PACCAR Inc) v Competition Appeal Tribunal [2023] UKSC 28 the Supreme Court held that a litigation funding agreement under which the funder’s return was calculated as a share of the damages was a damages-based agreement — and therefore unenforceable unless it complied with the DBA Regulations. The funding market responded by restructuring agreements to calculate the return by reference to a multiple of the funding provided rather than a share of the recovery. Legislation to reverse the decision has been under consideration; check the position at your sitting’s cut-off before treating any reform as in force.

9.2 Choosing the forum

The specification asks for “the characteristics of arbitration, mediation and litigation which make them an appropriate mechanism to resolve a dispute”. That is a comparison question, and it is best learned as a table plus a set of discriminating facts.

9.2.1 The comparison

Litigation Arbitration Mediation
Nature Adjudicative, by the state Adjudicative, by a private tribunal Facilitative — the mediator does not decide anything
Consent None needed; a claimant may sue Requires an arbitration agreement Requires agreement to mediate (but the court may now order the parties to engage)
Outcome Judgment, binding and enforceable Award, binding and enforceable No outcome unless the parties settle; a settlement binds as a contract
Who decides A judge assigned by the court An arbitrator chosen by the parties, often with technical expertise Nobody
Privacy Public — hearings and judgments are public Private and confidential Private and without prejudice
Procedure The CPR Whatever the parties agree, subject to the Arbitration Act 1996 Entirely flexible
Speed Slow; often 18 months to trial or more Variable; can be slow in complex cases Days, and often a single session
Cost High, but the court itself is subsidised High — the parties pay the arbitrators and the venue Low
Appeal Available with permission Very limited — ss.67–69 Arbitration Act 1996 Not applicable
Third parties Can be joined Cannot be joined without consent Can attend by agreement
Relationship Adversarial; usually destroys it Adversarial, but less publicly Preserves it
Enforcement abroad Depends on treaties; patchy Excellent — the New York Convention, with over 170 states Only as a contract

9.2.2 The discriminating facts

Exam questions signal the answer through the facts. Learn the signals.

Points to mediation: the parties have an ongoing commercial relationship they want to preserve; the dispute involves emotion or reputation as much as money; the sums are modest relative to the costs; the parties want a creative outcome a court could not order (an apology, a new supply contract, a payment plan); speed matters; confidentiality matters.

Points to arbitration: the contract already contains an arbitration clause; the dispute is technical and the parties want a decision-maker who understands the subject; the parties are in different countries and enforcement will be needed abroad; confidentiality is essential; finality is wanted, with no long appeal process.

Points to litigation: there is no agreement to arbitrate or mediate and the other side will not engage; the claimant needs a precedent or a public vindication; the claim needs a remedy only a court can give — an injunction against a third party, a freezing order, or default judgment; a third party must be joined; the claim is a straightforward debt where summary judgment is likely; the limitation period is about to expire.

Exam trap

Two errors recur. First, candidates describe mediation as producing a “binding decision”. It does not: the mediator has no power to decide. What binds is the settlement agreement the parties sign, and until they sign, either can walk away. Second, candidates treat an arbitration clause as optional. It is not: if the contract contains one, the court will normally stay court proceedings brought in breach of it under s.9 of the Arbitration Act 1996, unless the arbitration agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed.

9.2.3 Arbitration in a little more detail

Arbitration in England and Wales is governed by the Arbitration Act 1996.

  • Founded on agreement. Section 6: an arbitration agreement is an agreement to submit present or future disputes to arbitration. It is separable from the contract containing it (s.7), so a challenge to the validity of the main contract does not of itself destroy the arbitration agreement.
  • Party autonomy. Section 1 sets out the principles: the object is the fair resolution of disputes by an impartial tribunal without unnecessary delay or expense; the parties are free to agree how their disputes are resolved, subject to safeguards in the public interest; and the court should not intervene except as provided by the Act.
  • The stay. Section 9: a party to an arbitration agreement against whom court proceedings are brought may apply for a stay, and the court must grant it unless satisfied that the agreement is null and void, inoperative, or incapable of being performed.
  • The tribunal. The parties choose the number of arbitrators and the method of appointment (ss.15–16); the tribunal may rule on its own jurisdiction (s.30 — competence-competence).
  • The award is final and binding (s.58) and enforceable with the court’s permission as if it were a judgment (s.66).
  • Challenges are narrow: s.67 (substantive jurisdiction), s.68 (serious irregularity causing substantial injustice), and s.69 (appeal on a point of law, which the parties may exclude by agreement and which in any event requires permission).
The Arbitration Act 2025 — verify against your cut-off

The Arbitration Act 2025 received Royal Assent on 24 February 2025 and its substantive amendments to the 1996 Act came into force on 1 August 2025, applying to arbitrations and related court proceedings commenced on or after that date. The main changes:

  • Governing law of the arbitration agreement: where the parties have not expressly chosen it, the law of the seat applies — reversing the previous common law default.
  • Summary disposal: a new s.39A empowers a tribunal, on a party’s application, to make an award on a summary basis where a party has no real prospect of succeeding on a claim, defence or issue.
  • Arbitrator’s duty of disclosure: a statutory duty to disclose circumstances that might reasonably give rise to justifiable doubts as to impartiality.
  • Section 44 orders may be made against third parties.
  • Emergency arbitrators are expressly recognised where the parties have adopted rules providing for them.
  • Section 67 challenges to jurisdiction are restricted, so that a party that participated in the arbitration cannot generally run new evidence or new grounds on a full rehearing.

Check whether this is within your examinable law cut-off. If it is, note that the 1996 Act remains the governing statute — the 2025 Act amends it rather than replacing it.

9.2.4 Mediation, and the court’s power to compel it

Mediation is a facilitated negotiation. A neutral third party helps the parties reach their own settlement; the mediator has no power to impose one. Sessions are conducted without prejudice and under a confidentiality agreement, so what is said cannot later be put before the court.

The law here moved decisively, and it is the most likely ADR question on the paper.

  • Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust [2004] EWCA Civ 576 held — or was long read as holding — that to oblige unwilling parties to mediate would obstruct their right of access to the court under Article 6 ECHR. It also established that a successful party who unreasonably refuses ADR may be penalised in costs, and set out the factors: the nature of the dispute, the merits, the extent of other settlement attempts, whether the costs of ADR would be disproportionately high, delay, and whether ADR had a reasonable prospect of success.
  • Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council [2023] EWCA Civ 1416 held that the passage in Halsey was not binding, and that the court can lawfully stay proceedings and order the parties to engage in a non-court-based dispute resolution process — provided the order does not impair the claimant’s right to a judicial hearing, and is proportionate to the legitimate aim of settling the dispute fairly, quickly and at reasonable cost.
  • The CPR were amended on 1 October 2024 to reflect this. The overriding objective now expressly includes “promoting or using alternative dispute resolution” (CPR 1.1(2)(f)), and active case management includes “ordering or encouraging the parties to use, and facilitating the use of, alternative dispute resolution” (CPR 1.4(2)(e)). Corresponding changes were made to the court’s powers on allocation and directions.
  • Separately, since 22 May 2024, mediation through the free Small Claims Mediation Service has been compulsory for most Part 7 money claims allocated to the small claims track with a value up to £10,000.
Exam trap

An option that says “the court cannot compel the parties to mediate, because that would breach Article 6” states the pre-Churchill position and is wrong. But do not overcorrect: the court still cannot compel the parties to settle, and the refusal-to-mediate costs sanction in Halsey survives — what Churchill changed is the court’s power to order engagement with a process.

9.3 The solicitor’s duty to advise on ADR

A solicitor must advise the client on the options for resolving the dispute, and must do so at the outset and keep it under review. This is not merely good practice:

  • The Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols requires the parties to consider whether negotiation or some other form of ADR may enable settlement, and warns that the court may require evidence that the parties considered it.
  • Costs consequences follow from an unreasonable refusal (Halsey), and the court may now order engagement (Churchill).
  • Under the Code of Conduct, a solicitor must provide a competent service and must give the client information in a way they can understand, so that the client can make informed decisions — including about the costs and risks of litigating rather than settling.
Exam trap

The client decides whether to mediate; the solicitor advises. But a solicitor who fails to raise ADR at all, or who advises that a refusal carries no risk, has given negligent advice. The right answer in a scenario where the client says “I want my day in court” is usually that the solicitor should advise on the costs risk of refusing, record the advice, and — if the client persists — act on instructions, not that the solicitor must refuse to act or must mediate anyway.

9.4 Check yourself

Q1. Two companies have traded together for fifteen years. A dispute has arisen over the quality of goods supplied under the most recent order, worth £60,000. Both wish to continue trading together. The supply contract contains no dispute resolution clause. The buyer’s solicitor is asked to advise on the most appropriate mechanism.

Which of the following is the best advice?

A. Arbitration, because the dispute is technical and an arbitrator can be chosen for their expertise.

B. Litigation, because only a court can order the supplier to pay.

C. Mediation, because it is quick and inexpensive, is confidential, allows a commercially creative outcome, and is the mechanism most likely to preserve the trading relationship — and the parties remain free to litigate if it fails.

D. Arbitration, because the parties can be compelled to arbitrate under s.9 of the Arbitration Act 1996.

E. Litigation, because there is no arbitration agreement and the court will not permit mediation without one.

Q2. A contract between an English company and a German company contains a clause providing that all disputes shall be referred to arbitration in London. The German company nevertheless issues proceedings in the High Court in London. The English company wishes to hold it to the arbitration clause.

What should the English company do, and what is the likely outcome?

A. File a defence and ask the court to transfer the claim to arbitration.

B. Apply under s.9 of the Arbitration Act 1996 for a stay of the court proceedings; the court must grant the stay unless satisfied that the arbitration agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed.

C. Apply for summary judgment, because the claim is bound to fail.

D. Do nothing, because the arbitration clause automatically deprives the court of jurisdiction.

E. Apply to strike the claim out under CPR 3.4 as an abuse of process.

Q3. A defendant in a multi-track claim refuses the claimant’s repeated invitations to mediate, giving no reason. The defendant wins at trial.

Which of the following best describes the court’s approach to costs?

A. The defendant will recover its costs in full, because the successful party is always entitled to its costs.

B. The court has no power to take a refusal to mediate into account.

C. The court may reduce or disallow part of the successful defendant’s costs if the refusal was unreasonable, applying the factors in Halsey; and since 1 October 2024 the overriding objective expressly includes promoting or using alternative dispute resolution.

D. The defendant will be ordered to pay the claimant’s costs, because it refused to mediate.

E. The refusal is irrelevant, because mediation is voluntary and cannot be ordered.

Q4. A client asks whether a mediator will decide who is right.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The mediator will make a binding determination, which may be appealed only on a point of law.

B. The mediator will make a recommendation which becomes binding after 21 days unless rejected.

C. The mediator has no power to decide the dispute; the process is facilitative, and any outcome takes effect as a settlement contract between the parties.

D. The mediator will make a binding determination unless the parties agree otherwise in advance.

E. The mediator will decide the dispute, but the decision is enforceable only if the court approves it.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Every fact in the question points to mediation: a long-standing relationship both sides want to preserve, a modest sum relative to litigation costs, and no dispute resolution clause forcing another route. Mediation is confidential, cheap, fast, and can produce outcomes a court cannot order — a credit note, a revised specification, a continuing supply arrangement. Crucially, it costs the client nothing in rights: if it fails, litigation remains available.

  • A is a plausible distractor, but arbitration requires an agreement, and there is none. The parties could agree to arbitrate now, but that is slower, more expensive and no better at preserving the relationship.
  • B and E overstate the case for litigation. A mediated settlement is enforceable as a contract, and can be converted into a consent order.
  • D inverts s.9, which allows a party to stay court proceedings brought in breach of an arbitration agreement. It cannot create an obligation to arbitrate where none was agreed.

Q2 — B. Section 9 Arbitration Act 1996. A party to an arbitration agreement against whom proceedings are brought in respect of a matter within that agreement may apply for a stay, and the court must grant it unless satisfied that the agreement is null and void, inoperative or incapable of being performed. The stay is mandatory, not discretionary.

  • A is doubly wrong: there is no mechanism to “transfer” a claim to arbitration, and filing a defence risks being treated as a step in the proceedings, which bars a s.9 application.
  • C and E attack the merits or the propriety of the claim; the objection is to the forum.
  • D overstates the effect: an arbitration clause does not deprive the court of jurisdiction automatically. The defendant must apply, and must do so before taking a step in the proceedings.

Q3 — C. Halsey v Milton Keynes General NHS Trust established that a successful party who unreasonably refuses ADR may be deprived of some or all of its costs, and set out the factors for assessing reasonableness. The 1 October 2024 amendments strengthened the position by writing “promoting or using alternative dispute resolution” into CPR 1.1(2)(f) and the case management duty into CPR 1.4(2)(e).

  • A overstates CPR 44.2(2)(a): costs follow the event, but “the court may make a different order”, and conduct is an express factor under 44.2(4)(a).
  • B and E deny a power the court plainly has — and E additionally states the pre-Churchill position.
  • D goes too far the other way. The usual sanction is a reduction in or disallowance of the successful party’s costs, not a reversal of the costs order.

Q4 — C. Mediation is facilitative. The mediator helps the parties negotiate but has no adjudicative power. If the parties reach terms, those terms bind as a contract, and are commonly recorded in a settlement agreement or a consent or Tomlin order. Until then either party may walk away.

  • A and D describe arbitration.
  • B describes something closer to early neutral evaluation or expert determination with a contractual acceptance mechanism, and in any event misstates it.
  • E invents a court approval requirement.
