3  Running the business

Assessment objective 2: the management of a business and company decision making to ensure compliance with statutory and other legal requirements.

3.1 The two-forum system

Everything in this chapter rests on one structural idea. A company has two decision-making bodies, and they are not interchangeable:

  • The board of directors, which manages the business. Model Article 3 confers on the directors the power to “exercise all the powers of the company”, subject to the articles.
  • The members in general meeting, whose powers are the residue: those the Act reserves to them, those the articles reserve to them, and the power to change the articles themselves.

Two corollaries follow, and questions are built on both.

The members cannot manage. If management is delegated to the board by the articles, the members cannot override a board decision by ordinary resolution. Their remedies are to change the articles by special resolution, to direct the board by special resolution under Model Article 4, or to remove the directors under s.168. They cannot simply vote to do the board’s job.

Some decisions need both. A substantial property transaction, a director’s long-term service contract, a loan to a director, an off-market share buyback — each requires a board decision to enter into the transaction and a members’ resolution to approve it. A question that gives you only one of the two is usually testing whether you noticed.

Exam trap

Model Article 4 requires a special resolution for the members to direct the directors to take, or refrain from, specified action. An option that has 51% of the members instructing the board is wrong. And even a valid direction does not invalidate anything the directors have already done.

3.2 Directors

3.2.1 Who is a director

Section 250 CA 2006 defines “director” to include any person occupying the position of director, by whatever name called. Substance beats title, and three categories matter:

  • De jure directors — validly appointed and registered.
  • De facto directors — never validly appointed, but who act as directors and are held out as such, taking part in corporate governance on an equal footing with the board.
  • Shadow directors — a person in accordance with whose directions or instructions the directors are accustomed to act (s.251). A person is not a shadow director merely because the directors act on advice given in a professional capacity; nor, generally, is a holding company treated as a shadow director of its subsidiary for most purposes.

The distinction has consequences. All three may be liable for wrongful and fraudulent trading and may be disqualified. The general duties in ss.171–177 apply to de jure and de facto directors, and apply to shadow directors “where and to the extent that they are capable of so applying” (s.170(5)).

Other labels carry no separate legal meaning under the Act: an executive director is a director with a service contract, a non-executive director one without, and the general duties apply to both identically. There is no reduced standard for a non-executive.

Exam trap

“Alternate director”, “associate director”, “director of finance” and “shadow director” are not the same thing. A person given the courtesy title “associate director” who is not on the board and does not act as one is not a director. Conversely, a controlling shareholder who dictates every board decision may be a shadow director and carries a director’s exposure. Look at what the person does, not what the business card says.

3.2.3 Appointment

Model Article 17 gives two routes for a private company: by ordinary resolution of the members, or by decision of the directors. There is no requirement that vacancies be filled in any particular way, and no rotation requirement in the Model Articles for private companies.

Whichever route is used, the company must notify the Registrar within 14 days (s.167), and the appointment cannot be registered until the appointee’s identity has been verified.

Section 161 protects third parties: the acts of a person acting as a director are valid notwithstanding that it is afterwards discovered that there was a defect in their appointment, that they were disqualified, or that they were not entitled to vote.

3.2.4 Vacation of office and removal

A director may cease to hold office in several ways, and the exam turns on distinguishing them.

Automatically, under the articles. Model Article 18 provides that a person ceases to be a director when they cease to be a director by virtue of any provision of the Act or are prohibited by law from being one, when a bankruptcy order is made against them, when a composition is made with their creditors generally in satisfaction of their debts, or when they give the company notice of resignation. Articles frequently add further grounds — prolonged absence from board meetings, or a requirement to resign on ceasing to be an employee.

By resignation. A director may resign by notice. That does not end any liability already incurred and does not terminate a service contract; a director who resigns in breach of their service contract is exposed to a claim for damages.

By removal under s.168. This is the provision the exam cares about, and its mechanics are precise:

  • The members may remove a director by ordinary resolution at a general meeting, notwithstanding anything in any agreement between the company and the director.
  • Special notice is required (s.168(2) and s.312): the members proposing the resolution must give the company notice of intention at least 28 days before the meeting. The company must then give members notice of the resolution at the same time and in the same manner as notice of the meeting, or, where that is not practicable, by advertisement or other means allowed by the articles not less than 14 days before the meeting.
  • Section 169 gives the director the right to be heard at the meeting and to have written representations of reasonable length circulated to members (or read out at the meeting, if received too late to circulate).
  • Section 168(5): removal does not deprive the director of compensation or damages payable for the termination of their appointment or of any appointment terminating with it.
Exam trap

Four traps live in s.168, and all four appear regularly.

  1. A written resolution cannot be used. Section 288(2) excludes resolutions under s.168 (removal of a director) and s.510 (removal of an auditor) from the written resolution procedure. A general meeting is mandatory.
  2. Special notice is 28 days from the members to the company — it is not a notice period the company gives to the members.
  3. Removal does not end the contract. A director removed under s.168 in the middle of a five-year service contract is removed, and the company owes damages. The exam’s favourite phrasing is that the members “cannot remove him because his contract has three years to run”. They can. It just costs.
  4. Removing a director does not remove a shareholder. Removal from the board does nothing to the person’s shares, their dividend, or their votes.

The Bushell v Faith clause. In Bushell v Faith [1970] AC 1099 the articles provided that on a resolution to remove a director, that director’s shares should carry three votes each. The House of Lords upheld it: s.168 (then s.184 of the 1948 Act) requires an ordinary resolution, and the articles may validly determine how many votes a share carries. A weighted voting clause therefore makes a director irremovable in practice while leaving s.168 formally intact.

Worked example

A company has three shareholders holding 40, 40 and 20 shares. The articles contain a Bushell v Faith clause giving a director’s shares five votes each on a resolution for their removal. The 20% holder is also a director, and the other two wish to remove him.

Ordinary votes: 40 + 40 = 80 against, 20 for. With the clause: 80 against, 20 × 5 = 100 for retaining. The resolution fails. To defeat it, the majority must first change the articles — but that requires a special resolution of 75%, and on that resolution the clause does not apply, so they have 80 of 100 votes and can do it. The order of the steps is the point being tested.

3.2.5 Directors’ service contracts

Section 227 defines a director’s service contract, and two rules attach:

  • Section 188 — a term guaranteeing employment for a guaranteed term exceeding two years requires approval by ordinary resolution of the members, with a memorandum of the proposed contract made available for inspection for at least 15 days before the meeting and at the meeting. If approval is not obtained, the offending term is void and the contract is deemed to contain a term entitling the company to terminate at any time on reasonable notice.
  • Section 228 — a copy of every director’s service contract (or a written memorandum of its terms if not in writing) must be kept available for inspection by members, at the registered office or an alternative inspection location, for its duration and for one year after termination. Members may inspect without charge (s.229).

3.2.6 Disqualification

The Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986 keeps unfit people out of the boardroom. The examinable structure:

Section Ground Maximum (and minimum)
s.2 Conviction of an indictable offence in connection with the promotion, formation, management, liquidation or striking off of a company 15 years
s.3 Persistent breaches of companies legislation (filing defaults) 5 years
s.4 Fraud or breach of duty revealed in a winding up 15 years
s.6 Unfitness where the company has become insolvent — the court must disqualify Minimum 2, maximum 15
s.8 Unfitness following investigation 15 years
s.10 Participation in wrongful or fraudulent trading 15 years
s.11 Undischarged bankrupt, or subject to a debt relief restrictions order, acting without leave Automatic

Section 6 is the workhorse. The matters the court must consider are set out in Schedule 1 and include the extent of the director’s responsibility for the causes of the insolvency, for any failure to supply goods or services paid for, and for breaches of any applicable legislative or other requirement. Rather than contest proceedings, a director may give a disqualification undertaking (s.1A), with the same effect as an order.

The consequences of acting while disqualified are serious, and are examined:

  • It is a criminal offence (s.13).
  • Section 15 imposes personal liability, jointly and severally with the company, for the debts of the company incurred while the person was acting in contravention — and on any person who acts on that person’s instructions knowing them to be disqualified.
  • Section 15A allows the court to make a compensation order requiring a disqualified director to pay compensation for identifiable loss caused to creditors.
  • A disqualified person may apply for leave to act in relation to a particular company (s.17).

3.2.7 Check yourself: directors

Q1. A private company has five directors. Three of the shareholders, who between them hold 62% of the shares, wish to remove one of the directors, whose service contract has four years left to run. The articles are in the form of the Model Articles.

Which of the following is the correct advice?

A. The director cannot be removed until his service contract expires.

B. The director may be removed by a written resolution signed by holders of a majority of the total voting rights.

C. The director may be removed by ordinary resolution at a general meeting, provided special notice of the resolution is given to the company at least 28 days before the meeting; he may be entitled to damages for breach of his service contract.

D. The director may be removed only by special resolution, because removal is a constitutional matter.

E. The director may be removed by a decision of the other directors under Model Article 18.

Q2. A company’s articles provide that on any resolution to remove a director, the shares held by that director shall carry ten votes each. A director holds 15% of the shares; the remaining 85% is held by shareholders who wish to remove him.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The provision is void, because it conflicts with the members’ statutory right to remove a director by ordinary resolution.

B. The provision is valid; the director’s 15% will carry 150 votes against 85, so the removal resolution will fail, but the majority may first alter the articles by special resolution.

C. The provision is valid, and the articles cannot be altered to remove it because it is entrenched.

D. The provision is void, because it discriminates between shareholders of the same class.

E. The provision is valid, but may be overridden by the members by ordinary resolution.

Q3. A wealthy investor holds no shares in a company and has never been appointed to its board. However, the three registered directors have for two years followed her instructions on every significant matter without exception, and regard themselves as accountable to her. The company enters insolvent liquidation.

Which of the following best describes her position?

A. She has no liability, because she was never appointed a director and holds no shares.

B. She may be a shadow director, and as such may be liable for wrongful trading and may be disqualified.

C. She is a de facto director, because she participated in corporate governance on an equal footing with the board.

D. She is liable only if she received payments from the company.

E. She may be liable only if the corporate veil is pierced.

Q4. A director of an insolvent company was disqualified for six years under s.6 CDDA 1986. Eight months into the period, and without applying for leave, he takes over the running of a new company, instructing its sole registered director, who knows of the disqualification, on all matters.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Only the disqualified person commits an offence; the registered director bears no liability.

B. The disqualified person commits a criminal offence and is personally liable for the new company’s relevant debts; the registered director, who acted on his instructions knowing of the disqualification, is also personally liable for those debts.

C. Neither is liable, because the disqualification relates to a different company.

D. The disqualified person is liable only if the new company becomes insolvent.

E. The disqualification is automatically lifted after six months of good conduct.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Section 168 permits removal by ordinary resolution at a general meeting notwithstanding anything in any agreement. Special notice under s.312 must be given by the proposers to the company at least 28 days before the meeting. Section 168(5) preserves the director’s claim for damages for breach of his service contract.

  • A is the headline trap. A long service contract makes removal expensive, not impossible.
  • B is the second trap: s.288(2) expressly excludes s.168 resolutions from the written resolution procedure.
  • D applies the wrong majority: s.168 requires only an ordinary resolution.
  • E misreads Model Article 18, which lists events on which a director automatically vacates office; it confers no power on the board to remove a colleague.

Q2 — B. Bushell v Faith [1970] AC 1099. Weighted voting on a removal resolution is valid, because s.168 requires an ordinary resolution and the articles may determine the votes attaching to shares. 15% × 10 = 150 votes against 85. The route round it is to alter the articles by special resolution, on which the weighting does not apply.

  • A and D assert invalidity that the House of Lords rejected.
  • C wrongly assumes entrenchment; entrenchment requires the s.22 procedure and there is no suggestion of it.
  • E would allow an ordinary resolution to override the articles, which it cannot.

Q3 — B. Section 251: a shadow director is a person in accordance with whose directions or instructions the directors of the company are accustomed to act. Shadow directors are within the wrongful trading provisions (s.214 IA 1986) and the disqualification regime, and the general duties apply to the extent capable of applying (s.170(5)).

  • A treats appointment and shareholding as necessary. Section 250 and s.251 both look at conduct.
  • C describes the de facto director test, which requires the person to have acted as a director on an equal footing with the board — the opposite of directing it from outside.
  • D invents a payment condition.
  • E is unnecessary: liability arises directly under statute, not by piercing the veil.

Q4 — B. Section 13 CDDA makes acting while disqualified a criminal offence. Section 15 imposes personal liability, jointly and severally with the company, for the relevant debts of the company incurred while a person acts in contravention of a disqualification — and on any person who acts or is willing to act on the instructions of someone they know to be disqualified.

  • A overlooks the extension of s.15 to the person taking instructions.
  • C is wrong: disqualification is general, not company-specific, unless leave is granted under s.17.
  • D adds an insolvency condition that s.15 does not require.
  • E invents a good conduct discharge.

3.3 Directors’ duties

3.3.1 The framework

Sections 171–177 codify the general duties. They are owed to the company (s.170(1)) — not to individual shareholders, not to creditors, not to employees. That single sentence answers a surprising number of questions.

Section 170(2) extends two duties beyond office: a former director remains subject to s.175 as regards the exploitation of property, information or opportunity of which they became aware while a director, and to s.176 as regards things done or omitted before they ceased to be a director.

Section 179 confirms that more than one duty may apply to the same conduct, and s.178 supplies the consequences: the remedies for breach are the same as would apply if the corresponding common law rule or equitable principle applied. The duties are to be interpreted and applied in the same way as the common law rules and equitable principles they replace (s.170(4)).

3.3.2 s.171 — Duty to act within powers

A director must (a) act in accordance with the company’s constitution, and (b) only exercise powers for the purposes for which they are conferred.

Limb (b) is the proper purpose doctrine, and it is where the litigation is. The question is not whether the directors acted honestly or in what they believed to be the company’s interests — they usually did — but whether the power was used for the purpose for which it exists.

  • Howard Smith Ltd v Ampol Petroleum Ltd [1974] AC 821 — directors allotted shares to defeat a takeover bid and dilute a majority shareholder. The power to allot exists to raise capital, not to manipulate control. Breach, despite the directors’ honest belief that the bid was bad for the company.
  • Eclairs Group Ltd v JKX Oil & Gas plc [2015] UKSC 71 — the Supreme Court applied a “but for” test where there are mixed purposes: if the improper purpose was causative, the exercise is invalid even though a proper purpose was also present.

A transaction entered into in breach of s.171 may be voidable, but third-party rights intervene: see s.40 in Chapter 2.

3.3.3 s.172 — Duty to promote the success of the company

A director must act in the way he considers, in good faith, would be most likely to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, having regard (amongst other matters) to:

  1. the likely long-term consequences of any decision;
  2. the interests of the company’s employees;
  3. the need to foster the company’s business relationships with suppliers, customers and others;
  4. the impact of the company’s operations on the community and the environment;
  5. the desirability of maintaining a reputation for high standards of business conduct; and
  6. the need to act fairly as between members of the company.

Three points decide questions.

The test is subjective. It is what the director considers, in good faith — Re Smith & Fawcett Ltd [1942] Ch 304: directors must act “bona fide in what they consider — not what a court may consider — is in the interests of the company”. Where a director has given the matter no thought at all, the court asks objectively whether an honest and intelligent person in their position could have reasonably believed the transaction was for the company’s benefit (Charterbridge Corporation Ltd v Lloyds Bank Ltd [1970] Ch 62).

The (a)–(f) factors are considerations, not duties. They must be “had regard to”; they are not separate obligations owed to employees, suppliers or the environment. Employees have no standing to sue for a breach of s.172(b).

The creditor duty. Section 172(3) makes the duty subject to any enactment or rule of law requiring directors in certain circumstances to consider or act in the interests of creditors. BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA [2022] UKSC 25 settled its content:

  • The creditor duty is not a free-standing duty owed to creditors. It is a modification of the s.172 duty owed to the company, under which the directors must consider creditors’ interests.
  • It is engaged when the company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or when an insolvent liquidation or administration is probable. A real risk of insolvency at some future time is not enough.
  • Its weight is a sliding scale: as insolvency becomes more likely, creditors’ interests carry more weight, until insolvent liquidation is inevitable, when they become paramount.
  • On the facts, a lawful dividend paid when the company was solvent but faced a long-term contingent liability did not breach the duty, because insolvency was not imminent or probable.
Exam trap

“The directors owe a duty to the creditors” is wrong, and it is wrong in a specific way. The duty is owed to the company; creditors cannot sue on it directly. It is enforced after the event by a liquidator or administrator. An option giving an individual creditor a direct claim for breach of s.172 is the distractor.

3.3.4 s.173 — Duty to exercise independent judgment

A director must exercise independent judgment. The duty is not infringed by acting in accordance with an agreement duly entered into by the company that restricts the future exercise of discretion by its directors, or in a way authorised by the company’s constitution.

The practical application is the nominee director appointed by an investor or a parent company: they must form their own view in the interests of the company on whose board they sit, and may not simply transmit their appointor’s wishes.

3.3.5 s.174 — Duty to exercise reasonable care, skill and diligence

The standard is the care, skill and diligence that would be exercised by a reasonably diligent person with:

  1. the general knowledge, skill and experience that may reasonably be expected of a person carrying out the functions carried out by the director in relation to the company — the objective minimum; and
  2. the general knowledge, skill and experience that the director actually has — the subjective element, which can only raise the standard, never lower it.
  • Re D’Jan of London Ltd [1994] 1 BCLC 561 — a director who signed an insurance proposal form without reading it, rendering the policy voidable, was in breach; but he was granted partial relief under what is now s.1157 because he had acted honestly and reasonably and owned most of the shares.
  • Re Barings plc (No 5) [1999] 1 BCLC 433 — directors may delegate, but delegation does not absolve: there remains a residual duty to supervise the discharge of delegated functions, and directors must acquire and maintain sufficient knowledge of the business to discharge their responsibilities.
Exam trap

Section 174 is the only general duty whose breach sounds in damages for negligence. The others are fiduciary and attract the equitable remedies — account of profits, equitable compensation, rescission, constructive trust, injunction. An option offering “an account of profits” for a careless failure to supervise is wrong; an option offering “damages” for a secret profit is also wrong.

3.3.6 s.175 — Duty to avoid conflicts of interest

A director must avoid a situation in which he has, or can have, a direct or indirect interest that conflicts, or possibly may conflict, with the interests of the company. The section:

  • Applies in particular to the exploitation of any property, information or opportunity, and it is immaterial whether the company could take advantage of it (s.175(2)).
  • Does not apply to a conflict arising in relation to a transaction or arrangement with the company itself — that is s.177’s territory (s.175(3)).
  • Is not infringed if the situation cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict, or if the matter has been authorised by the directors (s.175(4)).

Board authorisation (s.175(5)–(6)) is the practical escape route, and its mechanics differ by company type:

Private company Public company
Board may authorise if nothing in the constitution invalidates such authorisation the constitution expressly enables it
Conflicted director’s vote Does not count Does not count
Conflicted director in quorum Not counted Not counted

The case law is unforgiving, and it is the strictness that is examined:

  • Aberdeen Railway Co v Blaikie Brothers (1854) 1 Macq 461 — no fiduciary may put himself in a position where duty and interest conflict.
  • Regal (Hastings) Ltd v Gulliver [1967] 2 AC 134 — directors personally subscribed for shares in a subsidiary because the company could not afford them, then sold at a profit. Liable to account, despite acting in good faith, despite the company being unable to take the opportunity, and despite the company having benefited. Liability arises from the fact of the profit, not from fraud. (They could have protected themselves by putting it to the members in general meeting.)
  • Industrial Development Consultants Ltd v Cooley [1972] 1 WLR 443 — a managing director learned that a third party would deal with him personally but not with his company. He resigned on a false pretext and took the contract. Liable to account for the whole profit; resignation is no escape.
  • Bhullar v Bhullar [2003] EWCA Civ 424 — directors bought a property adjacent to the company’s, having learned of it while acting as directors. The company had resolved not to buy more property. Liable: the opportunity came to them as directors, and the company’s inability or unwillingness to take it was irrelevant.
  • O’Donnell v Shanahan [2009] EWCA Civ 751 — there is no “scope of business” limitation on a director’s fiduciary duty; the opportunity need not fall within the company’s line of business.
Exam trap

The favourite distractors here are: “the company could not have taken the opportunity anyway”; “the company had decided not to pursue it”; “the director acted in good faith and the company suffered no loss”; and “the director resigned first”. None of them is a defence. What works is prior authorisation by the independent directors under s.175(5), or approval or ratification by the members.

3.3.7 s.176 — Duty not to accept benefits from third parties

A director must not accept a benefit conferred by reason of being a director, or of doing or not doing anything as a director. It is not infringed if acceptance cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict of interest.

The critical distinction from s.175: the board cannot authorise a s.176 benefit. Only the members can approve or ratify it. Corporate hospitality and ordinary business courtesies fall outside the section only because they cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict.

3.3.8 ss.177 and 182 — Declaring an interest in a transaction

Two provisions, doing different jobs, and reliably confused:

s.177 s.182
Applies to a proposed transaction or arrangement an existing transaction or arrangement
Nature a general duty a statutory obligation
Timing before the company enters into it as soon as reasonably practicable
Breach breach of duty; equitable consequences criminal offence (s.183), fine

Under both, the director must declare the nature and extent of the interest to the other directors. Declaration may be at a board meeting, by notice in writing (s.184), or by general notice (s.185). Neither is required where:

  • the director is not aware of the interest (but is treated as aware of matters of which he ought reasonably to be aware);
  • the interest cannot reasonably be regarded as likely to give rise to a conflict;
  • the other directors are already aware of it, or ought reasonably to be; or
  • it concerns the terms of the director’s service contract that have been or are to be considered by the board or a committee of it.

Alongside the statutory duty sits Model Article 14: a director who is interested in a proposed transaction may not vote on it and is not counted in the quorum, subject to specified exceptions (a guarantee given in respect of an obligation of the company, subscription for shares or debentures, and certain pension arrangements) and subject to the members disapplying the article by ordinary resolution.

Exam trap

Declaring an interest under s.177 satisfies the duty; it does not by itself make the director’s vote count. Model Article 14 is a separate obstacle, and a board meeting that is inquorate once the interested director is excluded cannot validly take the decision. On facts involving a two-director company where one is interested, the correct answer is usually that the board cannot act and the matter must go to the members — or Model Article 14 must first be disapplied.

3.3.9 Consequences of breach, ratification and relief

Remedies (s.178). For the fiduciary duties (ss.171–173, 175–177): account of profits, equitable compensation, rescission of the contract, restoration of company property, injunction, and a constructive trust over property or profits received. A third party who receives company property with knowledge, or who dishonestly assists the breach, may also be liable. For s.174: damages.

Ratification (s.239). Conduct amounting to negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust may be ratified by ordinary resolution of the members (or a higher threshold if the articles require). The essential mechanic:

  • The votes of the director in question, if a member, and of any member connected with him, are disregarded.
  • Where ratification is by written resolution, neither the director nor any connected member is an eligible member.
  • Section 239(6) preserves any rule of law as to acts that are incapable of ratification — including acts that are illegal, that constitute a fraud on the minority, or that prejudice creditors when the company is insolvent.

Court relief (s.1157). If in proceedings for negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust it appears that the director acted honestly and reasonably and that having regard to all the circumstances he ought fairly to be excused, the court may relieve him wholly or partly.

Protection the company may and may not give (ss.232–234). Any provision exempting a director from liability for negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust in relation to the company is void (s.232). But the company may purchase and maintain directors’ and officers’ insurance (s.233), and may give a qualifying third party indemnity — an indemnity against liability to persons other than the company, excluding criminal fines, regulatory penalties, and the costs of unsuccessfully defending criminal proceedings or civil proceedings brought by the company (s.234).

Exam trap

A company cannot indemnify its director against liability to the company itself. Options offering an indemnity for a breach of duty owed to the company are testing s.232. What is permitted is insurance, and an indemnity against liability to third parties within s.234.

3.3.10 Check yourself: directors’ duties

Q1. A director of a construction company learns, while attending a meeting on the company’s behalf, that a valuable site is coming up for sale. The board considers the site and resolves not to bid, because the company has no available funds. The director then buys the site personally and resells it eight months later at a profit of £600,000. He acted openly and the company suffered no loss.

Which of the following is correct?

A. He is not liable, because the company had resolved not to pursue the opportunity.

B. He is not liable, because the company could not have afforded the site.

C. He is not liable, because he acted in good faith and the company suffered no loss.

D. He is liable to account to the company for the £600,000, because he exploited an opportunity of which he became aware as a director, and the matter was not authorised by the independent directors or approved by the members.

E. He is liable to pay the company damages for negligence under s.174.

Q2. The board of a private company allots a large block of new shares to a friendly investor. The directors honestly believe the investor will be a stabilising influence, but their principal reason is to dilute a shareholder who has been pressing for their removal. The company had no need of the capital.

Which duty is most clearly breached?

A. Section 172, because the allotment was not in the interests of the members as a whole.

B. Section 171(b), because the power to allot shares was exercised for an improper purpose.

C. Section 174, because a reasonably diligent director would not have made the allotment.

D. Section 175, because the directors had a personal interest in retaining office.

E. Section 176, because the investor conferred a benefit on the directors.

Q3. A company has two directors, Priya and Tom, and no others. It proposes to buy premises from a company wholly owned by Priya, at a value well below the s.190 threshold. Priya discloses the nature and extent of her interest fully to Tom at a board meeting before the contract is made, and both then vote in favour. The articles are the unamended Model Articles.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The board decision is valid, because Priya declared her interest under s.177 before the company entered into the transaction.

B. The board decision is valid, because the transaction falls below the s.190 threshold.

C. The board decision is invalid, because Model Article 14 prevents Priya from voting or being counted in the quorum, leaving the meeting inquorate; the members must disapply Model Article 14 by ordinary resolution, or the transaction must be approved by them.

D. The board decision is invalid, because Priya has committed a criminal offence under s.183.

E. The board decision is valid, because a two-director board always has a quorum of one where the other is interested.

Q4. The directors of a company that is solvent, profitable and has no immediate financial difficulties declare a lawful dividend. The company has a long-term contingent liability of uncertain amount that may, on one view, render it insolvent in a decade’s time. Years later the company enters insolvent administration and the administrators allege that the dividend breached the directors’ duties.

Which of the following best reflects the current law?

A. The creditor duty was engaged, because there was a real risk that the company would become insolvent at some point in the future.

B. The creditor duty was engaged from the moment the contingent liability arose, and the directors were required to treat creditors’ interests as paramount.

C. The creditor duty was not engaged, because it arises only when the company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or when an insolvent liquidation or administration is probable.

D. The creditor duty is owed directly to creditors, who may sue the directors personally.

E. The creditor duty never applies to the payment of a lawful dividend.

Q5. A director causes his company to enter into a contract from which he makes a secret profit of £80,000. He holds 30% of the company’s shares. His wife, who holds a further 25%, is also a member. The remaining 45% is held by unconnected members. A general meeting is called to ratify the director’s conduct by ordinary resolution. All members vote: the director and his wife in favour, and members holding 24% of the shares also in favour, with 21% against.

Is the conduct validly ratified?

A. Yes — because 79% of the votes cast were in favour, which is more than a simple majority.

B. Yes — because the votes of the director and of his wife are disregarded, leaving 24% in favour and 21% against, which is a simple majority of the eligible votes cast.

C. No — because the votes of the director and of his wife are disregarded, and the resolution therefore fails for want of a simple majority of all the shares in issue.

D. No — because ratification of a secret profit requires a special resolution.

E. No — because a breach of the duty to avoid conflicts of interest is incapable of ratification in any circumstances.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — D. Section 175, on facts materially identical to Bhullar v Bhullar and Regal (Hastings) v Gulliver. Section 175(2) makes it immaterial whether the company could take advantage of the opportunity. Liability is to account for the profit; there is no requirement that the company suffered loss.

  • A, B and C are the three standard distractors, and each states a fact that the cases hold to be irrelevant. Note the further point in Regal: good faith is no defence to an account.
  • E applies the wrong duty and the wrong remedy. A secret profit attracts an account, not damages.

Q2 — B. Howard Smith Ltd v Ampol Petroleum Ltd and Eclairs Group Ltd v JKX Oil & Gas plc. The power to allot shares exists to raise capital, not to alter the balance of control. On the Eclairs “but for” analysis, the improper purpose was causative, so the exercise is invalid despite the honest belief.

  • A is the tempting answer, but s.172 is a subjective test and the directors honestly believed they were acting in the company’s interests — so s.171(b) is the cleaner and better answer, and the question asks which duty is most clearly breached.
  • C misapplies the negligence standard; the directors were not careless.
  • D describes an interest that s.175(3) excludes and that the facts do not establish.
  • E requires a benefit conferred by a third party on the directors; there is none.

Q3 — C. Section 177 was complied with, but compliance with the duty does not cure the procedural bar. Model Article 14 prevents an interested director from voting or being counted in the quorum, and none of its exceptions applies. With Priya excluded, only Tom remains, and Model Article 11 fixes the quorum at two. The board cannot validly resolve. The routes forward are for the members to disapply Model Article 14 by ordinary resolution, or to amend the articles.

  • A is the trap: it confuses discharging the duty with satisfying the article.
  • B is irrelevant; the s.190 threshold governs a different requirement.
  • D confuses s.177 (proposed transaction, general duty) with s.182 (existing transaction, criminal offence for non-declaration). Priya declared, and in any event the transaction is proposed.
  • E invents a rule. Model Article 11(3) allows a sole director to act only where the company has just one director.

Q4 — C. BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA [2022] UKSC 25. The creditor duty is engaged when the company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or when an insolvent liquidation or administration is probable. A real risk of insolvency at some future point is not enough — which was the actual outcome in Sequana itself.

  • A applies the “real risk” test that the Supreme Court rejected.
  • B gets both the trigger and the weighting wrong: creditors’ interests become paramount only when insolvent liquidation is inevitable.
  • D is the recurring structural error. The duty is a modification of the duty owed to the company; creditors have no direct claim.
  • E is too absolute — a dividend can engage the duty; it simply did not on these facts.

Q5 — B. Section 239(3)–(4): on a resolution to ratify, the votes of the director and of any member connected with him are disregarded. A spouse is a member of the director’s family and so a connected person (ss.252–253). Removing 30% + 25% leaves 45% of the shares eligible to vote, of which 24% voted in favour and 21% against. An ordinary resolution requires a simple majority of the votes cast by eligible members, and 24 exceeds 21. The conduct is validly ratified.

  • A reaches the right conclusion by counting the disregarded votes — the reasoning is exactly what s.239 forbids, so it is not the best answer.
  • C applies the right exclusion but the wrong majority: an ordinary resolution needs a majority of the votes cast, not of all the shares in issue.
  • D applies the wrong resolution type: s.239 requires an ordinary resolution unless the articles say otherwise.
  • E overstates s.239(6). Some acts cannot be ratified — illegality, fraud on the minority, acts prejudicing creditors of an insolvent company — but a secret profit in a solvent company is not among them; that is exactly how the directors in Regal (Hastings) could have protected themselves.

3.4 Transactions requiring member approval

Four categories of transaction are so open to abuse that Parliament requires the members to sign them off in addition to the board. In every case the resolution required is an ordinary one.

3.4.1 Connected persons

Three of the four regimes extend to persons connected with a director, so the definition has to be learned. Sections 252–256:

  • Members of the director’s family (s.253): spouse or civil partner; any other person with whom the director lives as a partner in an enduring family relationship; the director’s children or step-children; any children or step-children of that other person who are under 18 and live with the director; and the director’s parents.
  • Bodies corporate with which the director is connected (s.254): where the director, together with persons connected with them, holds 20% or more of the share capital or controls 20% or more of the voting power.
  • Trustees of a trust under which the director or a connected person is a beneficiary (s.255).
  • Partners of the director, acting in that capacity.
Exam trap

The family list is exhaustive and counter-intuitive. A director’s siblings, grandparents, grandchildren, aunts, uncles, nephews and nieces are NOT connected persons, nor are adult step-children of an unmarried partner who do not live with the director. Examiners build questions around a transaction with a director’s brother precisely because candidates assume family means family. It does not.

3.4.2 s.190 — Substantial property transactions

A company may not enter into an arrangement under which a director of the company or of its holding company, or a person connected with such a director, acquires from the company (or the company acquires from them) a substantial non-cash asset, unless the arrangement has been approved by ordinary resolution of the members — or is conditional on such approval being obtained.

“Substantial” (s.191). A non-cash asset is substantial if its value:

  • exceeds £100,000; or
  • exceeds 10% of the company’s asset value and is more than £5,000.

The company’s asset value is the value of its net assets as shown in its most recent statutory accounts, or, if no accounts have been prepared, the amount of its called-up share capital.

Exceptions (s.192). Approval is not required for:

  • a transaction with a person in their character as a member of the company;
  • a transaction between a holding company and its wholly-owned subsidiary, or between two wholly-owned subsidiaries of the same holding company;
  • a transaction by a company being wound up (unless a members’ voluntary winding up) or in administration.

Where the director is a director of the company’s holding company, the holding company’s members must also approve. No resolution is required from the members of a wholly-owned subsidiary.

Consequences of contravention (s.195). The arrangement is voidable at the instance of the company, unless: restitution is no longer possible; the company has been indemnified for the loss; rights acquired in good faith, for value and without actual notice by a third party would be affected; or the arrangement is affirmed by the members within a reasonable period. Whether or not it is avoided, the director or connected person, and any director who authorised it, is liable to account to the company for any gain and, jointly and severally, to indemnify the company for any loss. A connected person, and a director who authorised the arrangement, has a defence if they show they did not know the circumstances constituting the contravention; and a director connected with a contracting person has a defence if they took all reasonable steps to secure compliance.

Worked example

A company’s most recent accounts show net assets of £480,000. A director proposes to buy a company car from the company for £42,000, its market value.

Is approval required? £42,000 does not exceed £100,000. But 10% of £480,000 is £48,000, and £42,000 is less than that. So the asset is not substantial on either limb, and no members’ resolution is needed under s.190.

Change the net assets to £300,000. Now 10% is £30,000; £42,000 exceeds it and also exceeds £5,000. The asset is substantial and an ordinary resolution is required — even though the price is the full market value, and even though the company loses nothing. Section 190 is not about fairness; it is about disclosure to the members.

3.4.3 s.197 — Loans to directors

A company may not make a loan to a director of the company or of its holding company, or give a guarantee or provide security in connection with a loan made by any person to such a director, unless approved by ordinary resolution. A memorandum setting out the nature of the transaction, the amount, the purpose and the extent of the company’s liability must be made available to members for at least 15 days before the meeting and at the meeting (or sent with a written resolution).

Two extensions apply only to public companies and companies associated with a public company:

  • s.198 — quasi-loans (where the company pays a sum for the director, or agrees to reimburse expenditure incurred by the director, on terms that the director will reimburse the company).
  • s.201 — credit transactions (supplying goods, selling land under a hire-purchase or conditional sale, or otherwise disposing of land or supplying services on deferred payment terms).

Sections 200 and 201 also extend the loan and quasi-loan controls to persons connected with a director, again only for public companies and their associates.

Exceptions — the numbers matter and are examined:

Section Exception Limit
s.204 Expenditure on company business £50,000
s.205 Funding a director’s defence costs in proceedings relating to the company, or a regulatory investigation No cap; must be repaid if the defence fails
s.207(1) Minor loans and quasi-loans £10,000
s.207(2) Minor credit transactions £15,000
s.208 Intra-group transactions —
s.209 Money-lending companies lending in the ordinary course of business on ordinary terms —

Consequences of contravention (s.213). The transaction is voidable at the instance of the company on the same four conditions as s.195, and the director or connected person, and any director who authorised it, must account for any gain and indemnify the company for any loss. The transaction may be affirmed by the members within a reasonable period (s.214).

Exam trap

Quasi-loans and credit transactions are unrestricted for a stand-alone private company. A question describing a private company paying a director’s personal credit card bill on terms that he will reimburse it is describing a quasi-loan, which needs no approval — unless the company is associated with a public company. Candidates who have learned “all transactions with directors need approval” choose the wrong answer. (Note that there may still be a tax consequence: see the s.455 charge on loans to participators in Chapter 6.)

3.4.4 s.217 — Payments for loss of office

A company may not make a payment for loss of office to a director of the company or of its holding company (and, under ss.218–219, in connection with a transfer of the company’s undertaking or a takeover) unless approved by ordinary resolution, with a memorandum of the proposed payment made available in the same way.

“Payment for loss of office” (s.215) includes payments made in connection with retirement from office or employment, and payments to connected persons or at a director’s direction.

Exceptions (ss.220–221):

  • Payments made in good faith in discharge of an existing legal obligation (including damages for breach of such an obligation), by way of settlement or compromise of a claim arising in connection with the termination, or by way of pension in respect of past services.
  • Small payments: where the aggregate does not exceed £200.

Consequences (s.222). An unapproved payment is held on trust for the company, and any director who authorised it is jointly and severally liable to indemnify the company for any resulting loss.

Exam trap

Section 220 does most of the work in practice, and it is the discriminator. Contractual payments in lieu of notice, and genuine damages or settlement payments, are not caught, because they discharge an existing legal obligation. What is caught is the ex gratia golden handshake — the payment the company was not obliged to make. If the question stresses that the board wished to “recognise his long service”, the answer is that members’ approval is required.

3.4.5 s.188 — Long-term service contracts

Covered above with directors’ service contracts. Remember the distinctive sanction: it is the offending term that is void, not the contract, and the contract becomes terminable by the company at any time on reasonable notice.

3.4.6 Check yourself: transactions requiring approval

Q1. A private company’s most recent statutory accounts show net assets of £1.2 million. The board resolves to sell a warehouse, valued at £115,000, to the wife of one of the directors. No members’ resolution is passed.

Which of the following is correct?

A. No members’ approval is required, because £115,000 is less than 10% of the company’s net assets.

B. No members’ approval is required, because the buyer is not a director.

C. Members’ approval by ordinary resolution was required, because the value exceeds £100,000 and the buyer is a person connected with a director; the arrangement is voidable at the company’s instance.

D. Members’ approval by special resolution was required, because the transaction involves land.

E. No members’ approval is required, provided the warehouse is sold at full market value.

Q2. A private company with no connection to any public company pays a director’s personal hotel bill of £6,000, on terms that the director will reimburse the company within three months. The company is not a money-lending company.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Members’ approval by ordinary resolution is required, because this is a loan to a director.

B. Members’ approval by ordinary resolution is required, because this is a quasi-loan and exceeds £5,000.

C. No members’ approval is required, because the quasi-loan provisions in s.198 apply only to public companies and companies associated with a public company.

D. No members’ approval is required, because the amount falls within the £10,000 minor loan exception in s.207.

E. The payment is prohibited outright and cannot be authorised by any resolution.

Q3. A director retires. Her service contract entitles her to six months’ pay in lieu of notice, and the company pays her £45,000 accordingly. The board separately resolves to pay her an additional £25,000 “in recognition of twelve years of loyal service”, to which she has no contractual entitlement. No members’ resolution is passed in relation to either payment.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Both payments require approval by ordinary resolution, and both are held on trust for the company.

B. Neither payment requires approval, because both arise from the director’s employment.

C. The £45,000 does not require approval, because it discharges an existing legal obligation; the £25,000 does require approval, and is held on trust for the company, with the authorising directors jointly and severally liable to indemnify it.

D. Both payments are valid, because payments for loss of office are within the board’s ordinary management powers.

E. The £25,000 requires approval by special resolution, and the £45,000 by ordinary resolution.

Q4. A company proposes to enter into a service contract with its managing director for a fixed term of four years, during which the company cannot terminate except for cause. The board approves it. No members’ resolution is passed.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The whole contract is void.

B. The contract is valid, because a director’s service contract is a matter for the board.

C. The guaranteed term is void, and the contract is deemed to contain a term entitling the company to terminate at any time on reasonable notice.

D. The contract is voidable at the instance of the company, and the director must account for any salary received.

E. The contract is valid, but the company commits a criminal offence.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. A spouse is a member of the director’s family and therefore a connected person (s.253). The asset is substantial because its value exceeds £100,000 — the two limbs of s.191 are alternatives, so it does not matter that £115,000 is under 10% of £1.2 million. Section 195 makes the arrangement voidable at the company’s instance, and the director and connected person are liable to account and indemnify.

  • A treats the two limbs as cumulative. They are alternatives.
  • B ignores the extension to connected persons.
  • D invents a special resolution requirement; s.190 needs only an ordinary resolution.
  • E is the fairness fallacy. Section 190 applies at full market value; it is about members knowing, not about the company being short-changed.

Q2 — C. This is a quasi-loan: the company pays a sum on the director’s behalf on terms that she will reimburse it. Section 198 applies only to public companies and companies associated with a public company. A stand-alone private company may make a quasi-loan of any size without members’ approval.

  • A mischaracterises it as a loan; no money was lent to the director.
  • B applies s.198 to a company outside its scope and invents a £5,000 threshold.
  • D reaches the right result by the wrong route: the £10,000 exception in s.207 would matter only if s.198 applied in the first place, and the sum here is £6,000 in any event.
  • E invents an absolute prohibition.

Q3 — C. Section 220(1)(a): a payment made in good faith in discharge of an existing legal obligation is not a payment for loss of office requiring approval, so the contractual £45,000 is outside s.217. The ex gratia £25,000 is not owed and is a payment for loss of office. Without approval, s.222(1) makes it held on trust for the company, and any director who authorised it is jointly and severally liable to indemnify the company for resulting loss.

  • A sweeps in the contractual payment.
  • B and D ignore s.217 entirely.
  • E applies the wrong resolution to the ex gratia payment and wrongly requires approval for the contractual one.

Q4 — C. Section 188 requires an ordinary resolution for a guaranteed term exceeding two years. Section 189 supplies the sanction: the offending term is void, and the contract is deemed to contain a term entitling the company to terminate at any time on reasonable notice.

  • A over-applies the sanction; only the term is void.
  • B ignores s.188.
  • D applies the s.195 / s.213 consequences, which belong to substantial property transactions and loans, not to service contracts.
  • E invents a criminal offence.

3.5 Company decision-making

3.5.1 Board meetings

The Model Articles for private companies supply the default procedure:

Article Rule
MA 3 The directors may exercise all the powers of the company
MA 5 The directors may delegate to committees or individuals
MA 7 Decisions are taken collectively, at a meeting or by unanimous written decision; MA 7(2) allows a sole director to act alone where the company has only one director and the articles require no more
MA 8 A unanimous decision may be taken without a meeting where each eligible director indicates a shared view
MA 9 Any director may call a board meeting; notice must be given to each director but need not be in writing
MA 10 Directors may participate by any means enabling them to communicate
MA 11 Quorum: two, unless the directors fix a different number; if there is only one director, one
MA 12–13 The chair is appointed by the directors and has a casting vote
MA 14 An interested director may not vote or be counted in the quorum, subject to the exceptions and to disapplication by ordinary resolution

Decisions are taken by a simple majority of those voting. There is no concept of a “special” board resolution. Minutes must be kept for 10 years (s.248).

Exam trap

Notice of a board meeting must be reasonable in the circumstances but need not be in writing, need not state the business, and need not be of any fixed length. A question in which a director complains that he was given only two hours’ oral notice is usually testing whether you know there is no statutory notice period for board meetings — the issue is whether the notice was reasonable, judged against the company’s practice and the urgency.

3.5.2 General meetings: calling and notice

Who can call one. The directors may call a general meeting (s.302). The members may require the directors to call one under s.303, and may call one themselves under s.305 if the directors do not. The court may order a meeting under s.306 where it is otherwise impracticable to call one — the provision used where a 50/50 deadlock or a member’s refusal to attend makes a quorum impossible.

The s.303 requisition. Members holding at least 5% of the paid-up capital carrying voting rights may require the directors to call a general meeting. The request must state the general nature of the business and may include the text of a proposed resolution. The directors must then call the meeting within 21 days of the request, and the meeting must be held not more than 28 days after the date of the notice convening it (s.304). If they fail, the requisitioning members (or any of them representing more than half of their total voting rights) may call the meeting themselves within three months, and the company must reimburse their reasonable expenses (s.305).

Notice (ss.307–311):

Private company Public company
General meeting 14 clear days 14 clear days
AGM (not required) 21 clear days
Short notice consent Majority in number holding 90% in nominal value of the shares giving a right to attend and vote (articles may raise this to 95%) Majority in number holding 95%

“Clear days” excludes the day of service and the day of the meeting; where notice is sent by post the articles typically deem service 48 hours after posting, so two further days must be added.

The notice must state the time, date and place of the meeting and the general nature of the business (s.311), and must include a statement of the member’s right to appoint a proxy (s.325). Notice must go to every member and every director (s.310). Accidental failure to give notice to one member is disregarded if the articles so provide (s.313).

Worked example

A private company wishes to hold a general meeting on Thursday 15 October. Notice is to be posted, and the articles deem service 48 hours after posting.

Work backwards: 14 clear days before 15 October means the notice must be served by 30 September at the latest (excluding both the day of service and the day of the meeting). Deemed service 48 hours after posting means the notice must be posted by 28 September.

If the company cannot meet that deadline, its options are to move the meeting, or to obtain short notice consent from a majority in number of the members holding at least 90% in nominal value of the voting shares.

3.5.3 General meetings: conduct

  • Quorum (s.318): two qualifying persons, unless the company has only one member, in which case one. A “qualifying person” is a member present in person, a corporate representative, or a proxy. Two proxies for the same member do not make a quorum.
  • Voting (s.284): on a show of hands, every member present in person or by proxy has one vote; on a poll, every member has one vote per share.
  • Demanding a poll (s.321): the articles may not exclude the right to demand a poll on any question other than the election of the chair or the adjournment of the meeting, and may not make ineffective a demand by not fewer than five members having the right to vote, or by members representing 10% of the total voting rights, or by members holding shares on which 10% of the total sum paid up has been paid.
  • Proxies (s.324): every member entitled to attend and vote may appoint a proxy, who need not be a member, to exercise all their rights to attend, speak and vote.
  • Minutes (s.355): kept for 10 years; minutes signed by the chair are evidence of the proceedings (s.356).

3.5.4 Resolutions

Type Majority Where used
Ordinary (s.282) Simple majority of votes cast Everything not otherwise specified: appointing and removing directors, approving SPTs, loans, payments for loss of office and service contracts, declaring a final dividend, granting authority to allot under s.551, ratification under s.239
Special (s.283) 75% of votes cast Amending the articles, changing the name, reducing capital, disapplying pre-emption rights, re-registration, winding up voluntarily, approving a buyback out of capital, directing the board under MA 4

Filing. All special resolutions, and certain ordinary resolutions (notably a s.551 authority to allot), must be filed with the Registrar within 15 days (s.30).

3.5.5 Written resolutions

Available to private companies only (s.288). The mechanics are examined precisely:

  • The eligible members are those entitled to vote on the circulation date (s.289).
  • An ordinary written resolution is passed when members representing a simple majority of the total voting rights of eligible members signify agreement; a special written resolution needs 75% of the total voting rights of eligible members (ss.282(2), 283(2)).
  • Agreement, once signified, cannot be revoked (s.296(3)).
  • The resolution lapses if not passed within 28 days of the circulation date, unless the articles specify a different period (s.297).
  • Members holding 5% of the total voting rights may require the company to circulate a written resolution (s.292), together with a statement of up to 1,000 words (s.292(3)).
  • Two resolutions cannot be passed by written resolution: removal of a director under s.168 and removal of an auditor under s.510 (s.288(2)).
Exam trap

The denominator changes. At a meeting, the majority is of the votes actually cast; on a written resolution, it is of the total voting rights of all eligible members. A member who does not respond to a written resolution is, in effect, voting against it. A question giving you an apathetic 30% shareholder and a 71% shareholder seeking a special resolution is testing exactly this: 71% of the total is not 75%, so the written resolution fails, while a general meeting at which only the 71% holder votes would pass it comfortably.

3.5.6 Informal unanimous consent: the Duomatic principle

Re Duomatic Ltd [1969] 2 Ch 365: where all the members entitled to vote on a matter assent to it, that assent is as binding as a formal resolution, even if no meeting was held and no resolution passed. Section 281(4)(a) preserves the principle by providing that nothing in Chapter 3 affects any rule of law as to things done otherwise than by passing a resolution.

Its limits matter: it requires the assent of all members entitled to vote, given with full knowledge of what is being assented to; it cannot validate something the members themselves could not authorise (an unlawful distribution, or a transaction prejudicing creditors of an insolvent company); and it does not dispense with a statutory requirement of a filed resolution or a written document where the statute demands one.

Q2. Members holding 6% of the paid-up voting capital of a private company deliver a request to the directors requiring them to call a general meeting, stating the general nature of the business and enclosing the text of a proposed resolution. The directors take no action.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The members’ only remedy is to apply to the court under s.306.

B. The directors were not obliged to act, because a request under s.303 requires members holding at least 10% of the voting capital.

C. The directors were required to call the meeting within 21 days of the request, for a date not more than 28 days after the notice convening it; having failed to do so, the members may call the meeting themselves within three months and recover their reasonable expenses from the company.

D. The directors were required to call the meeting within 14 days, and the members’ only remedy for failure is an unfair prejudice petition.

E. The members may pass the resolution by written resolution instead, whatever its subject matter.

Q3. A private company’s articles are the unamended Model Articles. It has three directors. One of them, who is abroad, is given four hours’ oral notice of a board meeting at which a significant contract is approved by the other two. He objects that the meeting was invalid.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The meeting was invalid, because notice of a board meeting must be given in writing.

B. The meeting was invalid, because at least 14 clear days’ notice is required.

C. The meeting was invalid, because a director who is abroad cannot be given valid notice.

D. The meeting was valid if the notice was reasonable in the circumstances; there is no prescribed form or minimum period for notice of a board meeting, the quorum of two was satisfied, and a simple majority approved the contract.

E. The meeting was invalid, because a board decision requires the participation of all directors.

Q4. A private company has four members holding 25% each. Three of them meet informally, without notice, and all four subsequently indicate to each other in an exchange of emails that they agree the company should sell its principal asset. No meeting is held and no resolution is recorded. The transaction is within the board’s powers, and the company is solvent.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The members’ assent is of no effect, because no general meeting was held.

B. The members’ assent is effective under the Duomatic principle, because all the members entitled to vote assented with full knowledge of the matter.

C. The members’ assent is effective only if reduced to a signed written resolution.

D. The members’ assent is effective only if 75% of them assent.

E. The members’ assent is of no effect, because informal assent cannot bind a company in any circumstances.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Section 283(2): a written resolution is passed as a special resolution if it is passed by members representing not less than 75% of the total voting rights of eligible members. The denominator is the total, not the votes cast. 72% falls short, and under s.297 the resolution lapses after 28 days.

  • A and B apply the meeting-based “votes cast” denominator, which is the central trap. Had a general meeting been held on proper notice and only the 72% holder attended and voted, the resolution would have passed.
  • D invents deemed agreement; silence is not assent.
  • E is wrong: the articles may be amended by written resolution. Only ss.168 and 510 are excluded.

Q2 — C. Section 303 requires members holding at least 5% of the paid-up voting capital; 6% qualifies. Section 304 requires the directors to call the meeting within 21 days, to be held not more than 28 days after the notice convening it. Section 305 gives the requisitionists the right to call the meeting themselves within three months and to be reimbursed their reasonable expenses.

  • A treats s.306 as the only route; it is a residual power for cases where calling a meeting is impracticable.
  • B states the wrong threshold.
  • D states the wrong period and the wrong remedy.
  • E ignores that written resolutions cannot be used for a s.168 removal, and in any event the members’ s.292 right to require circulation of a written resolution is a separate mechanism.

Q3 — D. Model Article 9: any director may call a board meeting, and notice must be given to each director but need not be in writing. There is no minimum period; the requirement is that notice be reasonable in the circumstances. The quorum under Model Article 11 is two, which was satisfied, and board decisions are taken by simple majority.

  • A contradicts MA 9(2) expressly.
  • B imports the general meeting notice period.
  • C contradicts MA 9(3), which addresses directors absent from the UK.
  • E confuses quorum with unanimity.

Q4 — B. Re Duomatic Ltd, preserved by s.281(4)(a). The unanimous informal assent of all members entitled to vote, given with full knowledge, is as effective as a formal resolution. All four assented and the company is solvent, so none of the principle’s limits is triggered.

  • A and E deny the principle altogether.
  • C imposes a formality Duomatic exists to dispense with.
  • D applies a special resolution threshold; Duomatic requires unanimity, which is present.

3.6 Records, filing and disclosure

The specification asks about “documentary, record-keeping, statutory filing and disclosure requirements”. This is dry, and it is examined, because deadlines make clean single-best-answer questions.

If your sitting’s cut-off falls before 18 November 2025, the pre-reform position — local registers for all of these — is the examinable one.

Failure to file is usually an offence by the company and every officer in default, punishable by fine, with civil penalties for late accounts that escalate with delay. Persistent default is a ground for disqualification under s.3 CDDA, and can lead the Registrar to strike the company off. Only in one case does non-compliance destroy a right: failure to register a charge within 21 days makes it void against a liquidator, an administrator and any creditor of the company (s.859H) — see Chapter 5.

3.6.3 Accounts and audit

Directors must prepare accounts for each financial year (s.394), circulate them to members (s.423), and file them (s.441). Directors are also responsible for the directors’ report (s.415), and a company that is not small must include a strategic report (s.414A), which for a large company must contain a statement describing how the directors have had regard to the s.172(1) factors.

Small company exemptions. A company that qualifies as small may file abridged or filleted accounts and, if it also qualifies for the audit exemption, need not have its accounts audited (s.477).

Thresholds — check these against your sitting

For financial years beginning on or after 6 April 2025, a company is small if it meets at least two of: turnover not more than £15 million; balance sheet total not more than £7.5 million; not more than 50 employees. A micro-entity meets two of: turnover not more than £1 million; balance sheet total not more than £500,000; not more than 10 employees. The previous small company limits were £10.2 million / £5.1 million / 50 employees.

The size test generally requires the conditions to be met (or failed) for two consecutive years before the classification changes, except in the company’s first financial year.

Audit exemption is not available, however small the company, to a public company, an authorised insurance company, a banking company, an e-money issuer, a MiFID investment firm, a UCITS management company, or a company carrying on insurance market activity, nor generally to a member of an ineligible group. And under s.476, members holding 10% or more of the nominal value of the issued share capital (or any class of it), or 10% of the members if there is no share capital, may by notice require an audit — a minority protection that survives every exemption.

3.6.4 Persons with significant control

A PSC is an individual who, in relation to the company:

  • holds, directly or indirectly, more than 25% of the shares; or
  • holds, directly or indirectly, more than 25% of the voting rights; or
  • holds the right, directly or indirectly, to appoint or remove a majority of the board; or
  • otherwise exercises, or has the right to exercise, significant influence or control; or
  • exercises, or has the right to exercise, significant influence or control over a trust or firm which itself satisfies any of the above.

The company must take reasonable steps to identify its PSCs and may serve notices requiring information; failure to respond can lead to restrictions being placed on the relevant shares. PSC particulars are filed with the Registrar, and since 18 November 2025 individual PSCs must verify their identity.

3.7 Partnership and LLP decision-making

The specification names “partnership decision-making and authority of partners” separately, and the rules are set out in full in Chapter 2. The comparison worth holding in your head:

Change in the nature of the business Unanimity (s.24(8)) Board decision; objects are unrestricted unless the articles restrict them

Exam trap

Partnership voting is per capita, not by capital or profit share, unless the agreement says otherwise. A partner with 60% of the capital has one vote under s.24(8), the same as a partner with 5%. Company voting is by shares. Mixing the two is a reliable way to lose a mark.

For an LLP, the default regulations reproduce the partnership position — every member may take part in management, ordinary matters by majority, change in the nature of the business by unanimity, no new member without unanimity, and no power of expulsion — but with the two differences noted in Chapter 2: a member may resign on reasonable notice, and no member can dissolve the LLP.

3.8 The solicitor’s position

Who is the client? When a solicitor is instructed by a company, the client is the company, not the director giving the instructions. Where the director’s personal interests diverge from the company’s — a substantial property transaction with himself, a loan, an ex gratia payment — the solicitor must be clear that they act for the company, must not advise the director personally on the same matter without addressing the conflict, and should ensure that the disinterested members of the board understand the position.

Taking instructions from one director. A solicitor may generally act on instructions from a person with authority to give them, but should be alert to a director purporting to commit the company to a transaction that requires a board or members’ resolution. Where the transaction plainly requires approval that has not been obtained, proceeding on one director’s say-so risks facilitating a breach of duty.

Confidentiality and the board. Information given to the solicitor by the company is the company’s, not any individual director’s. A director who resigns cannot demand the company’s confidential material, and a solicitor should not disclose company information to a former director without authority.

When directors and shareholders disagree. A solicitor acting for the company owes duties to the company as an entity. Advising a majority shareholder on how to remove the directors, while also acting for the company, is a conflict; the usual answer is that the solicitor must decline to act for one of them, and may need to cease acting for both.

3.9 Chapter review

Q1. A private company has three directors and four shareholders. One shareholder holds 55%, and the three directors hold 15% each. The 55% shareholder wishes to (i) remove one of the directors and (ii) change the company’s name. The articles are the unamended Model Articles.

Which of the following correctly identifies the procedure for both?

A. An ordinary resolution by written resolution for (i), and a special resolution by written resolution for (ii).

B. An ordinary resolution at a general meeting on special notice for (i), and a special resolution (which may be a written resolution) for (ii) — although with only 55% of the votes she cannot pass the special resolution.

C. A special resolution for both, at a general meeting.

D. A board resolution for (i) and a special resolution for (ii).

E. An ordinary resolution at a general meeting for both.

Q2. A director of a private company arranges for the company to lend him £30,000, repayable in twelve months, to fund the purchase of a holiday home. The company is not associated with any public company and is not a money-lending company. The director has no other outstanding transaction with the company. No members’ resolution is passed.

Which of the following is correct?

A. No approval is required, because the loan provisions apply only to public companies and companies associated with a public company.

B. No approval is required, because the loan falls within the exception in s.207(1) for minor transactions.

C. Approval by ordinary resolution, supported by a memorandum made available to the members, was required; the loan is voidable at the company’s instance and the director must account for any gain and indemnify the company for any loss.

D. Approval by special resolution was required, because the loan exceeds £15,000.

E. The loan is prohibited outright and cannot be authorised by any resolution.

Q3. The sole director and 100% shareholder of a company, having taken no advice, causes the company to make a payment that turns out to be a breach of the duty of care in s.174. The company later enters insolvent liquidation and the liquidator brings a claim.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The director may rely on his own approval as sole shareholder to ratify the breach under s.239.

B. The director may not rely on ratification, because on a resolution under s.239 his own votes as the director in question are disregarded, and in any event the Duomatic principle does not validate acts that prejudice creditors of an insolvent company.

C. The claim must fail, because a sole director cannot be in breach of duty to a company he wholly owns.

D. The claim must fail, because the company may indemnify the director under s.234.

E. The claim must succeed in full, because s.1157 relief is available only to non-executive directors.

Q4. Which of the following requires filing at Companies House within 21 days?

A. A special resolution amending the articles.

B. The appointment of a new director.

C. The creation of a charge over the company’s property.

D. A return of allotment of shares.

E. The annual accounts of a private company.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. Removal of a director requires an ordinary resolution under s.168, which cannot be passed as a written resolution (s.288(2)) and requires special notice (s.312). The 55% holder has enough votes for an ordinary resolution at a general meeting. Changing the name requires a special resolution (s.77), for which 75% is needed — and 55% is not enough, whether at a meeting or by written resolution.

  • A overlooks the s.288(2) exclusion and the 75% threshold.
  • C applies a special resolution to the removal.
  • D gives the board a removal power it does not have.
  • E applies an ordinary resolution to the change of name.

Q2 — C. Section 197 applies to all companies, private and public alike: a loan to a director requires approval by ordinary resolution, with a memorandum of the transaction made available to members for at least 15 days before the meeting and at the meeting. The exception in s.207(1) covers loans and quasi-loans whose aggregate value does not exceed £10,000, and £30,000 exceeds it. Section 213 makes the unapproved transaction voidable at the company’s instance and requires the director to account for any gain and indemnify the company for any loss.

  • A confuses s.197 with ss.198 and 201. It is quasi-loans and credit transactions that are confined to public companies and their associates. Ordinary loans are caught whatever the company.
  • B applies the £10,000 exception to a £30,000 loan.
  • D applies the wrong resolution and the wrong threshold — £15,000 is the s.207(2) limit for credit transactions, not loans, and the resolution required is an ordinary one.
  • E invents an absolute prohibition; the point of s.197 is that members may approve.

Q3 — B. Two independent obstacles. On a s.239 resolution the votes of the director in question are disregarded, so a sole director-shareholder cannot ratify his own breach by voting his own shares. And the Duomatic principle does not extend to validating acts that prejudice creditors where the company is insolvent or approaching insolvency.

  • A ignores s.239(4).
  • C contradicts Salomon: the duty is owed to the company, which is a separate person from its sole member.
  • D misapplies s.234, which permits an indemnity against liability to third parties, not to the company; s.232 makes an indemnity against liability to the company void.
  • E invents a restriction on s.1157, which is available to any officer who acted honestly and reasonably.

Q4 — C. Section 859A: particulars of a charge, with the instrument, must be delivered within 21 days beginning with the day after the date of creation. This is also the one filing whose omission has a substantive consequence — the charge becomes void against a liquidator, administrator or creditor (s.859H).

  • A — 15 days (s.30).
  • B — 14 days (s.167).
  • D — one month (s.555).
  • E — 9 months after the accounting reference date (s.442).
