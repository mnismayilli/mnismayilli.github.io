5  Financing the business

Assessment objective 4: financing a business.

5.1 Debt or equity: the choice that frames the chapter

A company that needs money has two sources, and the difference between them runs through everything below.

Equity Debt
The provider becomes a member a creditor
Return a dividend, payable only out of distributable profits, at the company’s discretion interest, payable whether or not there are profits
Tax treatment of the return in the company’s hands Not deductible — dividends are paid out of taxed profits Interest is generally deductible in computing taxable profits
Control Dilutes existing shareholders No dilution, but covenants restrict what the company may do
Capital Locked in by the maintenance of capital rules Repayable on the agreed terms
On insolvency Ranks last Ranks ahead of members; secured creditors ahead of unsecured
Security None Fixed and floating charges available
Exam trap

The tax asymmetry is a favourite. Interest on borrowings is a deduction in computing the company’s taxable trading profits; a dividend is a distribution of profit already taxed, and gets no deduction. That is why owner-managers are often advised on the split between salary, interest on a director’s loan, and dividends — and why a question that asks which structure is most tax-efficient is usually testing this line.

5.2 Equity finance

5.2.1 Shares and share capital

Every share in a limited company having a share capital must have a fixed nominal value (s.542), and an allotment purporting to be of a share without one is void. The vocabulary matters:

  • Nominal (par) value — the notional value stamped on the share, and the measure of the shareholder’s liability. A £1 share issued at £1 and paid up carries no further liability.
  • Issue price — what the company actually charges. It may be more than nominal (the excess is a premium), never less (s.580: shares must not be allotted at a discount to their nominal value — Ooregum Gold Mining Co of India Ltd v Roper [1892] AC 125).
  • Share premium account (s.610) — the premium must be credited to this account, which is treated largely as if it were share capital and is not distributable.
  • Called-up and paid-up capital — the amounts demanded and actually received.
  • Allotment and issue — shares are allotted when a person acquires the unconditional right to be entered in the register of members (s.558); they are issued when that entry is made.

Classes of share most often examined:

Class Features
Ordinary Votes, dividend at the company’s discretion, residue of capital on a winding up. The default
Preference A fixed dividend, paid in priority; normally presumed cumulative, so arrears carry forward; usually no vote and no participation in surplus capital unless expressly given
Redeemable Issued on terms that they are, or may be, redeemed
Non-voting / deferred Used to separate economic and voting rights

5.2.2 Allotting shares: the three questions

A question about a new issue of shares is always answered by working through the same three steps in the same order.

Step 1 — Do the directors have authority to allot?

  • Section 550: a private company with only one class of shares may allot shares by board decision alone, unless the articles prohibit it.
  • Section 551: in every other case (a private company with more than one class, or any public company) the directors need authority, given either by the articles or by ordinary resolution. The authority must state the maximum number of shares that may be allotted and an expiry date not more than five years away. It may be renewed, revoked or varied by ordinary resolution, and must be filed with the Registrar within 15 days (s.30).
  • An allotment made without authority is nevertheless valid (s.549(6)); the directors commit an offence.

Step 2 — Do pre-emption rights apply, and if so, have they been dealt with?

Section 561: a company must not allot equity securities to a person unless it has first offered them, on the same or more favourable terms, to each existing holder of ordinary shares in proportion as nearly as practicable to their existing holdings. The offer must be open for at least 14 days (s.562(5)).

“Equity securities” means ordinary shares or rights to subscribe for or convert into them (s.560), and “ordinary shares” excludes shares that carry a right to participate only up to a specified amount in both a distribution and a return of capital — so a straightforward preference share is outside the regime.

Exclusions — pre-emption never applies to:

  • bonus shares (s.564);
  • an allotment for a consideration that is wholly or partly non-cash (s.565); or
  • shares held under an employees’ share scheme (s.566).

Disapplication — the routes, in increasing order of specificity:

Route Company Requirement
s.567 Private only The articles may exclude ss.561–562 generally
s.569 Private with one class only Special resolution, or a provision in the articles, empowering the directors to allot as if s.561 did not apply
s.570 Any company where directors have a general s.551 authority Special resolution, or a provision in the articles
s.571 Any company Special resolution disapplying for a specific allotment, supported by a directors’ written statement of their reasons, the amount to be paid and their justification, circulated with the notice of the meeting

Contravention does not invalidate the allotment. Section 563 makes the company and every officer knowingly party to the contravention jointly and severally liable to compensate any person to whom an offer should have been made, with a two-year limitation period.

Step 3 — Is the consideration acceptable, and are the filings made?

  • A private company may accept any consideration having value. The court will not inquire into its adequacy unless it is illusory or manifestly inadequate — Re Wragg Ltd [1897] 1 Ch 796.
  • A public company must not accept an undertaking to do work or perform services (s.585); must receive at least 25% of the nominal value plus the whole of any premium on allotment (s.586); must have any non-cash consideration independently valued within the six months before the allotment (s.593); and cannot accept an undertaking to be performed more than five years after allotment (s.587).
  • Filing: a return of allotment with a statement of capital within one month (s.555); entry in the register of members; and a share certificate within two months (s.769).
Worked example

A private company has 100 ordinary shares of £1, all of one class, held 60 by P and 40 by Q. The directors wish to allot 50 new ordinary shares to a new investor for £10 each in cash. The articles are the unamended Model Articles.

  • Authority. One class, private company: s.550 applies, so the board may allot without a members’ resolution.
  • Pre-emption. Section 561 does apply — ordinary shares, cash consideration. P must be offered 30 and Q 20, for at least 14 days, before any can go to the investor. To allot them all to the investor, the members must disapply s.561: with only one class, a special resolution under s.569 will do, so P alone (60%) is not enough — Q’s agreement is needed to reach 75%.
  • Consideration. £1 nominal, £9 premium per share. £450 goes to the share premium account and £50 to share capital.
  • Filing. Return of allotment and statement of capital within one month; certificate within two months.

Change one fact: the company has ordinary and preference shares. Now s.550 does not apply, and the board also needs a s.551 authority by ordinary resolution — which P alone can pass.

5.2.3 Class rights and their variation

Class rights are rights attached to a class of shares — typically dividend, voting and capital rights. They may be set out in the articles, in the terms of issue, or in a resolution.

Variation (s.630): rights attached to a class may be varied only in accordance with provision in the articles for their variation, or, where the articles contain no such provision, with the consent of the holders of 75% in nominal value of the issued shares of that class, or by a special resolution passed at a separate general meeting of that class.

Objection (s.633): holders of not less than 15% of the issued shares of the class, who did not consent to or vote in favour of the variation, may apply to the court within 21 days to have it cancelled. The variation does not take effect until confirmed. The court may disallow the variation if satisfied that it would unfairly prejudice the shareholders of the class.

What counts as a variation is the narrow and heavily litigated point. The courts distinguish a variation of the rights themselves from a change in the enjoyment of unaltered rights:

  • Greenhalgh v Arderne Cinemas Ltd [1946] 1 All ER 512 — subdividing 10s shares into 1s shares, so that a previously blocking minority lost control on a poll, was not a variation of class rights. The right (one vote per share) was untouched; only its practical value changed.
  • White v Bristol Aeroplane Co Ltd [1953] Ch 65 — a bonus issue of new preference and ordinary shares that diluted the existing preference shareholders’ voting strength was not a variation. Their rights as such were unaffected.
Exam trap

The Greenhalgh / White line means that most of the things a minority most resents — dilution, subdivision, bonus issues — are not variations of class rights and do not trigger s.630. The minority’s route is s.994 unfair prejudice (Chapter 4), not the class rights machinery. An option offering a class consent requirement on facts of that kind is usually the distractor.

5.2.4 Transferring shares

Shares are transferable in accordance with the articles (s.544). The mechanics:

  1. The transferor executes a stock transfer form (Stock Transfer Act 1963) and delivers it with the share certificate to the transferee.
  2. Stamp duty at 0.5% of the consideration, rounded up to the nearest £5, is paid on the form — unless the consideration is £1,000 or less and the form is certified as within an exempt category.
  3. The transferee sends the stamped form and certificate to the company.
  4. Section 770: the company may not register a transfer unless a proper instrument of transfer has been delivered to it, or the transfer is exempt.
  5. The directors consider the transfer. Model Article 26(5) gives them power to refuse to register; if they do, s.771 requires them to give the transferee notice of the refusal with reasons, together with such further information about the reasons as the transferee may reasonably request, within two months.
  6. On registration, the transferee’s name goes in the register of members and a certificate is issued within two months (s.776).

Between execution and registration the transferee holds an equitable interest and the transferor holds the legal title on trust for them, but is still the member for company law purposes — which decides who may vote and who receives a dividend declared in the interim.

Transmission (Model Article 27) is different: on a member’s death or bankruptcy, the personal representative or trustee becomes entitled to the shares by operation of law, and may either be registered as a member or execute a transfer to a third party. The directors’ power to refuse registration applies as it would to a transfer by the deceased member.

Exam trap

Do not confuse a transfer (a private sale between shareholders — the company receives nothing) with an allotment (the company creates new shares and receives the money). Pre-emption rights under s.561 apply only to allotments. Pre-emption on transfer — a right of first refusal when an existing member wants to sell — exists only if the articles or a shareholders’ agreement create it.

5.2.5 Check yourself: equity finance

Q1. A private company has 200 issued ordinary shares of £1 each, its only class, held equally by two shareholders. The directors propose to allot 100 new ordinary shares to a third party for cash at £5 each. The articles are the unamended Model Articles and no resolutions have been passed.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The directors may allot the shares without any members’ resolution, because the company is private with one class of shares.

B. The directors need an ordinary resolution granting authority to allot, and a special resolution disapplying pre-emption rights.

C. The directors may allot without a s.551 authority, because s.550 applies, but they must first offer the shares to the existing members pro rata unless the members pass a special resolution disapplying s.561.

D. The directors must obtain a special resolution, because the shares are being issued at a premium.

E. The directors may allot the shares provided the existing members are given 7 days to object.

Q2. A company with an issued capital of ordinary and preference shares proposes to subdivide each existing ordinary share into ten. The effect is that a shareholder who previously held enough ordinary shares to block a special resolution will no longer do so, because new ordinary shares are to be issued to others. The rights attaching to each ordinary share are unchanged.

Which of the following is correct?

A. This is a variation of class rights and requires the consent of 75% in nominal value of the ordinary shareholders.

B. This is a variation of class rights and any 15% of the class may apply to court to cancel it.

C. This is not a variation of class rights, because the rights attaching to the shares are unaltered; only their enjoyment is affected.

D. This is a variation of class rights because it affects voting control.

E. This requires the unanimous consent of all members.

Q3. A shareholder executes a stock transfer form in favour of a buyer and hands over the form and the share certificate. Two weeks later, and before the company has registered the transfer, the company declares a final dividend.

Who is entitled to the dividend as against the company?

A. The buyer, because beneficial ownership passed on delivery of the executed form.

B. The seller, because until the transfer is registered the seller remains the member on the register.

C. Neither, because the entitlement is suspended until registration.

D. The buyer, because s.770 deems the transfer effective on delivery of a proper instrument.

E. The company may choose which of them to pay.

Q4. A public company proposes to allot shares to a supplier in exchange for the supplier’s undertaking to provide consultancy services over the next three years.

Which of the following is correct?

A. This is permissible, because a company may accept any consideration having value.

B. This is permissible provided an independent valuation is obtained.

C. This is not permissible: a public company must not accept an undertaking to do work or perform services as consideration for its shares.

D. This is permissible provided the shares are paid up as to at least 25% in cash.

E. This is permissible only if approved by special resolution.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Two separate questions, and the trap is answering only one. Authority: s.550 applies — private company, one class of shares, articles do not prohibit — so no s.551 resolution is needed. Pre-emption: s.561 applies, because these are ordinary shares allotted for cash. The existing members must be offered them pro rata for at least 14 days, unless the members disapply s.561 by special resolution (s.569, available because there is only one class) or the articles exclude the regime (s.567).

  • A answers the authority question and forgets pre-emption.
  • B answers the pre-emption question and adds an authority requirement that s.550 removes.
  • D invents a rule about premiums.
  • E invents a 7-day period; s.562(5) requires at least 14 days.

Q2 — C. Greenhalgh v Arderne Cinemas Ltd and White v Bristol Aeroplane Co Ltd. A subdivision or dilution that leaves the rights attached to each share untouched affects the enjoyment of those rights, not the rights themselves, and is not a variation for the purposes of s.630. The minority’s route, if any, is a s.994 petition.

  • A, B and D apply s.630 and s.633 to facts the case law puts outside them.
  • E invents a unanimity requirement.

Q3 — B. Membership is determined by the register of members (s.112). Until the transfer is registered, the seller remains the member and is the person entitled as against the company. In equity the seller holds the shares — and, as between the parties, the dividend — on trust for the buyer, but the company looks only to its register.

  • A and D confuse the equitable interest with membership, and misread s.770, which restricts when a company may register, not when title passes.
  • C and E invent rules.

Q4 — C. Section 585(1): a public company must not accept, in payment up of its shares or any premium, an undertaking given by any person that he or another should do work or perform services. This is one of the public-company-only capital rules, alongside ss.586, 587 and 593.

  • A states the rule for a private company (Re Wragg Ltd).
  • B applies the s.593 valuation requirement, which cannot cure a prohibited form of consideration.
  • D and E invent conditions.

5.3 Maintenance of capital

5.3.1 The principle

Trevor v Whitworth (1887) 12 App Cas 409: a limited company may not return capital to its members except in the ways the law permits. Share capital is the fund on the faith of which creditors are presumed to have given credit, and it must be kept intact. Section 658 gives the modern form: a limited company must not acquire its own shares, whether by purchase, subscription or otherwise, except in accordance with Part 18.

The permitted exits from that principle are four: a purchase of own shares, a redemption, a reduction of capital, and a lawful distribution. Each has its own machinery, and the machinery is what is examined.

5.3.2 Purchase of own shares (buyback)

Preconditions applying to every buyback:

  • The articles must not prohibit it.
  • The shares must be fully paid (s.691(1)) and must be paid for on purchase (s.691(2)).
  • The purchase must not leave only redeemable shares in issue (s.690(2)).
  • The purchase must be funded from one of the permitted sources.

Funding (s.692):

  1. Distributable profits;
  2. the proceeds of a fresh issue of shares made for the purpose of financing the purchase; or
  3. for a private company only, out of capital, either under the full Chapter 5 procedure below or under the small-scale route in s.692(1ZA) — which permits a purchase out of capital, if authorised by the articles, up to the lower of £15,000 and the nominal value of 5% of the fully paid share capital in any financial year.

Approval — off-market purchases (the normal case for a private company, since its shares are not traded on a recognised investment exchange):

  • The contract must be approved by ordinary resolution before the company enters into it (s.694), or the contract must be conditional on approval.
  • Section 695: the resolution is ineffective if the selling member votes and the resolution would not have been passed without their votes. In a written resolution, the selling member is not an eligible member at all.
  • Section 696: a copy of the contract (or a written memorandum of its terms) must be made available for inspection for at least 15 days before the meeting and at the meeting; for a written resolution, it must be sent to every eligible member with the resolution.

Afterwards:

  • The shares are cancelled, and the issued share capital reduced accordingly — or, where purchased out of distributable profits, may be held as treasury shares (ss.724–732), which carry no votes and no dividends and may later be sold, transferred under an employees’ share scheme, or cancelled.
  • A return must be delivered to the Registrar within 28 days (s.707), and notice of cancellation with a statement of capital within 28 days (s.708).
  • The contract must be retained for 10 years and be available for inspection (s.702).
  • Where the purchase is out of profits, a transfer to the capital redemption reserve maintains the capital position (s.733).

Buyback out of capital: the private company procedure (ss.709–723). This is the most procedurally detailed thing in the subject, and it is examined as a sequence:

  1. The company may pay out of capital only the permissible capital payment — the excess of the purchase price over available profits and the proceeds of any fresh issue (s.710).
  2. All the directors make a statement of solvency (s.714) specifying the permissible capital payment and stating their opinion that, immediately after the payment, the company will be able to pay its debts, and that it will be able to continue as a going concern and pay its debts as they fall due throughout the following year.
  3. An auditor’s report is annexed, stating that the auditor is not aware of anything indicating that the directors’ opinion is unreasonable (s.714(6)).
  4. A special resolution is passed within one week after the date of the directors’ statement (s.716). The seller’s votes are disregarded if the resolution would not otherwise have passed (s.717), and the statement and report must be available at the meeting (s.718).
  5. Within one week of the resolution, the company publishes a notice in the Gazette, and either in a national newspaper or by written notice to every creditor (s.719), and files the directors’ statement and auditor’s report with the Registrar no later than the first notice (s.720).
  6. Any creditor, or any member who did not consent, may apply to the court within five weeks of the resolution to cancel it (s.721).
  7. The payment must be made no earlier than five weeks and no later than seven weeks after the resolution (s.723).
Exam trap

Three of these steps generate most of the wrong answers. The special resolution must come within one week after the directors’ statement, not before it. The payment window is five to seven weeks after the resolution — not immediately, and not at large. And a public company can never buy back out of capital; if the question tells you the company is a plc, that route is closed whatever the procedure followed.

5.3.3 Redeemable shares

Sections 684–689. A private company may issue redeemable shares if its articles do not prohibit it; a public company may do so only if authorised by its articles. In either case:

  • The company must have non-redeemable shares in issue at the time (s.684(4)).
  • The terms and manner of redemption may be fixed by the directors, if they are authorised to do so by the articles or by ordinary resolution — and the terms must then be stated in the return of allotment (s.685).
  • The shares must be fully paid, and the terms must provide for payment on redemption (s.686).
  • Redemption is funded from distributable profits or the proceeds of a fresh issue; a private company may also redeem out of capital under the Chapter 5 procedure (s.687).
  • Redeemed shares are treated as cancelled on redemption (s.688).

The commercial difference from a buyback: redemption rights are built into the share on issue, so no further members’ resolution is needed at the time of redemption. A buyback is a fresh bargain and needs members’ approval.

5.3.4 Reduction of capital

Sections 641–653. Two routes:

  • Private company: special resolution supported by a solvency statement.
  • Any company (and the only route for a public company): special resolution confirmed by the court.

The solvency statement (s.643) is a statement by each of the directors that they have formed the opinion that there is no ground on which the company could then be found unable to pay its debts, and that the company will be able to pay its debts as they fall due during the year immediately following (or, if it is intended to wind up within twelve months, that it will be able to pay its debts in full within twelve months of the commencement of the winding up).

Timing and filing:

  • The statement must be made not more than 15 days before the date on which the resolution is passed (s.642(1)), and a copy must be made available to members.
  • Within 15 days after the resolution, the company delivers to the Registrar the resolution, the solvency statement, and a statement of capital (s.644).
  • The reduction takes effect on registration by the Registrar.

Making a solvency statement without reasonable grounds is a criminal offence (s.643(4)).

A reduction of capital is the standard mechanism for creating distributable reserves in a company that has accumulated losses, and for returning surplus capital to members.

5.3.5 Financial assistance

Sections 677–683. The prohibition is now confined to public companies, and knowing its scope is most of the battle:

  • Section 678: it is unlawful for a public company, or for any of its subsidiaries, to give financial assistance directly or indirectly for the purpose of the acquisition of shares in that public company, before or at the same time as the acquisition, or to reduce or discharge a liability incurred for that purpose.
  • Section 679: it is unlawful for a private company to give financial assistance for the acquisition of shares in its public holding company.
  • Financial assistance includes gifts, guarantees, security, indemnities, loans, and any other assistance that materially reduces the company’s net assets (s.677).

Exceptions:

  • Purpose exceptions (s.678(2), (4)): assistance is permitted where the company’s principal purpose is not to give it for the acquisition, or where the assistance is an incidental part of some larger purpose, and in either case it is given in good faith in the interests of the company.
  • Unconditional exceptions (s.681): a lawful dividend; a distribution in a winding up; an allotment of bonus shares; a reduction of capital; a redemption or purchase of own shares; anything done under a court-approved scheme of arrangement or a compromise with creditors.
  • Conditional exceptions (s.682): money-lending in the ordinary course of business, employees’ share schemes, and loans to employees other than directors — where the company is public, only if its net assets are not reduced, or to the extent that the assistance is provided out of distributable profits.

Consequences (s.680): the company and every officer in default commit a criminal offence. The assistance itself, and often the transaction it supports, is unenforceable.

Exam trap

The 2006 Act abolished the prohibition for private companies, along with the old “whitewash” procedure. A private company may lawfully give financial assistance for the acquisition of its own shares — subject to the directors’ duties, the maintenance of capital rules if the assistance amounts to a distribution, and the insolvency clawback provisions. An option that says a private company must follow a whitewash procedure is testing whether you know the regime changed.

5.4 Distributions

5.4.1 When a dividend may be paid

Section 829 defines a distribution as every description of distribution of a company’s assets to its members, whether in cash or otherwise, except: an issue of bonus shares; a redemption or purchase of the company’s own shares out of capital or unrealised profits; a reduction of capital; and a distribution of assets to members on a winding up.

Section 830 — the rule for all companies. A company may only make a distribution out of profits available for the purpose, defined as its accumulated, realised profits (so far as not previously utilised by distribution or capitalisation) less its accumulated, realised losses (so far as not previously written off).

Four words in that definition do all the work:

  • Accumulated — you look at the company’s whole history, not just the current year. A profitable year does not permit a dividend if past losses exceed it.
  • Realised — unrealised revaluation surpluses cannot be distributed.
  • Profits less losses — both revenue and capital profits count, provided they are realised.

Section 831 — an additional test for public companies only. A public company may make a distribution only if, and to the extent that, the amount of its net assets is not less than the aggregate of its called-up share capital and undistributable reserves, and the distribution does not reduce net assets below that aggregate.

Section 836 — the amount available must be justified by reference to relevant accounts: normally the last annual accounts, but interim accounts or initial accounts may be used where the last annual accounts do not justify the distribution.

5.4.2 Mechanics

Model Article 30: a final dividend is declared by ordinary resolution of the members, and may not exceed the amount recommended by the directors. The directors may pay interim dividends on their own authority. A declared final dividend becomes a debt owed to the members; an interim dividend does not, and may be reversed before payment.

5.4.3 Unlawful distributions

Members (s.847): a member who, at the time of the distribution, knows or has reasonable grounds for believing that it is made in contravention of Part 23 is liable to repay it to the company (or, if the distribution was other than in cash, to pay a sum equal to its value).

It’s a Wrap (UK) Ltd v Gula [2006] EWCA Civ 544 settles what must be known: it is knowledge of the facts which constitute the contravention, not knowledge that those facts amount to a breach of the Act. Directors who paid themselves dividends out of a company with no distributable profits could not escape by saying they did not know the law. Ignorance of the legal consequence is no defence.

Directors: a director who pays or permits an unlawful dividend is in breach of duty and liable to restore the sum to the company — Bairstow v Queens Moat Houses plc [2001] EWCA Civ 712, where directors were liable for the full amount of unlawful dividends paid, not merely for the company’s net loss. Section 1157 relief may be available where the director acted honestly and reasonably.

Exam trap

The three-way distinction: an unlawful dividend is one paid without distributable profits; a preference is a payment that improperly advantages one creditor before an insolvency; a transaction at an undervalue is a disposal for significantly less than the company receives. A dividend can be attacked as all three on the same facts, and Sequana confirms that a dividend can be a transaction at an undervalue under s.423. Read the question to see which the examiner has set up.

5.4.4 Check yourself: capital and distributions

Q1. A private company wishes to buy back 5,000 of its £1 ordinary shares from a retiring shareholder for £120,000. Its distributable profits are £40,000 and it does not propose to issue new shares. The articles permit purchases out of capital.

Which of the following correctly describes the requirements?

A. An ordinary resolution approving the contract is sufficient, because the company is private.

B. The company may pay £40,000 out of profits and the balance out of capital, following the Chapter 5 procedure: a directors’ statement of solvency supported by an auditor’s report, a special resolution passed within one week of the statement, notice in the Gazette and to creditors, and payment between five and seven weeks after the resolution.

C. The company may not buy back the shares at all, because its distributable profits are less than the purchase price.

D. The company must first reduce its capital by special resolution and solvency statement, and may then buy back the shares out of profits.

E. The company may pay the whole £120,000 out of capital under s.692(1ZA), because the articles authorise purchases out of capital.

Q2. A company’s accumulated realised profits are £300,000 and its accumulated realised losses are £260,000. It has an unrealised revaluation surplus on its freehold premises of £500,000. The directors propose a dividend of £200,000.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The dividend is lawful, because the company’s total reserves exceed £500,000.

B. The dividend is lawful up to £40,000 only; profits available for distribution are accumulated realised profits less accumulated realised losses, and the revaluation surplus is unrealised.

C. The dividend is lawful, because a revaluation surplus is a realised profit once the revaluation is recognised in the accounts.

D. The dividend is lawful up to £300,000, because accumulated realised losses are disregarded.

E. The dividend is unlawful in its entirety, because no dividend may be paid while accumulated losses remain.

Q3. A shareholder who is also a director receives a dividend of £70,000. The company in fact had no distributable profits, and the shareholder knew the figures in the management accounts but believed, wrongly, that a dividend could lawfully be paid out of the year’s trading profit while past losses were carried forward.

Which of the following is correct?

A. She need not repay the dividend, because she did not know that the payment was unlawful.

B. She must repay the dividend, because s.847 requires only knowledge of the facts constituting the contravention, not knowledge that they render the distribution unlawful.

C. She must repay only the amount by which the company was left unable to pay its creditors.

D. She need not repay the dividend, because a dividend once declared is a debt due to the shareholder.

E. She must repay the dividend only if the company enters liquidation within two years.

Q4. A private company gives a guarantee to a bank to support a loan that the bank is making to an individual to enable him to buy the entire issued share capital of that company. The company is not a subsidiary of any public company.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The guarantee is unlawful financial assistance and the company commits a criminal offence.

B. The guarantee is lawful only if a whitewash procedure is followed, involving a statutory declaration of solvency and an auditor’s report.

C. The prohibition on financial assistance applies only to public companies and to private subsidiaries of public companies; the guarantee is not unlawful under ss.678–679, though the directors must still consider their duties and the maintenance of capital and insolvency rules.

D. The guarantee is lawful only if approved by special resolution.

E. The guarantee is void, because a company may not assist a person to acquire its own shares in any circumstances.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. Section 692 permits a purchase out of distributable profits, the proceeds of a fresh issue, or (for a private company) out of capital. The permissible capital payment under s.710 is the excess of the price over available profits — £120,000 less £40,000 = £80,000 — and Chapter 5 supplies the machinery: directors’ solvency statement (s.714) with auditor’s report, special resolution within one week of the statement (s.716), Gazette and creditor notice within one week of the resolution (s.719), filings (s.720), a five-week window for objection (s.721), and payment between five and seven weeks after the resolution (s.723).

  • A gives only the s.694 contract approval and omits the entire out-of-capital procedure.
  • C treats insufficient profits as an absolute bar; it is a bar only for a public company.
  • D describes a workable but unnecessary alternative, and is not what the question asks about.
  • E misapplies s.692(1ZA), which caps the small-scale route at the lower of £15,000 and 5% of the fully paid share capital. £120,000 far exceeds it.

Q2 — B. Section 830: profits available for distribution are accumulated realised profits less accumulated realised losses. £300,000 − £260,000 = £40,000. An unrealised revaluation surplus is not a realised profit and cannot be distributed.

  • A and C treat the revaluation surplus as distributable.
  • D ignores accumulated losses, which is exactly what “accumulated” prevents.
  • E is too absolute: past losses reduce the available profits, they do not extinguish the ability to pay any dividend at all.

Q3 — B. It’s a Wrap (UK) Ltd v Gula. Section 847 requires knowledge of the facts constituting the contravention. She knew the company’s financial position; a mistake about the legal effect of those facts is no answer. She must repay.

  • A is the error the Court of Appeal rejected.
  • C invents a net loss measure; compare Bairstow, where directors were liable for the full amount.
  • D confuses the general rule that a declared final dividend is a debt with the position where the distribution was unlawful in the first place.
  • E invents a limitation trigger.

Q4 — C. Sections 678 and 679 prohibit financial assistance only where the shares being acquired are in a public company. The Companies Act 2006 abolished the prohibition for private companies and, with it, the whitewash procedure. The guarantee is not caught — though the directors must still act within ss.171–177, and if the guarantee is called it may be attacked under the insolvency clawback provisions.

  • A applies the public company rule to a private company.
  • B describes the abolished whitewash procedure.
  • D and E invent requirements.

5.5 Debt finance

5.5.1 The instruments

  • Overdraft — a facility on a current account, repayable on demand. Flexible and expensive, and its on-demand nature makes it dangerous for a company under pressure.
  • Term loan — a fixed sum for a fixed period, drawn in one or more tranches, repayable by instalments or at the end (a bullet repayment). Governed by a facility agreement containing representations, covenants and events of default.
  • Revolving credit facility — a limit within which the company may draw, repay and redraw.
  • Debenture — in company law, a document that acknowledges or creates a debt; in practice, the security document a bank takes over a company’s assets, or a series of loan stock issued to investors. Debentures are registrable in the same way as any other charge, and a company must keep a register of debenture holders if it has one.

Covenants are the lender’s real control: financial covenants (gearing, interest cover), negative covenants (no further borrowing, no disposals, no dividends, negative pledge — no further security ranking ahead), and information covenants. Breach triggers an event of default, which accelerates the loan.

5.5.2 Fixed and floating charges

This is the most consequential distinction in the chapter, because it decides who gets paid.

A fixed charge attaches to specific, identified assets at the moment of creation. The chargor cannot dispose of them without the chargee’s consent. Typical subjects: land, buildings, plant and machinery, shares, intellectual property.

A floating charge hovers over a class of assets which changes in the ordinary course of business. Re Yorkshire Woolcombers Association Ltd [1903] 2 Ch 284, Romer LJ’s three characteristics:

  1. it is a charge on a class of assets, present and future;
  2. that class is one which, in the ordinary course of the company’s business, changes from time to time; and
  3. until some step is taken by the chargee, the company may carry on its business and deal with the assets in the ordinary course.

Only a body corporate can grant a floating charge — which is one of the practical reasons a business incorporates.

The label is irrelevant. Two cases decide this, and they are examinable by name because the principle is what practitioners call by them:

  • Agnew v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (Re Brumark Investments Ltd) [2001] UKPC 28 — a two-stage approach: construe the instrument to find the rights and obligations the parties intended, then categorise the charge as a matter of law. The parties cannot choose the label.
  • Re Spectrum Plus Ltd [2005] UKHL 41 — a charge over book debts which required the proceeds to be paid into an account with the chargee bank, but permitted the company to draw freely on that account, was a floating charge, whatever it called itself. Siebe Gorman was overruled. The decisive question is whether the chargor retains control over the proceeds.

Crystallisation converts a floating charge into a fixed charge over the assets then within its scope. It occurs on:

  • the commencement of winding up;
  • the appointment of an administrator or a receiver;
  • the company ceasing to carry on business;
  • any event specified in the charge instrument as causing automatic crystallisation; or
  • the giving of notice by the chargee, where the instrument so provides.
Exam trap

Crystallisation does not improve the charge holder’s ranking. A crystallised floating charge is still, for the purposes of the insolvency priority rules and s.245 IA 1986, a floating charge — it is defined by reference to how it was created, not to its state at the relevant date. Candidates who think crystallisation promotes the holder above the preferential creditors and the prescribed part get the whole distribution question wrong.

5.5.3 Why the distinction matters

Fixed charge Floating charge
Ranking on insolvency Paid first, out of the charged asset After the expenses of the liquidation, the preferential creditors and the prescribed part
Vulnerability Ordinary clawback provisions only Additionally vulnerable under s.245 IA 1986
Chargor’s freedom to deal None without consent Free, in the ordinary course, until crystallisation
Appointment of administrator out of court No Yes, if the holder is a qualifying floating charge holder

5.5.4 Registering charges

Section 859A: where a company creates a charge, the company (or any person interested in it) must deliver to the Registrar the prescribed particulars, with a certified copy of the instrument, before the end of 21 days beginning with the day after the date of creation.

Section 859H — the consequence of failure, and the reason this deadline is worth memorising. If the charge is not registered in time, it is void against a liquidator, an administrator and any creditor of the company. The charge remains valid against the company itself, but the sum secured becomes immediately payable. In practice a lender whose charge is unregistered is an unsecured creditor in the only situation where security mattered.

Section 859F allows the court to extend the period where the failure was accidental, due to inadvertence or some other sufficient cause, or is not of a nature to prejudice creditors or shareholders, or where it is otherwise just and equitable — usually on terms preserving the rights of those who acquired rights in the interim.

Section 859I(6): the Registrar’s certificate of registration is conclusive evidence that the documents were delivered before the end of the 21-day period.

Charges over land must additionally be registered at HM Land Registry to bind third parties.

5.5.5 Priority between charges

The working rules:

  • A fixed charge over an asset ranks ahead of a floating charge over the same asset, even if the floating charge was created first — because the company was free to deal with the asset in the ordinary course.
  • Between fixed charges, the general rule is order of creation, subject to the rules about legal and equitable interests and notice, and to registration at HM Land Registry where the asset is land.
  • Between floating charges over the same assets, order of creation.
  • A negative pledge clause in a floating charge prohibits the company from creating any later charge ranking ahead. Its efficacy depends on notice: the particulars delivered under s.859D allow the negative pledge to be recorded on the register, so a later chargee who searches is fixed with notice and cannot obtain priority.
Worked example

In January, Bank A takes a debenture containing a floating charge over the whole of the company’s undertaking, including a negative pledge, and registers it within 21 days with the negative pledge noted. In June, Bank B takes a fixed legal mortgage over the company’s warehouse and registers within 21 days. The company goes into liquidation in December.

Without the negative pledge, Bank B’s later fixed charge would rank ahead of Bank A’s earlier floating charge over the warehouse. With the negative pledge noted on the register, Bank B is taken to have had notice of it, so it cannot take priority: Bank A ranks first over the warehouse.

Change one fact: Bank A registered 30 days after creation. Its charge is void against the liquidator and against any creditor under s.859H. Bank A is an unsecured creditor; Bank B’s fixed charge stands.

5.6 Accounting requirements

Financing questions frequently end with a filing or accounting requirement, and the details are set out in Chapter 3. The points that bear specifically on finance:

  • The company must keep adequate accounting records sufficient to show and explain its transactions, disclose its financial position with reasonable accuracy at any time, and enable the directors to ensure the accounts comply with the Act (s.386). Retention: 3 years (private), 6 years (public).
  • Distributions must be justified by reference to relevant accounts (s.836), which is why a company proposing an interim dividend part way through a loss-making year needs interim accounts.
  • Filing deadlines: accounts within 9 months of the accounting reference date (private) or 6 months (public); the confirmation statement within 14 days of the end of each review period.
  • Audit exemption thresholds and the s.476 right of 10% of members to require an audit are set out in Chapter 3.

5.7 The solicitor’s position

Acting for lender and borrower. A firm asked to act for both the bank and the company on a secured facility faces an obvious conflict of interest: the terms of the facility and of the security are matters on which their interests differ. The narrow exceptions in the Code of Conduct — substantially common interest, or competing for the same objective — will rarely be satisfied on a negotiated facility. Where the terms are entirely standard and not open to negotiation, and both clients give informed written consent, acting may be possible with effective safeguards; the safer and usually correct answer is that separate representation is required.

Personal guarantees. Where a director or their spouse is asked to guarantee the company’s borrowing, or to charge the family home, the solicitor must advise the guarantor separately and be alert to the risk of undue influence. A lender who is on notice that a guarantee is not to the guarantor’s financial advantage, and that there is a relationship of trust, will require confirmation that the guarantor has received independent legal advice.

Registering the charge. The 21-day deadline in s.859A is a classic negligence trap. Missing it makes the charge void against a liquidator, an administrator and any creditor, and the loss falls on the lender — and then on the firm.

5.8 Chapter review

Q1. A bank takes a charge over a company’s book debts. The charge document is expressed to create a “first fixed charge”. It requires all proceeds to be paid into the company’s account with the bank, but the company is free to draw on that account for its ordinary trading purposes without restriction. The company goes into liquidation.

How does the charge rank?

A. As a fixed charge, because it is expressed to be one and the parties’ intention governs.

B. As a fixed charge, because the proceeds must be paid into an account with the chargee.

C. As a floating charge, because the company retained the freedom to deal with the proceeds; it therefore ranks after the expenses of the liquidation, the preferential creditors and the prescribed part.

D. As a fixed charge over the debts and a floating charge over the proceeds.

E. The charge is void, because a charge over future book debts cannot be created.

Q2. A company creates a charge on 1 March. The particulars and a certified copy of the instrument are delivered to the Registrar on 28 March. The company enters administration in September.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The charge is valid, because it was registered within one month of creation.

B. The charge is void against the administrator and any creditor, because it was not delivered within 21 days beginning with the day after the date of creation; the secured sum became immediately payable.

C. The charge is valid, because the Registrar issued a certificate of registration.

D. The charge is void against the company itself as well as against the administrator.

E. The charge is valid, because the 21-day period runs from the date the instrument is executed by all parties.

Q3. A private company’s articles are silent on the purchase of its own shares. The board wishes to buy back shares from a departing shareholder using the company’s substantial distributable profits.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The buyback is prohibited, because the articles do not authorise it.

B. The buyback may proceed: the articles need only not prohibit the purchase; the contract must be approved by ordinary resolution before the company enters into it, the shares must be fully paid and paid for on purchase, the selling member’s votes are disregarded if the resolution would not otherwise have passed, and the contract must be available for inspection for 15 days before the meeting.

C. The buyback requires a special resolution and a solvency statement.

D. The buyback requires the Chapter 5 out-of-capital procedure, whatever the source of funds.

E. The buyback is prohibited by s.658 in all circumstances.

Q4. A company proposes to allot shares to raise capital. Which of the following, on its own, removes the need to offer the shares to existing ordinary shareholders first?

A. The shares are being allotted at a premium.

B. The shares are being allotted wholly in consideration for a non-cash asset.

C. The directors have authority to allot under s.551.

D. The company is a private company with only one class of shares.

E. The allottee has agreed to a lock-up preventing resale for three years.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Re Spectrum Plus Ltd [2005] UKHL 41, applying Agnew v Commissioner of Inland Revenue. The label is irrelevant; what matters is whether the chargor retained control over the proceeds. Free drawing rights mean the charge is floating, so it ranks after the expenses of the liquidation, the preferential creditors and the prescribed part.

  • A and B are the two propositions Spectrum Plus rejected — the second was the reasoning of Siebe Gorman, which was overruled.
  • D splits the charge in a way the House of Lords declined to accept.
  • E is wrong: a charge over present and future book debts is perfectly valid.

Q2 — B. Section 859A requires delivery within 21 days beginning with the day after the date of creation. Creation on 1 March means the period ends on 22 March; delivery on 28 March is late. Section 859H makes the charge void against a liquidator, an administrator and any creditor, and the sum secured becomes immediately payable. Relief is available only on an application under s.859F.

  • A applies a month, not 21 days.
  • C misreads s.859I(6): the certificate is conclusive that the documents were delivered before the end of the period — which cannot be true where they were not, and in any event the Registrar would not have accepted a late filing without a court order.
  • D overstates the sanction: the charge remains valid against the company.
  • E invents a start date; the period runs from creation.

Q3 — B. Sections 690–696. There is no requirement that the articles authorise a buyback; the requirement is that they do not prohibit it. The shares must be fully paid (s.691(1)) and paid for on purchase (s.691(2)); the off-market contract must be approved by ordinary resolution before the company enters into it (s.694); the selling member’s votes are disregarded if the resolution would not have passed without them (s.695); and the contract must be available for inspection for 15 days before the meeting and at it (s.696).

  • A reverses the test.
  • C imports the reduction of capital procedure.
  • D imports the out-of-capital procedure, which is needed only where profits and fresh issue proceeds are insufficient.
  • E ignores that s.658 is expressly subject to Part 18.

Q4 — B. Section 565: pre-emption rights do not apply to an allotment of equity securities that are paid up otherwise than in cash, wholly or partly. This is an exclusion that operates automatically, without any resolution.

  • A is irrelevant; a premium has nothing to do with pre-emption.
  • C addresses authority to allot, which is a different question from pre-emption. Section 550 or s.551 gets the directors past step one; s.561 still has to be dealt with at step two.
  • D likewise addresses authority under s.550. Being a private company with one class makes the s.569 disapplication route available, but a special resolution is still needed — the status alone does not remove the requirement.
  • E is irrelevant.
