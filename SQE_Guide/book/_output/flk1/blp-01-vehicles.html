2  Choosing and forming the vehicle

Assessment objective 1: starting a new business through the vehicle of a company, partnership, LLP or as a sole trader.

2.1 Why the choice matters

A client starting a business is really choosing among five trade-offs, and almost every exam question on this topic is testing one of them.

Liability. Will the founders’ personal assets be exposed if the business fails? This is the first question most clients ask and the one they most often misunderstand.

Tax. Is the business itself taxed, or are the owners taxed on its profits directly? The difference between a transparent vehicle and an opaque one drives a great deal of real-world structuring advice, and it is the reason the tax chapter and this chapter have to be learned together.

Formality and cost. Some vehicles come into existence by accident. Others require registration, filings, accounts and a constitution.

Publicity. Incorporation buys limited liability at the price of putting your accounts, officers and ownership on a public register that anyone — including your competitors — can search for free.

Raising money and getting out. A company can issue shares, grant a floating charge and be sold by a share transfer. A sole trade cannot do any of those things.

Exam trap

The LLP is the hybrid, and the hybrid is where questions live. It is a body corporate with separate legal personality and limited liability — but it is taxed transparently, like a partnership. Any option telling you an LLP pays corporation tax on its profits is wrong. Any option telling you an LLP has no separate legal personality is also wrong. Candidates who have half-learned the point reliably pick one or the other.

2.3 The sole trader

There is nothing to form. A person who starts trading on their own account is a sole trader, and the business has no legal existence separate from them. Its assets are their assets, its debts are their debts, and its profits are their income.

The absence of separate personality is the whole point, and it has consequences that questions like to test:

  • The sole trader cannot contract with the business, because there is no second party.
  • The sole trader cannot be employed by the business, so there is no employment income, no PAYE on their own drawings, and no Class 1 National Insurance on what they take out.
  • The sole trader cannot limit exposure to creditors. A trade creditor sues the individual, and can enforce against the family home.
  • The business cannot grant a floating charge, because that is a creature of company law. A sole trader can grant fixed security, and can grant a general assignment of book debts, but the floating charge is not available.
  • The business cannot survive its owner. On death the trade does not pass as an entity; the assets pass under the will or intestacy.

Against that: no registration, no constitution, no public filing, no audit, and complete freedom to take money out whenever the client likes.

Tax position in one line. Trading profits are charged to income tax, computed on the tax year basis, together with Class 4 National Insurance; Class 2 has effectively been abolished for those with profits above the small profits threshold, who receive a National Insurance credit instead. See Chapter 6.

Names. Regulation reaches even here. The restrictions on business names in Part 41 of the Companies Act 2006, and the associated regulations, apply to sole traders and partnerships and not only to companies: a sole trader may not use a name suggesting a connection with government, may not use words requiring approval without it, and may not use “limited”, “Ltd”, “LLP” or “plc” at all. Where a sole trader or partnership trades under a name other than the proprietors’ own surnames, the names and an address for service must appear on business letters, order forms, invoices, receipts and the business website, and be displayed at any premises to which customers have access.

Exam trap

“Sole trader” describes ownership, not staffing. A sole trader may have fifty employees. What makes them a sole trader is that one person owns the business and bears its risk. An option that says a person cannot be a sole trader because they employ staff is wrong.

2.4 The general partnership

2.4.1 How it arises

Section 1(1) of the Partnership Act 1890 defines partnership as “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”.

Read that carefully. It says nothing about agreement, documentation, or intention to form a partnership. If the elements are present, the relation exists. Three elements, each doing work:

  • A business. Section 45 defines business to include every trade, occupation or profession. A single joint venture can qualify, but passive co-ownership of property does not: s.2(1) provides that joint tenancy, tenancy in common, joint property or part ownership does not of itself create a partnership, whether or not the co-owners share profits from its use.
  • Carried on in common. There must be a mutual undertaking, not one person trading and another lending or advising.
  • With a view of profit. The parties must intend to make a profit, though they need not make one. A members’ club or a not-for-profit venture fails this limb.

Section 2 supplies the interpretive rules that questions are built on:

Provision Rule
s.2(1) Co-ownership does not of itself create a partnership
s.2(2) Sharing gross returns does not of itself create a partnership
s.2(3) Receipt of a share of the profits is prima facie evidence of partnership — but not conclusive
s.2(3)(a)–(e) Five relationships that are not partnership despite profit-sharing: repayment of a debt by instalments out of profits; remuneration of an employee or agent by a share of profits; an annuity to a deceased partner’s widow, widower, civil partner or child; a loan at a rate of interest varying with profits, where the contract is in writing and signed; and the sale of goodwill paid for by an annuity out of profits
Exam trap

Partnership arises by conduct. A scenario in which two people never signed anything, never used the word “partnership”, and would deny being partners can still be a partnership — and each will then be liable for the other’s business debts without limit. The examiner’s favourite version is two friends who “just started working together”. Look for business, in common, with a view of profit. The mirror-image trap is a scenario built on s.2(3)(d): a lender whose return varies with profits under a signed written agreement is a creditor, not a partner, however profit-dependent the return looks.

In England and Wales a partnership has no separate legal personality. The firm is the partners. (Scots law differs — a distraction the examiner sometimes uses. Note also that a partnership may sue and be sued in the firm name under the Civil Procedure Rules; that is a procedural convenience, not legal personality.)

2.4.2 The default terms

Where the partners have no agreement, or their agreement is silent, the Act supplies terms. These are heavily examined, because the question usually turns on what happens absent an agreement.

Section Default rule
s.19 The partners may vary the terms by consent, express or inferred from a course of dealing
s.20–21 Partnership property must be held and applied exclusively for the firm
s.24(1) Partners share equally in capital and profits, and contribute equally to losses — regardless of unequal capital contributions
s.24(2) The firm must indemnify a partner for payments and liabilities properly incurred in the ordinary conduct of the business
s.24(3) A partner who makes an advance beyond their agreed capital is entitled to 5% interest on it
s.24(4) A partner is not entitled to interest on their capital before profits are ascertained
s.24(5) Every partner may take part in management
s.24(6) No partner is entitled to a salary
s.24(7) No new partner may be introduced without unanimous consent
s.24(8) Ordinary matters decided by majority; a change in the nature of the business requires unanimity
s.24(9) Partnership books to be kept at the place of business; every partner may inspect and copy them
s.25 No majority may expel a partner unless the agreement says so
s.26 A partnership at will may be dissolved by any partner giving notice — with immediate effect
s.28 Duty to render true accounts and full information to any partner or their legal representatives
s.29 Duty to account for private profits derived from any transaction concerning the partnership, or from use of partnership property, name or business connection
s.30 Duty not to compete: a partner who carries on a competing business without consent must account for the profits
Exam trap

Section 24(1) produces the unfair-looking answer. A partner who contributed £90,000 of the capital and a partner who contributed £10,000 share profits equally unless they agreed otherwise. Candidates instinctively apportion by contribution. The Act does not. Note the pairing with s.24(3)–(4): an advance attracts 5% interest, capital attracts none. Questions exploit the difference by describing money paid in and asking what return it earns.

Worked example

Dev, Elena and Farid trade as consultants with no written agreement. Dev put in £60,000 of capital, Elena £30,000, Farid nothing. Elena separately lent the firm £20,000 last year, described in the firm’s books as a loan. In the year just ended the firm made £120,000.

Farid wants to bring in his brother as a partner; Dev and Elena refuse. Elena wants to be paid £40,000 for the extra hours she has worked, and Dev proposes the firm move from consultancy into property development, with himself and Elena in favour and Farid against.

  • Profit share. £40,000 each — s.24(1), equal shares regardless of contributions.
  • Elena’s loan. She is entitled to 5% interest on the £20,000 advance — s.24(3). Neither she nor Dev is entitled to interest on capital — s.24(4).
  • Elena’s salary. Nothing. Section 24(6): no partner is entitled to a salary, however much more work they do.
  • The new partner. Blocked. Section 24(7) requires unanimity, and two partners object.
  • The change of business. Blocked. A change in the nature of the business requires unanimity under s.24(8), so Farid’s single vote defeats it — even though the other two are a majority and hold 75% of the capital.

2.4.3 Authority and liability to outsiders

Section 5 is the partnership equivalent of agency, and it is the most examined provision in the Act. Every partner is an agent of the firm and of the other partners. A partner’s act binds the firm where it is for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm — unless the partner had no authority and the third party either knew that or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Both limbs of the exception are needed to escape liability.

The analysis therefore runs in this order:

  1. Was the act of a kind usually done in this type of business? If not, s.5 is not engaged at all and the firm is bound only if the partner had actual authority.
  2. If it was, did the partner have actual authority (express, implied, or from a course of dealing)? If yes, the firm is bound and the question ends.
  3. If not, did the third party know of the lack of authority, or not know or believe the person to be a partner? Only then is the firm free.
Worked example

Anna, Ben and Chi are partners in a firm of architects. Their agreement says no partner may commit the firm to more than £5,000 without the others’ consent. Anna orders £12,000 of drafting software from a supplier who knows nothing of the restriction.

Is the firm bound? Ordering professional software is an act for carrying on in the usual way the business of a firm of architects, so s.5 is engaged. Anna lacked actual authority — but the supplier neither knew that nor doubted she was a partner. The firm is bound. The internal restriction is enforceable between the partners, not against the supplier; Anna must indemnify her partners.

Change one fact: the supplier had been told of the £5,000 limit. Now both limbs are satisfied and the firm is not bound.

Change a different fact: Anna orders £12,000 of racing bicycles. This is not an act for carrying on in the usual way the business of a firm of architects. Section 5 is not engaged, Anna had no actual authority, and the firm is not bound even though the supplier knew nothing.

Related provisions worth knowing precisely:

  • s.6 — an act or instrument relating to the firm’s business, executed in the firm name or in any way showing an intention to bind the firm, binds the firm and all the partners.
  • s.9 — partners are jointly liable for the firm’s debts and obligations incurred while they are a partner; the estate of a deceased partner is also severally liable, subject to prior payment of separate debts.
  • ss.10–12 — partners are jointly and severally liable for wrongs (torts and other wrongful acts or omissions) committed in the ordinary course of the firm’s business or with the co-partners’ authority, and for the misapplication of money or property received by the firm.
  • s.14 — a person who represents themselves, or knowingly allows themselves to be represented, as a partner is liable as one to anyone who gives credit on the faith of that representation (holding out). The representation need not be made by the person to the creditor directly, but the creditor must have relied on it.
  • s.17(1) — an incoming partner is not liable for debts incurred before they joined.
  • s.17(2) — an outgoing partner remains liable for debts incurred while they were a partner. A retiring partner escapes only by novation — a tripartite agreement with the creditor and the continuing partners.
  • s.36 — an outgoing partner remains liable for later debts to those who dealt with the firm before the change, unless notice is given: actual notice to existing customers, and London Gazette notice as against those who had no prior dealings.
  • s.38 — after dissolution, the partners’ authority continues so far as necessary to wind up the firm and complete unfinished transactions.
Exam trap

The retiring partner who “told everyone” is a standing favourite. Retirement alone does not end exposure to future creditors who previously dealt with the firm. Actual notice to those customers is what protects against them; the Gazette notice protects against strangers. And note the asymmetry the examiner exploits: a new customer who never dealt with the firm before the retirement could not have relied on the retiring partner’s membership, so on those facts the absence of Gazette notice does not itself create liability.

2.4.4 What a partnership agreement adds

The specification names “common provisions in partnership agreements”. The point of every one of them is to displace a default that would otherwise produce an unwanted result:

Clause Default it displaces
Profit and capital sharing in agreed proportions s.24(1) equal sharing
Salaries or “prior shares” for working partners s.24(6) no salary
Interest on capital s.24(4) no interest
Fixed term or requirement of notice to dissolve s.26 dissolution on immediate notice
Expulsion clause s.25 no power to expel
Continuation on death or retirement, with a mechanism to buy out the outgoing partner’s share s.33 automatic dissolution on death or bankruptcy
Decision-making thresholds for specified matters s.24(8) majority / unanimity split
Restrictive covenants (non-compete, non-solicitation) Nothing — the Act is silent, and covenants must be reasonable to be enforceable
Authority limits on individual partners Nothing as against third parties; effective only internally (see s.5)
Exam trap

A partnership at will — one with no fixed term and no agreed notice requirement — can be dissolved by any one partner at any time, with immediate effect and without giving a reason (ss.26 and 32(c)). Candidates assume a reasonable notice period. There is none. This is the single most commercially dangerous feature of an unwritten partnership, and it is examined by scenarios in which one partner announces dissolution during a dispute.

2.4.5 Limited partnerships: a footnote worth one sentence

A limited partnership under the Limited Partnerships Act 1907 is a different animal from a limited liability partnership. It must be registered, must have at least one general partner with unlimited liability, and its limited partners enjoy limited liability only so long as they take no part in management — a limited partner who manages becomes liable as a general partner for the debts incurred while they did so. Limited partnerships have no separate legal personality in England and Wales. They survive mainly as fund vehicles.

Exam trap

“Limited partnership” (LPA 1907) and “limited liability partnership” (LLPA 2000) are not synonyms, and an option that describes one using the features of the other is a deliberate distractor.

2.4.6 Check yourself: sole traders and partnerships

Q1. Two sisters begin restoring and reselling vintage furniture together. They have no written agreement and have never described themselves as partners. They share the proceeds of each sale equally after deducting the cost of materials, and both work on every piece. One sister orders £8,000 of restoration materials from a regular supplier, without telling her sister, and the business then fails owing the supplier in full.

Which of the following best describes the other sister’s position?

A. She has no liability, because there was no agreement to form a partnership and she did not authorise the order.

B. She is liable for half of the debt only, because the sisters shared proceeds equally.

C. She is liable in full for the debt, because the sisters were carrying on a business in common with a view of profit and the order was made in the usual way of that business.

D. She has no liability, because sharing gross returns does not create a partnership.

E. She is liable only if the supplier can show that she personally represented herself as a partner.

Q2. Three partners have no written agreement. Ravi contributed £150,000 of capital, Sasha £50,000, and Tom nothing. Tom devotes six days a week to the business; Ravi devotes one. The firm’s profit for the year is £180,000. Tom asks to be paid a salary of £30,000 in recognition of the extra work, and the others refuse.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Tom is entitled to a salary of a reasonable amount, because he does substantially more work.

B. Tom is entitled to no salary, and the profit is shared £90,000 to Ravi, £30,000 to Sasha and £60,000 to Tom, in proportion to capital and effort.

C. Tom is entitled to no salary, and the profit is shared equally at £60,000 each.

D. Tom is entitled to no salary, and the profit is shared £135,000 to Ravi and £45,000 to Sasha in proportion to capital, with nothing to Tom.

E. Tom is entitled to no salary, but is entitled to interest at 5% on the value of his labour.

Q3. A partner retires from a firm of surveyors. The firm writes to every existing client informing them of her retirement, but no notice is placed in the London Gazette. Six months later the firm contracts with a new client who had never dealt with the firm before, and fails to perform. The new client sues the retired partner.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The retired partner is liable, because no notice was placed in the London Gazette.

B. The retired partner is not liable, because the new client had no previous dealings with the firm and cannot have relied on her being a partner.

C. The retired partner is liable, because a partner remains liable for two years after retirement.

D. The retired partner is not liable, because she is protected by s.17(1) of the Partnership Act 1890.

E. The retired partner is liable jointly and severally with the continuing partners, because the claim is for breach of contract.

Q4. A solicitor advises a client who has lent £100,000 to a trading firm. Under a written agreement signed by both parties, the client receives interest at a rate that rises and falls with the firm’s annual profits. The client takes no part in management. The firm becomes insolvent.

Which of the following best describes the client’s position?

A. The client is a partner, because receipt of a share of profits is conclusive evidence of partnership.

B. The client is a partner, because the return varies with profits.

C. The client is not a partner, but is liable by holding out because the loan was publicised.

D. The client is not a partner, and ranks as a creditor of the firm, though the loan is postponed to the claims of other creditors.

E. The client is not a partner, and ranks equally with all other unsecured creditors of the firm.

Q5. Two partners in a partnership at will fall out. One of them writes to the other stating that the partnership is dissolved with immediate effect. The partnership agreement — there is none — does not deal with dissolution, and the other partner objects that a reasonable period of notice is required.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The dissolution is ineffective, because a reasonable period of notice is implied.

B. The dissolution is ineffective, because dissolution requires unanimity under s.24(8).

C. The dissolution is effective, because any partner may dissolve a partnership at will by notice, with immediate effect.

D. The dissolution is effective only if confirmed by an order of the court under s.35.

E. The dissolution is effective only after three months, being the period specified in the Partnership Act 1890.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. All three elements of s.1(1) are present: a business (trade), carried on in common (both work on every piece, both share the proceeds), with a view of profit. Partnership arises by conduct; no agreement or self-description is required. Ordering restoration materials from a regular supplier is an act for carrying on in the usual way the business of a furniture restoration business, so s.5 binds the firm, and under s.9 the partners are liable for the firm’s debts. Partnership liability is unlimited, not proportionate.

  • A treats intention as necessary. It is not.
  • B is the most attractive wrong answer: profit-sharing ratios govern the position between partners (and the right of indemnity), not liability to an outsider.
  • D misapplies s.2(2). The sisters share net proceeds after deducting costs — that is profit, not gross returns — and in any event they satisfy s.1(1) on the facts.
  • E describes holding out under s.14, which is the route to liability for someone who is not a partner. Here she is one.

Q2 — C. Section 24(6): no partner is entitled to a salary in the absence of agreement, however unequal the workload. Section 24(1): partners share profits equally regardless of unequal capital contributions. £180,000 ÷ 3 = £60,000 each.

  • A imports a fairness principle the Act does not contain.
  • B and D apportion by contribution or effort, which is the instinctive but wrong approach.
  • E confuses s.24(3), which gives 5% interest on an advance beyond agreed capital, with a reward for labour. Labour is not an advance.

Q3 — B. Section 36(1) protects those who dealt with the firm before the change, and they are entitled to treat the apparent members of the old firm as still being members until they have notice — which is why the letters to existing clients matter. The Gazette notice under s.36(2) operates as notice to persons who had no prior dealings. But s.36(3) settles this scenario directly: a partner “not having been known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner” is not liable for partnership debts contracted after their retirement. A client who never dealt with the firm before did not know her to be a partner, so she is not liable, Gazette notice or not. Note the contrast the examiner is testing: had this been an existing client who received no actual notice, she would be exposed.

  • A treats the Gazette notice as creating liability rather than protecting against it.
  • C invents a limitation-style rule that does not exist.
  • D misapplies s.17(1), which concerns incoming partners and debts incurred before they joined.
  • E applies the wrong liability rule: contract debts are joint under s.9; joint and several liability under ss.10–12 attaches to wrongs.

Q4 — D. Section 2(3)(d): a contract for a loan at a rate of interest varying with profits does not of itself make the lender a partner, provided the contract is in writing and signed by or on behalf of all the parties. Both conditions are satisfied. Section 3 then postpones the lender’s claim: on the borrower’s insolvency the lender is not entitled to recover until the claims of the other creditors have been satisfied.

  • A misstates s.2(3): profit-sharing is prima facie evidence, not conclusive.
  • B ignores the s.2(3)(d) exception entirely.
  • C invents a representation; publicity is not holding out.
  • E is the near-miss: the client is a creditor, but s.3 subordinates the claim. Option D is the better statement of the position and is therefore the single best answer.

Q5 — C. Sections 26 and 32(c): where no fixed term has been agreed, any partner may dissolve the partnership at any time by giving notice to the others, and dissolution takes effect from the date mentioned in the notice — or, if none is mentioned, from the date of communication. No reason and no notice period is required.

  • A is what commercial instinct suggests and is exactly the trap.
  • B misapplies s.24(8), which governs decisions about the conduct of the business, not the decision to end it.
  • D confuses dissolution by notice with dissolution by court order under s.35, which is needed only in the cases there listed (permanent incapacity, prejudicial conduct, wilful breach, carrying on at a loss, just and equitable).
  • E invents a statutory period.

2.5 The limited liability partnership

The LLP was created by the Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000 for professional firms that wanted limited liability without becoming companies. It is genuinely a hybrid, and the exam tests the hybridity.

2.5.1 Its corporate side

  • It is a body corporate with separate legal personality and unlimited capacity (s.1(2)–(3) LLPA 2000). It can own property, sue and be sued, and grant a floating charge.
  • It is formed by incorporation: two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view of profit subscribe to an incorporation document (form LL IN01) delivered to the Registrar with a statement of compliance. The Registrar issues a certificate of incorporation, which is conclusive evidence that the requirements have been met.
  • It must have at least two designated members, who carry the administrative duties a company secretary would otherwise bear: signing and filing the accounts, filing the confirmation statement, appointing auditors, and acting on winding up. If the incorporation document names none or only one, every member is a designated member.
  • It files accounts and a confirmation statement, so it trades publicity for limited liability in the same bargain a company makes. It must register charges, and its members are subject to the Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986.
  • Large parts of the Companies Act 2006 are applied to LLPs by regulations, with modifications.

2.5.2 Its partnership side

  • Its owners are members, not partners, and not shareholders. There is no share capital.
  • Its internal affairs are governed by a members’ agreement; where there is none, the default regulations supply terms closely mirroring the PA 1890 defaults: equal sharing of capital and profits, indemnity for proper payments, every member may take part in management, no member is entitled to remuneration, no new member or assignment of a member’s interest without unanimity, ordinary matters by majority but a change in the nature of the business by unanimity, books kept at the registered office, and duties to render true accounts and to account for private profits and for competing business.
  • It is tax transparent: the LLP itself pays no corporation tax, and members are taxed individually on their shares of profit as though the business were carried on in partnership.
Exam trap

Two defaults are conspicuously absent from the LLP regulations, and questions exploit both. There is no power to expel a member unless the members’ agreement confers one — same as s.25. But there is no equivalent of s.26: a member cannot dissolve the LLP by notice. A member may resign by reasonable notice (s.4(3) LLPA 2000), which removes them but leaves the LLP in existence. An LLP does not dissolve because a member walks out.

2.5.3 Where the limited liability stops

Limited liability is not absolute, and the exceptions are examinable:

  • Section 214A Insolvency Act 1986 (inserted for LLPs) allows a liquidator to claw back withdrawals — drawings, salary, repayment of a loan, share of profits — made by a member in the two years before winding up, where at the time of the withdrawal the member knew or had reasonable ground to believe the LLP was, or would become as a result, unable to pay its debts.
  • Wrongful and fraudulent trading (ss.214 and 213 IA 1986) apply to LLP members as they do to directors.
  • Personal negligence: an LLP member who personally assumes a duty of care to a client may be personally liable in tort. Limited liability protects against the LLP’s debts, not against one’s own torts.
  • Personal guarantees: banks routinely require them from the members of a small LLP, which removes the protection by contract — a point that applies equally to small companies.

2.5.4 Check yourself: LLPs

Q1. An LLP carrying on an architectural practice makes a profit of £600,000 in its financial year. The four members agree to leave £200,000 in the business and draw £100,000 each.

How is the £600,000 taxed?

A. The LLP pays corporation tax on £600,000; the members pay income tax on the £100,000 each that they draw.

B. The LLP pays corporation tax on the £200,000 retained; the members pay income tax on the amounts drawn.

C. The LLP pays no tax on the profit; each member is charged to income tax on their share of the £600,000, whether or not it is drawn.

D. The LLP pays no tax on the profit; each member is charged to income tax only on the £100,000 actually drawn.

E. The LLP pays corporation tax on the whole £600,000 and the members have no further liability.

Q2. A member of an LLP is in dispute with the other three members. He wishes to bring the LLP to an end. The members’ agreement is silent on resignation and dissolution.

Which of the following is correct?

A. He may dissolve the LLP by giving notice to the other members, as a partner may under s.26 of the Partnership Act 1890.

B. He may resign as a member by giving reasonable notice, but this does not dissolve the LLP.

C. He may neither resign nor dissolve the LLP without the unanimous agreement of the members.

D. He may require the other members to purchase his interest at a price fixed by the court.

E. He may petition for the LLP to be struck off the register on giving notice to the Registrar.

Q3. An LLP is placed into liquidation in November. In the previous eighteen months one of its members withdrew £180,000 by way of drawings and profit share. At the time of each withdrawal she knew that the LLP would be unable to pay its debts as a result.

Which of the following best describes the liquidator’s position?

A. The liquidator has no remedy, because members of an LLP enjoy limited liability.

B. The liquidator may apply for a declaration that the member contribute to the assets, because the withdrawals were made within two years of the commencement of winding up and she knew the LLP would become unable to pay its debts.

C. The liquidator may recover the withdrawals only if they were made within six months of the commencement of winding up.

D. The liquidator may recover the withdrawals as a preference, because the member is a connected person.

E. The liquidator may recover the withdrawals only if the LLP’s members’ agreement prohibited them.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. An LLP is tax transparent. It pays no corporation tax on its trading profit. Each member is charged to income tax (and Class 4 National Insurance) on their share of the profits as allocated, whether or not those profits are drawn. Retaining profit in the business does not defer the members’ tax.

  • A, B and E treat the LLP as opaque, which is the single most common LLP error.
  • D is the sophisticated trap: it gets transparency right but the timing wrong. Members are taxed on their allocated share, not on drawings. This is a real difference from a company, where a shareholder is taxed only when a dividend is paid.

Q2 — B. Section 4(3) LLPA 2000: a member may cease to be a member by giving reasonable notice to the other members, in the absence of agreement. Resignation removes the member; it does not end the LLP, which is a body corporate and continues in existence.

  • A is the central trap: there is no s.26 equivalent for LLPs. The partnership rule that any partner may dissolve a partnership at will does not carry across.
  • C is wrong as to resignation, which needs no one’s consent.
  • D describes a buy-out order of the kind available on an unfair prejudice petition; s.994 CA 2006 is applied to LLPs, but only if the members have not excluded it, and it is not the automatic consequence of a dispute.
  • E confuses striking off, which the LLP itself may apply for, with a member’s unilateral right.

Q3 — B. Section 214A IA 1986 applies to LLPs. The liquidator may seek a declaration that a member contribute to the assets where, within the two years before the commencement of winding up, the member withdrew property from the LLP and at that time knew or had reasonable ground to believe that the LLP was unable to pay its debts, or would become so as a result. Eighteen months falls within the period, and the state of knowledge is admitted.

  • A overstates limited liability, which does not survive s.214A.
  • C applies the preference period in s.239.
  • D mischaracterises the claim: a preference requires a creditor, surety or guarantor to be put in a better position, influenced by a desire to prefer. A member drawing profits is not being preferred as a creditor.
  • E invents a condition; s.214A does not depend on the terms of the members’ agreement.

The prohibition in s.755 is on a private company offering its shares to the public. It is not a prohibition on having many shareholders, nor on a private company being large. And the sanction is not that the allotment is void: the court may order re-registration as a public company, or grant remedial relief. An option stating that shares allotted in breach of s.755 are void is wrong.

2.6.2 Separate legal personality

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 is the foundation of the whole subject. A properly incorporated company is a legal person distinct from its shareholders, even where one person owns and controls it entirely. Mr Salomon’s company owed money to Mr Salomon, and he ranked as a secured creditor ahead of the unsecured trade creditors. The House of Lords held that the company had been validly formed and that the consequences followed: “the company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers”.

Three consequences are examined more often than the principle itself:

  • Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd [1925] AC 619 — a shareholder has no insurable interest in the company’s assets. Macaura sold his timber to a company he owned but kept the insurance in his own name; when the timber burned, his claim failed. The company owned the timber; he owned shares.
  • Lee v Lee’s Air Farming Ltd [1961] AC 12 — a person can be simultaneously the controlling shareholder, the sole director, and an employee of their own company. Lee’s widow recovered as the dependant of a worker killed in the course of employment.
  • Adams v Cape Industries plc [1990] Ch 433 — a parent company is not liable for the debts of its subsidiary merely because they form a single economic unit. There is no general “group enterprise” doctrine in English law.

The corollary matters as much: the company’s assets are not the shareholders’ assets, the company’s contracts are not the shareholders’ contracts, and a shareholder cannot sue for a loss that is merely a reflection of the company’s own loss (the reflective loss principle — see Chapter 4).

2.6.3 Piercing the corporate veil

The courts will disregard separate personality only in very narrow circumstances. Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34 restated the law and drew a distinction that is the whole of the modern doctrine:

  • The concealment principle: the court looks behind the company to identify who is really acting, or who really owns something. This is not piercing at all — it is ordinary fact-finding, and it is always available. (In Prest itself the properties were recovered not by piercing but because the companies held them on resulting trust for the husband.)
  • The evasion principle: the court disregards the company where a person under an existing legal obligation, liability or restriction deliberately interposes a company to frustrate or evade it. This is the only true piercing, and it is exceptional.

The two cases that survive as illustrations of evasion:

  • Gilford Motor Co Ltd v Horne [1933] Ch 935 — a former employee bound by a valid restrictive covenant set up a company to solicit his old employer’s customers. Injunction granted against both the man and the company.
  • Jones v Lipman [1962] 1 WLR 832 — a vendor who had contracted to sell land transferred it to a company he controlled to defeat specific performance. Order made against both.
Exam trap

“The company was a one-man company”, “the company was a sham”, “the shareholder controlled everything”, “it would be just and equitable”, “the group operated as a single economic unit” — none of these is sufficient. Look for a pre-existing obligation that the company was interposed to escape. Absent that, the veil stands, however unattractive the outcome. And note that in Prest the Supreme Court reached the right result without piercing: if there is another route — trust, agency, tort, statute — the court will take it, and an option offering that route is usually the better answer.

Statute achieves by other means much of what the common law refuses. Wrongful trading (s.214 Insolvency Act 1986) and fraudulent trading (s.213) impose personal liability on directors without disturbing the company’s separate personality; so does disqualification and the associated compensation order regime. Those are covered in Chapter 7. A parent may also owe a direct duty of care in negligence in respect of a subsidiary’s operations where it has taken over or advised on the relevant part of the subsidiary’s business — that is ordinary tort law, not veil-piercing.

2.6.4 Check yourself: personality and the veil

Q1. A client is the sole shareholder and sole director of a company that owns a fleet of delivery vans. He insures the vans in his own name, describing himself as the owner. Two vans are destroyed in a fire and the insurer refuses to pay.

Which of the following best explains the insurer’s position?

A. The claim fails because the client did not disclose that the company existed.

B. The claim fails because the client, as shareholder, has no insurable interest in assets owned by the company.

C. The claim succeeds because the client is the sole shareholder and in substance owns the vans.

D. The claim succeeds because the court will pierce the corporate veil where a company is wholly owned by one person.

E. The claim fails because a company cannot own vehicles used in a business carried on by its director.

Q2. A former sales director is bound by a valid two-year covenant not to solicit her previous employer’s customers. Six months after leaving, she incorporates a company of which she is the sole shareholder and director, and the company begins approaching those customers.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The covenant cannot be enforced against the company, because the company is a separate legal person and is not a party to it.

B. The court may grant an injunction against both the former director and the company, applying the evasion principle, because the company was interposed to frustrate an existing legal obligation.

C. The court will pierce the veil because the company is a one-person company.

D. The covenant is unenforceable, because restrictive covenants in employment contracts are void as restraints of trade.

E. The court may pierce the veil only if the company was incorporated before the covenant was entered into.

Q3. A parent company and its wholly owned subsidiary trade under a common brand, present consolidated accounts, and are described in marketing material as “one group, one team”. The subsidiary becomes insolvent owing substantial sums to trade creditors. The creditors seek to recover from the parent.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The parent is liable, because the group operated as a single economic unit.

B. The parent is liable, because it wholly owns the subsidiary and controls its board.

C. The parent is not liable merely because of the group relationship; some independent basis of liability, such as a guarantee, an assumption of a direct duty of care, or a statutory claim, is required.

D. The parent is liable, because presenting consolidated accounts amounts to holding out.

E. The parent is liable, because the veil will always be pierced in favour of trade creditors.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. This is Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd on its facts. The company owns the vans; the shareholder owns shares. A shareholder has no legal or equitable interest in the company’s assets and therefore no insurable interest in them.

  • A raises non-disclosure, which is not the reason the claim fails.
  • C is the intuitive answer and the classic error: economic reality does not displace legal ownership.
  • D states a rule that does not exist; sole ownership is never a ground for piercing.
  • E is simply wrong — a company can own whatever it likes.

Q2 — B. Gilford Motor Co Ltd v Horne, and squarely within the evasion principle as restated in Prest v Petrodel: a person subject to an existing legal obligation interposed a company for the purpose of frustrating it. Relief is available against both.

  • A states the Salomon principle correctly but stops one step short; the evasion principle is the exception that applies.
  • C gives the right outcome for the wrong reason. One-person status is irrelevant; deliberate evasion of a pre-existing obligation is the trigger.
  • D is wrong: reasonable covenants protecting a legitimate business interest are enforceable.
  • E inverts the rule. It is precisely because the obligation pre-dated the company that the evasion principle bites.

Q3 — C. Adams v Cape Industries plc. There is no single economic unit doctrine in English law. Liability requires an independent basis — a contractual guarantee, a direct duty of care assumed by the parent, a statutory claim such as wrongful trading against directors, or the evasion principle.

  • A, B, D and E each state a version of the group liability argument that Adams rejected.

2.7 Forming a company

2.7.1 What is delivered to the Registrar

A company is formed under s.7 CA 2006 by one or more persons subscribing to a memorandum of association and complying with the registration requirements. What goes to Companies House:

  1. Memorandum of association (s.8) — a short prescribed-form document in which the subscribers state that they wish to form a company and agree to become members, taking at least one share each. It is a historical record of formation. It is not the company’s constitution and it cannot be amended.
  2. Application for registration (s.9) — form IN01, stating the proposed name; whether the liability of members is to be limited, and whether by shares or by guarantee; whether the company is to be private or public; the intended address of the registered office and whether it is to be situated in England and Wales, Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland; and a registered email address. It is accompanied by:
    • a statement of capital and initial shareholdings (s.10) — the number of shares, aggregate nominal value, amounts paid up and unpaid, and the rights attached to each class;
    • a statement of proposed officers (s.12), with each proposed director’s and secretary’s consent to act;
    • a statement of initial significant control (s.12A), identifying the persons with significant control;
    • a statement of lawful purposes confirming that the company’s intended future activities are lawful.
  3. Articles of association, unless the relevant Model Articles are to apply in full by default.
  4. Statement of compliance (s.13).
  5. The registration fee.

The Registrar issues a certificate of incorporation (s.15), which is conclusive evidence that the registration requirements have been complied with and that the company is duly registered (s.15(4)). The company comes into existence on the date of incorporation shown on the certificate (s.16), and the subscribers become the first members.

Exam trap

The memorandum under the 2006 Act is not the constitutional document it was under the 1985 Act. The company’s constitution is its articles (s.17). An option describing the memorandum as containing an objects clause, as setting out the authorised share capital, or as amendable by special resolution, is testing whether you know the regime changed. Under s.31, a company’s objects are unrestricted unless the articles specifically restrict them.

Verify before relying on this

The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 has been commencing in stages. Two tranches are already in force and are examinable for sittings after their commencement:

  • From 4 March 2024: a registered office must be an appropriate address (a PO box alone will not do); a registered email address is required; a statement of lawful purposes is required on incorporation and on each confirmation statement; and the Registrar has enhanced powers to query, reject and remove information.
  • From 18 November 2025: identity verification is mandatory. New directors must verify before appointment can be registered, new PSCs within 14 days, with a 12-month transition for those already in post — existing directors provide their personal code with the company’s next confirmation statement. Companies House rejects filings relating to unverified individuals. On the same date the requirement for a company to keep its own registers of directors, directors’ residential addresses, secretaries and PSCs was removed; those particulars are now held centrally at Companies House. The register of members must still be kept, and the option for a private company to keep it at Companies House has gone.

Other provisions — notably the prohibition on corporate directors — were still awaiting commencement at the time of writing. Check what is in force at your sitting’s cut-off before treating any of it as the answer.

2.7.2 The articles

The articles are the company’s constitution. If no articles are registered, or the registered articles do not exclude or modify them, the relevant Model Articles apply by default (s.20 CA 2006). There are separate Model Articles for private companies limited by shares, private companies limited by guarantee, and public companies. A company incorporated under an earlier Act keeps the articles it had; a company with Table A articles from the 1985 regime does not automatically get Model Articles.

Section 33 gives the articles contractual force: the provisions of a company’s constitution bind the company and its members to the same extent as if there were covenants on the part of the company and of each member to observe them. Four propositions define its reach:

  • The contract binds member to company — Hickman v Kent or Romney Marsh Sheep-Breeders’ Association [1915] 1 Ch 881 (a member was held to an arbitration clause in the articles).
  • It binds company to member, in respect of membership rights — Pender v Lushington (1877) 6 Ch D 70 (a member’s right to have his vote counted was enforceable).
  • It binds member to member directly, at least in a small company — Rayfield v Hands [1960] Ch 1 (directors who were also members were required to buy a retiring member’s shares).
  • It is enforceable only in respect of rights held qua member — Eley v Positive Government Security Life Assurance Co (1876) 1 Ex D 88 (an article appointing Eley the company’s solicitor for life gave him no contractual right, because he sued in the capacity of solicitor, not member).

Amendment requires a special resolution (s.21) — 75% of those voting. Four limits:

  • A member is not bound by an alteration made after they became a member which requires them to take more shares or otherwise increases their liability to contribute or to pay money to the company, unless they agree in writing (s.25).
  • Provisions may be entrenched (s.22), so that they can be amended only if conditions more restrictive than a special resolution are met. Entrenchment can be introduced only on formation or by unanimous agreement of all the members, and the Registrar must be notified.
  • The power must be exercised bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole — Allen v Gold Reefs of West Africa Ltd [1900] 1 Ch 656. The modern test is whether the alteration is one that no reasonable person could consider to be for the benefit of the company; the fact that the majority also benefits does not invalidate it (Citco Banking Corporation NV v Pusser’s Ltd [2007] UKPC 13).
  • Class rights in the articles may additionally require the separate consent of the class (see Chapter 5).

A copy of the amended articles, and the special resolution itself, must be filed at Companies House within 15 days (ss.26 and 30).

Exam trap

Entrenchment does not mean unalterable. Section 22(3) preserves the members’ ability to alter an entrenched provision by unanimous agreement or by court order. An option saying an entrenched article can never be changed is wrong. The trap in the other direction is the 78% special resolution that purports to create entrenchment: a special resolution amends, but it cannot entrench.

2.7.3 Shareholders’ agreements: what the articles cannot do

The articles are public, alterable by 75%, and enforceable only as to membership rights. A shareholders’ agreement is private, requires unanimity to change (because it is an ordinary contract), and can bind the parties personally in ways the articles cannot. It is therefore the standard vehicle for:

  • Veto rights over specified decisions, so that a minority holder can block them.
  • A contractual promise about how each party will vote, including on a resolution to alter the articles. Such a promise binds the shareholders personally; it does not fetter the company’s statutory power to amend, which cannot be contracted away (Russell v Northern Bank Development Corporation Ltd [1992] 1 WLR 588), but the shareholders can be restrained from voting in breach.
  • Transfer controls, pre-emption on transfer, drag-along and tag-along.
  • Dividend policy, board composition and deadlock resolution.
Exam trap

Russell draws the line the examiner tests: an agreement by the company not to exercise its statutory powers is void; the same agreement between the shareholders is valid and enforceable against them. Read the option carefully to see who has promised what.

2.7.4 The company name

Sections 53–57 CA 2006 and the associated regulations restrict names:

  • A private limited company’s name must end with “limited” or “ltd” (or the Welsh equivalents cyfyngedig / cyf); a public company’s with “public limited company” or “plc” (cwmni cyfyngedig cyhoeddus / ccc).
  • A name that is the same as one already on the index is prohibited (s.66), and rules prescribe what differences are to be disregarded in making that comparison.
  • Names suggesting a connection with government or a public authority, and sensitive words, require approval (ss.54–55).
  • Offensive names and names whose use would constitute an offence are prohibited (s.53).

A company may change its name by special resolution, or by any other means provided for in its articles (s.77). The change takes effect when the Registrar issues a new certificate of incorporation on the change of name (s.81). Two post-registration challenges are worth knowing: the Registrar may direct a change where a name is too like an existing one (s.67), and a company names adjudicator may order a change where a name was registered opportunistically to take advantage of an existing goodwill (ss.69–74). Separately, the common law tort of passing off is unaffected by registration.

Trading disclosures. A company must display its registered name at its registered office and at any place of business (with limited exceptions), and state its registered name, registered number, part of the UK in which it is registered, and registered office address on its business letters, order forms and website.

2.7.5 Pre-incorporation contracts

A company does not exist before its certificate of incorporation, so it cannot contract, and it cannot ratify a contract made before it existed. This produces the most reliably examined trap in the formation topic.

Section 51(1) CA 2006: a contract that purports to be made by or on behalf of a company at a time when the company has not been formed has effect, subject to any agreement to the contrary, as one made with the person purporting to act for the company or as agent for it, and he is personally liable on it accordingly.

  • Kelner v Baxter (1866) LR 2 CP 174 — the common law origin: promoters who contracted for a company not yet formed were personally liable, and the company could not ratify.
  • Phonogram Ltd v Lane [1982] QB 938 — s.51’s predecessor applies even where both parties knew the company did not exist, and “subject to any agreement to the contrary” requires a clear exclusion of personal liability; merely signing “for and on behalf of” the unformed company is not enough.
  • Braymist Ltd v Wise Finance Co Ltd [2002] EWCA Civ 127 — the personal liability cuts both ways: the person who purported to act may also enforce the contract.

The practical solutions, in the order a solicitor would consider them:

  1. Wait until the company is incorporated before contracting.
  2. Use a novation: after incorporation, the company, the third party and the promoter agree that the company takes the promoter’s place. This is a new contract, and requires the third party’s consent.
  3. Draft a clear exclusion: an express term that the promoter is not to be personally liable and that the third party’s only recourse is against the company once formed — with the commercial consequence that the third party may have no one to sue if the company is never incorporated.
Exam trap

“The company ratified the contract at its first board meeting” is a wrong answer, every time. Ratification requires the principal to have been in existence and capable of being bound at the date of the act. A company cannot ratify a pre-incorporation contract; only novation works.

2.7.6 Corporate capacity and the authority of the board

Two provisions protect third parties dealing with a company, and questions blur them:

  • Section 39: the validity of an act done by a company cannot be called into question on the ground of lack of capacity by reason of anything in the company’s constitution. This is about the company’s capacity, and it protects the transaction absolutely.
  • Section 40: in favour of a person dealing with a company in good faith, the power of the directors to bind the company, or to authorise others to do so, is deemed free of any limitation under the company’s constitution. A person is not bound to enquire as to any limitation, is presumed to have acted in good faith unless the contrary is proved, and is not to be regarded as acting in bad faith merely because they knew the act was beyond the directors’ powers.
  • Section 41 qualifies s.40 where the other party to the transaction is a director of the company or its holding company, or a person connected with such a director: the transaction is voidable at the company’s instance, and the director must account for any gain and indemnify the company for any loss.
Exam trap

Knowledge alone does not defeat s.40. The statute says so expressly. The distractor is an option reading “the third party cannot rely on s.40 because it knew of the restriction in the articles” — that is precisely what s.40(2)(b)(iii) rules out. What does defeat protection is being a director or connected person, which takes you to s.41.

2.7.7 Off-the-shelf or tailor-made

A shelf company is bought ready-formed and then adapted: the subscriber shares are transferred, new directors are appointed and the old ones resign, the name is changed by special resolution, the articles are amended or replaced, the accounting reference date is changed, and the registered office is moved. Formation from scratch avoids that unwinding but takes slightly longer. Electronic incorporation has narrowed the gap so far that the shelf company is now largely of historical interest — but it appears in questions, and the point being tested is usually the sequence of steps required to adapt one, and the fact that each requires a filing.

2.7.8 First steps after incorporation

The specification asks about “other steps required … to enable the entity to commence operating”. For a newly incorporated private company, a competent solicitor’s checklist runs:

  • Hold a first board meeting: report the incorporation, appoint a chair, appoint any additional directors, approve the transfer of subscriber shares if a shelf company, open the bank account, approve the accounting reference date, appoint auditors if needed, and authorise the necessary filings.
  • Ensure identity verification is complete for every director and PSC.
  • Enter the members in the register of members and issue share certificates within two months of allotment or of the lodging of a transfer (s.769, s.776).
  • Register for corporation tax with HMRC within three months of starting business activity; for PAYE before the first payday; and for VAT if the registration threshold is met or is expected to be.
  • Put in place a shareholders’ agreement, service contracts, and any necessary insurance.
  • Register any charge created over the company’s assets within 21 days (s.859A).

For a public company there is one more, and it is examined: the company cannot do business or exercise any borrowing powers until the Registrar has issued a trading certificate under s.761, which requires the allotted share capital to be not less than the authorised minimum of £50,000. A transaction entered into before the certificate is nevertheless valid, but the company and its officers commit an offence, and if the company fails to comply with its obligations under the transaction within 21 days of being called on to do so, the directors are jointly and severally liable to indemnify the other party (s.767).

2.7.9 Check yourself: formation and constitution

Q1. A solicitor is instructed to incorporate a private company limited by shares. The client asks which document will govern how decisions are taken within the company, and whether it can be changed later.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The memorandum of association, which may be amended by special resolution.

B. The articles of association, which may be amended by special resolution, subject to any entrenched provisions.

C. The memorandum of association, which is unalterable, together with the certificate of incorporation.

D. The articles of association, which may be amended only by unanimous agreement of the members.

E. The statement of capital and initial shareholdings, which may be amended by ordinary resolution.

Q2. A promoter, acting for a company that has not yet been incorporated, signs a two-year lease of business premises. He signs “for and on behalf of Wentworth Trading Ltd (a company in the course of formation)”. Both parties know the company does not yet exist. The company is incorporated two weeks later and its board passes a resolution “ratifying and adopting” the lease. The company then fails to pay the rent.

Against whom may the landlord enforce the lease?

A. The company only, because the board ratified the lease.

B. The promoter only, because a company cannot ratify a contract made before it existed and the words used did not clearly exclude his personal liability.

C. Neither, because the lease was a nullity.

D. The company and the promoter jointly, because ratification adds the company without releasing the promoter.

E. The promoter only, but only because the landlord knew the company did not exist.

Q3. A company’s articles provide that a particular provision may be amended only by a resolution passed by holders of 90% of the company’s shares. The provision was inserted eight months after incorporation, by a special resolution passed on a 78% vote. No notice of entrenchment was given to the Registrar.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The 90% requirement is effective, because it was inserted by a validly passed special resolution.

B. The 90% requirement is not effective as an entrenched provision, because entrenchment may be introduced only on formation or by unanimous agreement of the members.

C. The 90% requirement is effective, but only if the company files a copy of the amended articles within 15 days.

D. The 90% requirement is void in its entirety, and the articles revert to the Model Articles.

E. The 90% requirement is effective, because a special resolution requires 75% and 78% exceeds that threshold.

Q4. A supplier contracts with a company for the sale of machinery worth £400,000. The company’s articles provide that the directors may not enter into any contract worth more than £250,000 without the prior approval of the members in general meeting. No such approval was given. The supplier had read the articles and knew of the restriction, but believed the directors would obtain approval.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The contract is void, because the directors acted beyond the powers conferred by the articles.

B. The contract is voidable at the company’s option, because the supplier had actual knowledge of the restriction.

C. The contract binds the company, because in favour of a person dealing with the company in good faith the directors’ power to bind it is deemed free of limitations under the constitution, and knowledge of the limitation does not by itself amount to bad faith.

D. The contract binds the company only if the supplier can prove it made reasonable enquiries as to the directors’ authority.

E. The contract is unenforceable, because the company lacked capacity to enter into it.

Q5. A newly incorporated public company, having been issued with its certificate of incorporation, immediately enters into a substantial supply contract. It has not yet obtained a trading certificate.

Which of the following best describes the position?

A. The contract is void, because a public company has no capacity to trade before a trading certificate is issued.

B. The contract is valid, and there are no consequences, because the certificate of incorporation is conclusive evidence of due registration.

C. The contract is valid, but the company and its officers commit an offence, and if the company fails to comply with its obligations within 21 days of being called on to do so, the directors are jointly and severally liable to indemnify the other party.

D. The contract is valid, but the other party’s only remedy is against the Registrar.

E. The contract is voidable at the option of the other party, who may rescind on discovering the absence of a trading certificate.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. The constitution is the articles (s.17). They are amended by special resolution (s.21), subject to s.22 entrenchment, s.25 (no increased liability without written consent), and the Allen v Gold Reefs requirement of good faith.

  • A and C treat the memorandum as constitutional. Under the 2006 Act it is a short prescribed-form historical record that cannot be amended at all.
  • D applies the entrenchment or unanimity standard to ordinary amendment.
  • E confuses a registration document with the constitution.

Q2 — B. Section 51(1): the contract takes effect as one made with the person purporting to act for the unformed company, who is personally liable, subject to any agreement to the contrary. Signing “for and on behalf of” is not a clear exclusion of personal liability (Phonogram Ltd v Lane), and a company cannot ratify a contract made before it existed (Kelner v Baxter). The board resolution is of no legal effect against the landlord.

  • A is the classic error: ratification is impossible.
  • C is wrong; the contract is perfectly valid, just with a different counterparty than intended.
  • D would be right only after a novation, which requires the landlord’s agreement — and a novation substitutes the company rather than adding it.
  • E reaches the right outcome by the wrong route: Phonogram establishes that the parties’ knowledge is irrelevant, so the reason given is wrong.

Q3 — B. Section 22(2): a provision for entrenchment may be made only in the company’s articles on formation, or by an amendment agreed to by all the members. A 78% special resolution can amend the articles, but it cannot entrench. The 90% requirement therefore has no protected status and can itself be removed by ordinary special resolution.

  • A and E confuse the majority needed to amend with the procedure needed to entrench.
  • C treats a filing requirement as a validity requirement. Filing within 15 days is required (s.26), but non-compliance is an offence, not invalidity.
  • D over-corrects: the provision is not void, and articles do not revert to the Model Articles.

Q4 — C. Section 40(1): in favour of a person dealing with a company in good faith, the power of the directors to bind the company is deemed free of any limitation under the constitution. Section 40(2)(b) provides that such a person is not bound to enquire as to any limitation, is presumed to have acted in good faith unless the contrary is proved, and is not to be regarded as acting in bad faith by reason only of knowing that the act is beyond the directors’ powers.

  • A and E invoke lack of capacity, which s.39 removes.
  • B is the trap, and states the pre-2006 constructive notice position. Knowledge alone does not defeat s.40; the voidable consequence in s.41 applies only where the counterparty is a director or connected person.
  • D reverses the burden; there is no duty to enquire.

Q5 — C. Section 761 prohibits a public company from doing business or exercising borrowing powers before the Registrar issues a trading certificate. Section 767 provides the consequences: the transaction is valid, but the company and every officer in default commit an offence, and if the company fails to comply with its obligations within 21 days of being called on to do so, the directors at the time of the transaction are jointly and severally liable to indemnify the other party for any loss.

  • A is wrong: s.767(1) expressly preserves validity.
  • B ignores the offence and the directors’ indemnity.
  • D and E invent remedies that do not exist.

2.8 The solicitor’s position

Three conduct issues recur in formation scenarios, and each is worth more marks than its length suggests.

Who is the client? Once a company is incorporated it is a client distinct from its founders. A solicitor instructed to “set up the business for Anna and Ben” must be clear whether they act for Anna, for Ben, for both, or for the company — and the answer changes who they owe duties to when the founders fall out. Before incorporation there is no company to act for; after incorporation, the solicitor should make clear, in writing, whose interests they are advancing.

Conflict of interest. Acting for two or more founders whose interests may diverge — on shareholdings, on control, on what happens if one leaves — engages the conflict provisions of the Code of Conduct. The safe course, and usually the right answer, is to advise each to take independent advice on the terms of the shareholders’ agreement rather than to proceed on the basis that everyone is currently friendly.

Client identification and source of funds. Incorporating a company and opening its bank account engages the firm’s obligations on client due diligence. A client who is evasive about beneficial ownership, or who wants a corporate structure with no evident commercial purpose, is a warning sign and not merely an inconvenience.

Exam trap

The client’s consent does not cure everything. Where there is a conflict, or a significant risk of one, the exceptions permitting a solicitor to act are narrow: the clients must have a substantially common interest, or be competing for the same objective, and even then the solicitor needs informed written consent, effective safeguards, and a reasonable belief that it is in the clients’ best interests to act. An option that says “may act because both clients consented in writing” is incomplete, and is usually wrong.

2.9 Chapter review

Q1. A client wishes to start a consultancy business with two colleagues. All three want limited liability, want the flexibility of an internal agreement rather than a public constitution, and are content that each of them should be taxed personally on their share of the profits as the profits arise. They also want the business to be able to grant a floating charge over its assets to its bank.

Which vehicle best meets all of the client’s requirements?

A. A general partnership under the Partnership Act 1890.

B. A limited partnership under the Limited Partnerships Act 1907.

C. A limited liability partnership.

D. A private company limited by shares.

E. Three separate sole trader businesses operating under a co-operation agreement.

Q2. Which of the following statements about a private company limited by shares is incorrect?

A. It may pass a written resolution instead of holding a general meeting.

B. It must have at least one director.

C. It must have a company secretary.

D. It is not required to hold an annual general meeting.

E. It must not offer its shares to the public.

Q3. A partnership agreement provides that no partner may enter into a contract on behalf of the firm worth more than £10,000 without the written consent of all the partners. One partner, without consent, contracts with a long-standing supplier for £25,000 of stock of the kind the firm regularly buys. The supplier has traded with the firm for years and has never been told of any limit.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The firm is not bound, because the partner exceeded the authority conferred by the partnership agreement.

B. The firm is bound, because the act was for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm and the supplier neither knew of the limit nor doubted that the partner was a partner.

C. The firm is bound only to the extent of £10,000, being the limit in the agreement.

D. The firm is not bound, but the contracting partner is personally liable to the supplier.

E. The firm is bound only if the other partners subsequently ratify the contract.

Q4. A solicitor is asked to advise on the difference between the memorandum of association and the articles of association of a company incorporated in 2024.

Which of the following best describes the memorandum?

A. It sets out the company’s objects, which restrict its capacity.

B. It states the authorised share capital of the company, which may be increased by ordinary resolution.

C. It is a short prescribed-form statement by the subscribers that they wish to form the company and agree to become members, taking at least one share each; it cannot be amended.

D. It is the company’s constitution and may be amended by special resolution.

E. It records the identity of the persons with significant control and must be updated whenever control changes.

Q5. Four people carry on a business in common with a view of profit. There is no written agreement. One of them, without consulting the others, agrees on behalf of the business to sell a major item of equipment for substantially less than it is worth, to a buyer who is her cousin. The buyer does not know of any limit on her authority, and the sale of surplus equipment is something the business does from time to time.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The sale does not bind the firm, because the price was substantially below value.

B. The sale does not bind the firm, because the buyer is connected with the selling partner.

C. The sale binds the firm, and the selling partner may be liable to her partners for breach of her duties under ss.28–30 of the Partnership Act 1890 and for any loss caused.

D. The sale binds the firm, and the other partners have no recourse against the selling partner because s.24(5) entitles every partner to take part in management.

E. The sale is voidable at the option of the other partners because it was a transaction with a connected person.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Only the LLP satisfies every requirement simultaneously: limited liability (unlike A, B for the general partner, and E), a private members’ agreement rather than public articles (unlike D), tax transparency so that members are taxed on profits as they arise (unlike D, where the company pays corporation tax and the shareholders are taxed only on distributions), and the ability to create a floating charge, which is available to bodies corporate (unlike A, B and E).

  • D is the strongest distractor: a company gives limited liability and can grant a floating charge, but it is opaque for tax and its constitution is public.
  • B fails because at least one partner must accept unlimited liability, and any limited partner who participates in management loses the protection.

Q2 — C. A private company may appoint a secretary but is not required to (s.270). It is a public company that must have one (s.271). Every other statement is correct: written resolutions are available to private companies only; the minimum is one director (s.154); there is no AGM requirement for a private company; and s.755 prohibits an offer to the public.

Q3 — B. Section 5 PA 1890. The act was for carrying on in the usual way the business of the kind carried on by the firm; the partner lacked actual authority, but the exception requires both that she had no authority and that the third party knew that or did not know or believe her to be a partner. Neither limb of the third-party condition is satisfied.

  • A and C treat an internal restriction as effective against an outsider. It is not; it gives the other partners a claim for indemnity against the contracting partner.
  • D misallocates liability: the firm is bound, so the supplier sues the firm.
  • E imports ratification, which is unnecessary where the firm is already bound.

Q4 — C. Section 8 CA 2006 and the prescribed form. The memorandum is a historical record of formation and cannot be amended.

  • A reflects the pre-2006 position; under s.31 objects are unrestricted unless the articles restrict them.
  • B describes authorised share capital, abolished by the 2006 Act.
  • D describes the articles.
  • E describes the PSC information, now held at Companies House.

Q5 — C. Selling surplus equipment is an act for carrying on in the usual way the business of the kind carried on by the firm, and the buyer neither knew of a want of authority nor doubted that she was a partner, so s.5 binds the firm. That does not absolve the partner internally: s.29 requires her to account for any private benefit derived from a transaction concerning the partnership, s.28 requires full disclosure to her partners, and she will be liable to indemnify the firm for loss caused by acting outside her authority.

  • A and B would defeat the third party on grounds s.5 does not recognise. The buyer’s knowledge of the relationship is not knowledge of a want of authority.
  • D confuses a right to participate in management with a licence to breach fiduciary duties.
  • E imports the s.190 CA 2006 substantial property transaction regime, which applies to companies and directors, not to partnerships.
