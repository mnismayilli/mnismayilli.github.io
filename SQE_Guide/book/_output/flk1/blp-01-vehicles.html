2  Choosing and forming the vehicle

Assessment objective 1: starting a new business through the vehicle of a company, partnership, LLP or as a sole trader.

2.1 Why the choice matters

A client starting a business is really choosing among four trade-offs, and almost every exam question on this topic is testing one of them.

Liability. Will the founders’ personal assets be exposed if the business fails? This is the first question most clients ask and the one they most often misunderstand.

Tax. Is the business itself taxed, or are the owners taxed on its profits directly? The difference between a transparent vehicle and an opaque one drives a great deal of real-world structuring advice.

Formality and cost. Some vehicles come into existence by accident. Others require registration, filings, accounts and a constitution.

Publicity and control. Incorporation buys limited liability at the price of putting your accounts, officers and ownership on a public register.

Exam trap

The LLP is the hybrid, and the hybrid is where questions live. It is a body corporate with separate legal personality and limited liability — but it is taxed transparently, like a partnership. Any option telling you an LLP pays corporation tax on its profits is wrong. Any option telling you an LLP has no separate legal personality is also wrong. Candidates who have half-learned the point reliably pick one or the other.

2.3 The sole trader

There is nothing to form. A person who starts trading on their own account is a sole trader, and the business has no legal existence separate from them. Its assets are their assets, its debts are their debts, and its profits are their income, charged to income tax with Class 2 and Class 4 National Insurance contributions.

The absence of separate personality is the whole point. A sole trader cannot contract with their own business, cannot be employed by it, and cannot limit their exposure to its creditors. Against that, there is no registration, no constitution, no public filing and no audit.

Trading names are regulated even here: the restrictions on business names in Part 41 of the Companies Act 2006 apply to sole traders and partnerships, not only to companies.

2.4 The general partnership

2.4.1 How it arises

Section 1(1) of the Partnership Act 1890 defines partnership as “the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit”.

Read that carefully. It says nothing about agreement, documentation, or intention to form a partnership. If the elements are present, the relation exists.

Exam trap

Partnership arises by conduct. A scenario in which two people never signed anything, never used the word “partnership”, and would deny being partners can still be a partnership — and each will then be liable for the other’s business debts without limit. The examiner’s favourite version is two friends who “just started working together”. Look for business, in common, with a view of profit. Sharing gross returns alone does not create a partnership (s.2(2)); receipt of a share of profits is prima facie evidence but not conclusive (s.2(3)).

In England and Wales a partnership has no separate legal personality. The firm is the partners. (Scots law differs — a distraction the examiner sometimes uses.)

2.4.2 The default terms

Where the partners have no agreement, or their agreement is silent, the Act supplies terms. These are heavily examined because the question usually turns on what happens absent an agreement.

s.24(8) Ordinary matters decided by majority; a change in the nature of the business requires unanimity

Exam trap

Section 24(1) is the one that produces the unfair-looking answer. A partner who contributed £90,000 of the capital and a partner who contributed £10,000 share profits equally unless they agreed otherwise. Candidates instinctively apportion by contribution. The Act does not.

2.4.3 Authority and liability to outsiders

Section 5 is the partnership equivalent of agency. A partner’s act binds the firm where it is for carrying on in the usual way business of the kind carried on by the firm — unless the partner had no authority and the third party either knew that or did not know or believe them to be a partner. Both limbs are needed to escape liability.

Worked example

Anna, Ben and Chi are partners in a firm of architects. Their agreement says no partner may commit the firm to more than £5,000 without the others’ consent. Anna orders £12,000 of drafting software from a supplier who knows nothing of the restriction.

Is the firm bound? Ordering professional software is an act for carrying on in the usual way the business of a firm of architects, so s.5 is engaged. Anna lacked actual authority — but the supplier neither knew that nor doubted she was a partner. The firm is bound. The internal restriction is enforceable between the partners, not against the supplier. Anna is liable to indemnify her partners.

Change one fact: the supplier had been told of the £5,000 limit. Now both limbs are satisfied and the firm is not bound.

Related provisions worth knowing precisely:

  • s.9 — partners are jointly liable for the firm’s debts and obligations.
  • ss.10–12 — partners are jointly and severally liable for wrongs committed in the ordinary course of business.
  • s.14 — a person who represents themselves, or knowingly allows themselves to be represented, as a partner is liable as one to anyone who gives credit on the faith of it (holding out).
  • s.17(1) — an incoming partner is not liable for debts incurred before they joined.
  • s.17(2) — an outgoing partner remains liable for debts incurred while they were a partner.
  • s.36 — and remains liable for later debts to those who dealt with the firm before, unless notice is given: actual notice to existing customers, and London Gazette notice for the world.
Exam trap

The retiring partner who “told everyone” is a standing favourite. Retirement alone does not end exposure to future creditors who previously dealt with the firm. Actual notice to them plus Gazette notice is what does it.

2.5 The limited liability partnership

The LLP was created by the Limited Liability Partnerships Act 2000 for professional firms that wanted limited liability without becoming companies. It is genuinely a hybrid.

  • It is a body corporate with separate legal personality and unlimited capacity (s.1 LLPA 2000).
  • Its owners are members, not partners. At least two are designated members, carrying the administrative duties a company secretary would otherwise bear.
  • It is formed by incorporation — two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view of profit, subscribing to an incorporation document delivered to the Registrar.
  • Its internal affairs are governed by a members’ agreement; where there is none, default regulations supply terms broadly mirroring the PA 1890 defaults.
  • It files accounts and a confirmation statement, so it trades publicity for limited liability in the same bargain a company makes.
  • It is tax transparent: the LLP itself pays no corporation tax, and members are taxed individually on their shares of profit.

The limited liability is not absolute. Section 214A of the Insolvency Act 1986 allows a liquidator to claw back withdrawals made by a member in the two years before winding up, where the member knew or had reasonable ground to believe the LLP was, or would become, unable to pay its debts.

2.6 Companies

2.6.1 Private and public

A private company limited by shares is the default vehicle for a small business. An unlisted public company is rarer and mostly examined by contrast.

2.6.2 Separate legal personality

Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22 is the foundation. A properly incorporated company is a legal person distinct from its shareholders, even where one person owns and controls it entirely. Mr Salomon’s company owed money to Mr Salomon, and he ranked as a secured creditor ahead of the unsecured ones. The House of Lords held the company was validly formed and the consequences followed.

Two consequences are examined more often than the principle itself:

  • Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd [1925] AC 619 — a shareholder has no insurable interest in the company’s assets. Macaura insured timber in his own name; the company owned it; his claim failed.
  • Lee v Lee’s Air Farming Ltd [1961] AC 12 — a person can be simultaneously the controlling shareholder, sole director, and an employee of their own company. Lee’s widow recovered as the dependant of a worker killed in the course of employment.

2.6.3 Piercing the corporate veil

The courts will disregard separate personality only in very narrow circumstances. Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34 restated the law and drew a distinction that is the whole of the modern doctrine:

  • The concealment principle: the court looks behind the company to identify who is really acting. This is not piercing at all — it is ordinary fact-finding, and it is always available.
  • The evasion principle: the court disregards the company where a person under an existing legal obligation deliberately interposes a company to frustrate or evade it. This is the only true piercing, and it is exceptional.
Exam trap

“The company was a one-man company”, “the company was a sham”, “the shareholder controlled everything”, “it would be just and equitable” — none of these is sufficient. Look for a pre-existing obligation that the company was interposed to escape. Absent that, the veil stands, however unattractive the outcome.

Statute achieves by other means much of what the common law refuses: wrongful trading (s.214 Insolvency Act 1986) and fraudulent trading (s.213) impose personal liability on directors without disturbing the company’s separate personality. Those are covered in Chapter 7.

2.6.4 Formation

A company is formed under s.7 CA 2006 by one or more persons subscribing to a memorandum of association and complying with the registration requirements. What is delivered to the Registrar:

  1. Memorandum of association (s.8) — now a short prescribed-form document in which the subscribers state that they wish to form a company and agree to become members, taking at least one share each. It is a historical record of formation; it is not the company’s constitution and cannot be amended.
  2. Application for registration (s.9) — form IN01, stating the proposed name, whether liability is limited, whether the company is private or public, the situation of the registered office (England and Wales, Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland), a statement of capital and initial shareholdings, a statement of proposed officers, and a statement of the intended registered office address.
  3. Articles of association, unless the relevant Model Articles are adopted in full by default.
  4. Statement of compliance (s.13).
  5. The registration fee.

The Registrar issues a certificate of incorporation (s.15), which is conclusive evidence that the registration requirements were met (s.15(4)). The company exists from the date on the certificate (s.16).

Exam trap

The memorandum under the 2006 Act is not the constitutional document it was under the 1985 Act. The company’s constitution is its articles. An option describing the memorandum as containing the objects clause, or as amendable by special resolution, is testing whether you know the regime changed.

Verify before relying on this

The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 introduced identity verification for directors, persons with significant control and those filing at Companies House, together with enhanced Registrar powers. These provisions have been commencing in stages. Check what is in force, and — see Section 2 — whether it falls before your sitting’s examinable law cut-off, before treating any of it as the answer.

2.6.5 The articles

The articles are the company’s constitution. If none are registered, or the registered articles do not exclude or modify them, the relevant Model Articles apply by default (s.20 CA 2006).

Section 33 gives the articles contractual force: the provisions of a company’s constitution bind the company and its members as if there were covenants by each to observe them. The contract is enforceable only in respect of membership rights — a member suing in some other capacity, such as as a solicitor engaged by the company, cannot rely on s.33.

Amendment requires a special resolution (s.21) — 75%. Three limits:

  • A member cannot be bound by an alteration made after they became a member which requires them to take more shares or increases their liability, unless they agree in writing (s.25).
  • Provisions may be entrenched (s.22), requiring a more restrictive procedure than a special resolution. Entrenchment can be introduced only on formation or by unanimous agreement.
  • The power must be exercised bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole (Allen v Gold Reefs of West Africa Ltd [1900] 1 Ch 656).

A copy of any amended articles must be filed within 15 days.

2.6.6 The company name

Sections 53–57 CA 2006 restrict names: a private limited company’s name must end with “limited” or “ltd” (or the Welsh equivalents cyfyngedig / cyf); a name that is the same as one already registered is prohibited; and certain sensitive or offensive words require approval. A company may change its name by special resolution (s.77), and the change takes effect when the Registrar issues a new certificate of incorporation on the change of name.

2.6.7 Off-the-shelf or tailor-made

A shelf company is bought ready-formed and then adapted: the subscriber shares are transferred, new directors appointed, the old ones resign, the name is changed and the articles amended. Formation from scratch avoids that unwinding but takes slightly longer. Electronic incorporation has narrowed the gap so far that the shelf company is now mostly of historical interest — but it appears in questions, and the point being tested is usually the sequence of steps needed to adapt one.

2.7 The solicitor’s position

Two conduct issues recur in formation scenarios, and both are worth more marks than their length suggests.

Who is the client? Once a company is incorporated it is a client distinct from its founders. A solicitor instructed to “set up the business for Anna and Ben” must be clear whether they act for Anna, for Ben, for both, or for the company — and the answer changes who they owe duties to when the founders fall out.

Conflict of interest. Acting for two or more founders whose interests may diverge — on shareholdings, on control, on what happens if one leaves — engages the conflict provisions of the Code of Conduct. The safe course, and usually the right answer, is to advise each to take independent advice on the terms of the shareholders’ agreement, rather than to proceed on the basis that everyone is currently friendly.

Exam trap

The client’s consent does not cure everything. Where there is a conflict, or a significant risk of one, the exceptions permitting a solicitor to act are narrow and depend on the clients having a substantially common interest or competing for the same objective, plus informed consent and appropriate safeguards. An option that says “may act because both clients consented in writing” is usually incomplete.

2.8 Test yourself

Five questions — answers hidden until you open them

1. Priya contributed £80,000 to a partnership; Sam contributed £20,000. There is no written agreement. The firm makes £50,000 profit. How is it shared?

2. A retiring partner writes to all the firm’s existing customers announcing her retirement but places no notice in the London Gazette. A new customer, who had never dealt with the firm before, later contracts with it and is unpaid. Is the retired partner liable?

3. A father, subject to a court order requiring him to transfer a specific property to his daughter, transfers it instead to a company he wholly owns. Will the court pierce the veil?

4. An LLP makes £400,000 of profit. Which tax is charged on that profit, and on whom?

5. A company’s articles contain a provision requiring 90% approval to remove a director. The provision was inserted after incorporation by special resolution passed on a 78% vote, with no entrenchment procedure followed. Is it effective as an entrenched provision?

Answers

1. Equally — £25,000 each. Section 24(1) provides for equal sharing of profits regardless of unequal capital contributions, and there is no agreement displacing it. The instinct to apportion 80:20 is the trap.

2. No. Section 36 protects those who dealt with the firm before the retirement, and requires actual notice to them; the Gazette notice protects against new customers. A customer who never dealt with the firm before the retirement cannot have relied on her membership, so the absence of Gazette notice does not make her liable here. (Note the contrast: had it been an existing customer who received no notice, she would be exposed.)

3. Yes, on the evasion principle in Prest v Petrodel. There is a pre-existing legal obligation — the court order — and the company was interposed to frustrate it. This is the narrow case in which the veil is genuinely pierced.

4. None on the LLP itself. An LLP is tax transparent: each member is charged to income tax on their share of the profit, whether or not it is drawn. Corporation tax is not in point.

5. No. Entrenchment under s.22 may be introduced only on formation or by unanimous agreement of all the members. A 78% special resolution is enough to amend the articles but not to entrench a provision, so the 90% requirement is not protected against later removal by ordinary special resolution.
