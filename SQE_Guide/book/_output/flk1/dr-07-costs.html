15  Who pays, and how much

Assessment objective 8: procedures and processes relevant to costs involved in dispute resolution.

15.1 The general rule and the court’s discretion

CPR 44.2(1): the court has a discretion as to whether costs are payable by one party to another, the amount, and when they are to be paid.

CPR 44.2(2): if the court decides to make an order, the general rule is that the unsuccessful party will be ordered to pay the costs of the successful party — “costs follow the event” — but the court may make a different order.

CPR 44.2(4): in deciding what order to make, the court will have regard to all the circumstances, including:

  1. the conduct of all the parties;
  2. whether a party has succeeded on part of its case, even if not wholly successful; and
  3. any admissible offer to settle which is drawn to the court’s attention and which is not a Part 36 offer.

CPR 44.2(5) — conduct includes:

  • conduct before as well as during the proceedings, and in particular the extent to which the parties followed the relevant pre-action protocol or the Practice Direction;
  • whether it was reasonable for a party to raise, pursue or contest a particular allegation or issue;
  • the manner in which a party has pursued or defended its case or a particular issue; and
  • whether a claimant who has succeeded in the claim, in whole or in part, exaggerated it.
Exam trap

“Costs follow the event” is a general rule, not a right. A successful party can be deprived of some or all of its costs, or even ordered to pay costs, because of its conduct — for unreasonably refusing ADR, for pursuing hopeless issues, for exaggerating the claim, or for failing to comply with a protocol. Options that treat the winner’s entitlement as automatic are wrong.

Note also the SLAPP amendment to CPR 44.2, made alongside the strike-out ground in CPR 3.4(2)(d) when ss.194–195 of the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 came into force on 18 June 2025, which constrains the recovery of costs by a claimant in a SLAPP claim.

15.2 The two bases of assessment

Costs are assessed on either the standard or the indemnity basis (CPR 44.3). The difference is worth roughly 15 to 20 percentage points of recovery, and it is examined constantly.

Standard basis Indemnity basis
Costs must be reasonably incurred and reasonable in amount Yes Yes
Costs must be proportionate Yes No
Doubt is resolved in favour of The paying party The receiving party
Typical recovery ~60–70% of costs actually incurred ~80–90%

CPR 44.3(2)(a) is the sting on the standard basis: costs which are disproportionate in amount may be disallowed or reduced even if they were reasonably or necessarily incurred.

Proportionality (CPR 44.3(5)): costs are proportionate if they bear a reasonable relationship to the sums in issue, the value of any non-monetary relief, the complexity of the litigation, any additional work generated by the conduct of the paying party, any wider factors such as reputation or public importance, and any additional work or expense arising from the vulnerability of a party or witness.

Indemnity costs are the exception, awarded where the conduct of the paying party takes the case out of the norm — pursuing a hopeless claim, serious misconduct, deliberate breaches of orders, or as an automatic consequence of a claimant beating its own Part 36 offer.

15.3 Interim costs orders

Every interim application produces a costs order, and the labels have fixed meanings that questions exploit.

Order Meaning
Costs in the case (or “costs in the application”) Whoever is awarded costs at the end of the claim recovers these costs too
Costs in any event The named party recovers these costs whatever the final outcome
Claimant’s costs in the case If the claimant wins overall, it recovers these costs; if it loses, no party recovers them
Defendant’s costs in the case The converse
Costs reserved The decision is deferred to a later occasion; if nothing is said later, they become costs in the case
Costs thrown away Where an order is set aside, the party who obtained it pays the costs wasted as a result
No order as to costs / each party to bear its own Nobody recovers

Payment on account. Under CPR 44.2(8), where the court orders a party to pay costs subject to detailed assessment, it will order a reasonable sum on account of those costs unless there is good reason not to. This matters commercially: detailed assessment can take a year.

15.4 Assessing the amount

15.4.1 Summary assessment

The court assesses the costs itself, immediately, and orders a specific sum. Practice Direction 44 provides that the court should make a summary assessment:

  • at the conclusion of a fast track trial, of the costs of the whole claim; and
  • at the conclusion of any other hearing lasting not more than one day, of the costs of that application or hearing.

Each party intending to claim costs must file and serve a statement of costs (form N260) not less than 24 hours before the hearing. A party that fails to do so may be penalised.

15.4.2 Detailed assessment

For larger or more complex bills, costs are assessed by a costs officer in separate proceedings under CPR Part 47:

  1. The receiving party serves a notice of commencement and a bill of costs within three months of the judgment or order giving the right to costs.
  2. The paying party serves points of dispute within 21 days.
  3. The receiving party may serve a reply within 21 days.
  4. The receiving party files a request for a detailed assessment hearing within three months of the expiry of the period for commencing.

Detailed assessment does not stay enforcement of the judgment itself.

15.4.3 Fixed recoverable costs

Since 1 October 2023, most claims worth up to £100,000 are subject to fixed recoverable costs under CPR Part 45. The regime applies to claims allocated to the fast track and the intermediate track, with the amount determined by:

  • the track;
  • the complexity band (1 to 4) to which the claim is assigned; and
  • the stage the claim has reached when it concludes — the intermediate track has a grid of stages running from pre-issue through to trial.

The consequence is that recovery no longer depends on what was actually spent. A party that spends £40,000 on a band 2 intermediate track claim recovers the fixed sum for the stage reached, and no more. The court may award more than the fixed amount only in exceptional circumstances, and there are limited exclusions from the regime.

Exam trap

Fixed costs and costs budgeting are alternatives, not companions. Fast and intermediate track claims are subject to fixed costs and are not budgeted. Multi-track Part 7 claims worth less than £10 million are subject to budgeting and their costs are assessed, not fixed. Small claims are subject to the very restricted costs rules in CPR 27.14 — essentially court fees, fixed commencement costs, limited witness expenses and a capped sum for an expert, plus costs where a party has behaved unreasonably.

15.5 Part 36 offers

Part 36 is a self-contained code with its own costs consequences, and it is the most powerful tactical instrument in civil litigation. It is examined more often than any other part of the costs syllabus.

15.5.1 Making a valid offer

CPR 36.5 — the offer must:

  • be in writing;
  • state on its face that it is intended to have the consequences of Part 36;
  • specify a relevant period of not less than 21 days within which the defendant will be liable for the claimant’s costs if the offer is accepted (unless made less than 21 days before trial);
  • state whether it relates to the whole of the claim or to part of it, and to which issues; and
  • state whether it takes into account any counterclaim.

CPR 36.6: a Part 36 offer to pay a sum of money must be an offer to pay a single sum which is payable within 14 days of acceptance.

An offer may be made at any time, including before proceedings are issued, and by either party.

15.5.2 Withdrawal and change

CPR 36.9–36.10. Before the relevant period expires, an offer may be withdrawn, or changed so as to be less advantageous to the offeree, only with the court’s permission — unless the offeror serves notice of withdrawal or change which then takes effect at the end of the relevant period, if the offer has not been accepted. After the relevant period has expired, the offeror may withdraw or change the offer freely, provided it has not already been accepted. An offer that is improved is treated as a new offer.

15.5.3 Acceptance and its costs consequences

CPR 36.11: a Part 36 offer may be accepted at any time, whether or not the offeree has subsequently made a different offer, unless it has been withdrawn. Permission is needed in limited cases, including where the trial has started.

CPR 36.13 — the costs consequences:

When accepted Costs
Within the relevant period The claimant is entitled to its costs of the proceedings, on the standard basis, up to the date on which notice of acceptance was served
After the relevant period has expired Unless the court considers it unjust, the claimant recovers costs up to the expiry of the relevant period, and the offeree pays the offeror’s costs from expiry to the date of acceptance

Acceptance stays the claim.

15.5.4 Consequences at judgment: CPR 36.17

This is the heart of Part 36. Two situations.

Where the claimant fails to obtain a judgment more advantageous than a defendant’s Part 36 offer (CPR 36.17(3)) — unless the court considers it unjust, the court must order that the defendant is entitled to:

  • its costs (including any recoverable pre-action costs) from the date on which the relevant period expired; and
  • interest on those costs.

So a claimant who wins, but for less than the defendant offered, pays the defendant’s costs from the expiry of the relevant period — often the great majority of the costs of the action.

Where judgment against the defendant is at least as advantageous to the claimant as its own Part 36 offer (CPR 36.17(4)) — unless the court considers it unjust, the court must order that the claimant is entitled to:

  1. interest on the whole or part of any sum awarded (excluding interest) at a rate not exceeding 10% above base rate, for the period from expiry of the relevant period;
  2. costs on the indemnity basis from the date on which the relevant period expired;
  3. interest on those costs at a rate not exceeding 10% above base rate; and
  4. an additional amount, not exceeding £75,000, calculated as:
  • 10% of the amount awarded where that is up to £500,000; and
  • 10% of the first £500,000 plus 5% of any amount above that, where the award exceeds £500,000.

“Unjust” (CPR 36.17(5)) — the court considers all the circumstances, including the terms of the offer, the stage at which it was made, the information available to the parties when it was made, the conduct of the parties as regards the giving or refusing to give information for the purpose of evaluating the offer, and whether the offer was a genuine attempt to settle.

Worked example

A claimant claims £400,000. In March the defendant offers £250,000 under Part 36, with a 21-day relevant period expiring on 1 April. The claimant does not accept. At trial in November the claimant is awarded £220,000.

The claimant has won — but has failed to beat the defendant’s offer. Under CPR 36.17(3):

  • The claimant recovers its costs up to 1 April.
  • The claimant pays the defendant’s costs from 1 April to judgment, plus interest on them.

Since the great majority of costs are incurred after March, the claimant’s £220,000 award may be substantially or entirely consumed. This is why the advice on whether to accept a Part 36 offer is the most consequential advice a litigator gives.

Now reverse it. Suppose the claimant had offered to accept £200,000 in March, the defendant refused, and the claimant was awarded £220,000 — at least as advantageous as its own offer. Under CPR 36.17(4) the claimant gets enhanced interest of up to 10% above base from 1 April, indemnity costs from 1 April, interest on those costs, and an additional amount of 10% of £220,000 = £22,000.

Exam trap

Three points recur.

“More advantageous” is not “beats by a lot.” CPR 36.17(2) provides that “more advantageous” means better in money terms by any amount, however small. A claimant awarded £250,001 against an offer of £250,000 has beaten it.

The claimant’s own offer needs only to be matched. For CPR 36.17(4) the judgment must be “at least as advantageous” as the claimant’s offer — equal is enough. For the defendant’s offer under 36.17(3), the claimant must obtain a judgment “more advantageous” to escape the consequences.

The offer must not be disclosed to the trial judge until all questions of liability and quantum have been decided (CPR 36.16). A Part 36 offer is not “without prejudice save as to costs” — it has its own regime — but the practical effect is the same.

15.6 Qualified one-way costs shifting

QOCS (CPR 44.13–44.17) modifies the general rule in personal injury litigation, so that an unsuccessful claimant is not ordinarily exposed to the defendant’s costs.

Scope (CPR 44.13): proceedings which include a claim for damages for personal injuries, under the Fatal Accidents Act 1976, or under the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1934 arising out of death or personal injury.

The protection (CPR 44.14): orders for costs made against a claimant may be enforced without the permission of the court, but only to the extent that the aggregate amount in money terms of such orders does not exceed the aggregate amount in money terms of any orders for, or agreements to pay or settle a claim for, damages, costs and interest made in favour of the claimant.

The April 2023 amendment

CPR 44.14 was amended for cases issued on or after 6 April 2023 and materially widened the defendant’s position. Before the amendment, a defendant could enforce only against orders for damages and interest. Now the pot against which enforcement is permitted includes agreements to pay or settle — so settlements, Part 36 acceptances and Tomlin orders count — and it includes costs as well as damages, permitting set-off of costs against costs.

The amendment reversed Cartwright v Venduct Engineering Ltd [2018] EWCA Civ 1654 (settlements did not count) and Ho v Adelekun [2021] UKSC 43 (no set-off of costs against costs). Options reflecting those decisions state the pre-April 2023 position.

The exceptions. QOCS protection is lost:

  • CPR 44.15 — without permission, where the proceedings have been struck out on the grounds that they disclosed no reasonable grounds for bringing the claim, that they were an abuse of process, or that the claimant’s conduct was likely to obstruct the just disposal of the proceedings.
  • CPR 44.16 — with the court’s permission, where the claim is found on the balance of probabilities to be fundamentally dishonest; or where the claim includes a claim made for the financial benefit of a person other than the claimant or a dependant; or where it is made other than on the claimant’s own behalf.

15.7 Security for costs

An order for security requires a claimant to provide security — usually a payment into court or a bank guarantee — for the defendant’s costs, failing which the claim is stayed or struck out.

The court may make an order only if it is satisfied, having regard to all the circumstances of the case, that it is just to do so, and one or more of the conditions in CPR 25.27 applies:

  • the claimant is resident out of the jurisdiction, and not resident in a state bound by the 2005 Hague Convention;
  • the claimant is a company or other body and there is reason to believe it will be unable to pay the defendant’s costs if ordered to do so;
  • the claimant has changed address since the claim was commenced with a view to evading the consequences of the litigation;
  • the claimant failed to give an address in the claim form, or gave an incorrect one;
  • the claimant is acting as a nominal claimant, other than as a representative claimant, and there is reason to believe it will be unable to pay the defendant’s costs if ordered to do so; or
  • the claimant has taken steps in relation to its assets that would make it difficult to enforce an order for costs against it.
Exam trap

Security is available against a claimant, not against a defendant who is merely defending. A defendant who brings a counterclaim can be ordered to give security in respect of that counterclaim — but only where the counterclaim is a genuinely independent claim, not simply a defence dressed up.

The second trap is the “impecunious claimant”. Being poor is not a condition on its own for an individual: the conditions are resident out of the jurisdiction, or a company with reason to believe it cannot pay. And even where a condition is met, the court must still find it just to make the order — and will consider whether an order would stifle a genuine claim, and whether the claimant’s impecuniosity was caused by the defendant’s own conduct.

15.8 Non-party costs orders

Section 51(1) and (3) Senior Courts Act 1981 gives the court power to order any person to pay costs, including someone who is not a party. CPR 46.2 supplies the procedure: the non-party must be added as a party for costs purposes only and given a reasonable opportunity to attend a hearing.

The jurisdiction is exceptional and is directed at the person who is in substance the “real party”. Dymocks Franchise Systems (NSW) Pty Ltd v Todd [2004] UKPC 39 identifies the touchstone: whether the non-party has not merely funded the proceedings but substantially also controlled them or was to benefit from them. Typical targets: a director who funds and controls litigation by an insolvent company for their own benefit; a commercial litigation funder; a person who procures a claim for their own ends.

Wasted costs orders are a distinct jurisdiction, directed at legal representatives. Under s.51(6) and (7) Senior Courts Act 1981 and CPR 46.8, the court may order a legal representative to meet costs wasted as a result of any improper, unreasonable or negligent act or omission. Ridehalgh v Horsefield [1994] Ch 205 sets out the three-stage approach: was the conduct improper, unreasonable or negligent; did it cause the applicant to incur unnecessary costs; and is it in all the circumstances just to make the order? Pursuing a hopeless case is not, without more, improper — a lawyer is entitled to act on the client’s instructions on a weak case — and the court will not require a waiver of privilege to be inferred against the represented party.

15.9 The solicitor’s position

Costs information. A solicitor must give the client the best possible information, both at the outset and as the matter progresses, about the likely overall cost and about how it will be paid. That includes explaining the client’s own liability for costs, the risk of paying the other side’s costs, the limits on recovery (fixed costs, the standard basis, proportionality), and the availability and implications of funding options.

Costs risk of Part 36. Advice on whether to accept a Part 36 offer must set out the consequences of failing to beat it. Failing to do so is a common source of negligence claims.

Funding. Where a conditional fee agreement is used, the client must understand that the success fee is not recoverable from the other side and comes out of the damages, subject to the statutory cap in personal injury claims. Where after-the-event insurance is taken out, the premium is generally not recoverable either.

Undertakings as to costs. Any undertaking given to another party or to the court about costs binds the solicitor personally.

15.10 Check yourself

Q1. A claimant claims £180,000. In February the defendant makes a Part 36 offer of £120,000, with a relevant period expiring on 1 March. The claimant does not accept. At trial in October the claimant is awarded £110,000.

What is the likely costs outcome?

A. The defendant pays the claimant’s costs throughout, because the claimant won.

B. Each party bears its own costs.

C. The defendant pays the claimant’s costs up to 1 March; the claimant pays the defendant’s costs from 1 March, together with interest on those costs, unless the court considers it unjust.

D. The claimant pays the defendant’s costs of the whole action.

E. The claimant recovers its costs on the indemnity basis, because it succeeded.

Q2. A claimant makes a Part 36 offer to accept £300,000, with a relevant period expiring on 1 June. The defendant refuses. At trial the claimant is awarded exactly £300,000.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Part 36 has no consequences, because the claimant did not beat its own offer — it merely matched it.

B. The claimant is entitled to enhanced interest at up to 10% above base rate from 1 June, indemnity costs from 1 June, interest on those costs, and an additional amount of 10% of £300,000, being £30,000 — unless the court considers it unjust.

C. The claimant is entitled to an additional amount of £75,000, being the statutory maximum.

D. The claimant is entitled to indemnity costs from the date of the offer, but nothing else.

E. The consequences apply only if the claimant is awarded more than the offer.

Q3. A personal injury claimant loses at trial. The claim was pursued honestly and was not struck out. During the litigation the claimant had obtained an interim costs order in its favour worth £8,000. The defendant’s costs are assessed at £60,000.

What may the defendant enforce?

A. Nothing, because QOCS gives the claimant complete immunity from costs.

B. The full £60,000, because the claimant lost.

C. Up to £8,000 — costs orders against the claimant may be enforced only to the extent of the aggregate of orders for, or agreements to pay or settle a claim for, damages, costs and interest in the claimant’s favour.

D. Up to £60,000, but only with the court’s permission.

E. Nothing, because enforcement is permitted only against orders for damages, and there were none.

Q4. A defendant seeks security for costs against a claimant company registered in England which is loss-making and has net liabilities. The claimant says the order would prevent it from pursuing a genuine claim, and that its financial difficulties were caused by the defendant’s breach of contract.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Security must be ordered, because the condition in CPR 25.27 relating to a company unable to pay costs is satisfied.

B. Security cannot be ordered, because the claimant is registered in England.

C. The condition is satisfied, but the court must also be satisfied that it is just to make the order having regard to all the circumstances — which includes whether the order would stifle a genuine claim and whether the claimant’s impecuniosity was caused by the defendant.

D. Security cannot be ordered against a company.

E. Security may be ordered only if the claimant is resident outside the jurisdiction.

Q5. After a two-hour interim application, the successful applicant asks the court to assess its costs.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Costs of an interim application are always subject to detailed assessment.

B. The court should make a summary assessment at the conclusion of a hearing lasting not more than one day; each party intending to claim costs should have filed and served a statement of costs not less than 24 hours before the hearing.

C. Costs are automatically costs in the case.

D. No costs order can be made until the end of the claim.

E. Costs must be assessed on the indemnity basis because the application succeeded.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. CPR 36.17(3). The claimant succeeded, but failed to obtain a judgment more advantageous than the defendant’s offer of £120,000. The consequence is that the defendant is entitled to its costs from the date on which the relevant period expired, plus interest on those costs, unless the court considers it unjust. The claimant keeps its costs up to that date.

  • A applies the general rule and ignores Part 36 entirely.
  • B and D overstate the sanction; the claimant does not lose its pre-expiry costs, and the defendant does not recover the costs of the whole action.
  • E applies the consequences of a claimant beating its own offer under CPR 36.17(4).

Q2 — B. CPR 36.17(4) is engaged where the judgment is “at least as advantageous” to the claimant as its own offer — matching is enough. All four consequences follow: enhanced interest at up to 10% above base from expiry of the relevant period, indemnity costs from that date, interest on those costs, and an additional amount of 10% of the amount awarded (£300,000 being under £500,000), which is £30,000.

  • A and E apply the “more advantageous” test that governs a defendant’s offer under CPR 36.17(3).
  • C applies the cap, which bites only where the calculation would otherwise exceed £75,000.
  • D gives only one of the four consequences.

Q3 — C. CPR 44.14 as amended for claims issued on or after 6 April 2023. Costs orders against a QOCS claimant may be enforced without permission, but only up to the aggregate amount in money terms of any orders for, or agreements to pay or settle a claim for, damages, costs and interest in the claimant’s favour. The claimant has an interim costs order worth £8,000, so £8,000 may be enforced. The balance of £52,000 is unenforceable.

  • A overstates QOCS, which is qualified, not absolute.
  • B ignores QOCS.
  • D describes CPR 44.16, which applies only in the exceptional cases — fundamental dishonesty and the others — none of which arises here.
  • E states the pre-April 2023 position, under which enforcement was limited to orders for damages and interest. The amendment added costs and agreements to settle, reversing Ho v Adelekun and Cartwright v Venduct Engineering.

Q4 — C. CPR 25.27 requires both that a condition is satisfied and that the court is satisfied, having regard to all the circumstances, that it is just to make the order. The condition relating to a company with reason to believe it cannot pay is met, but the discretion remains, and the two matters the claimant raises — stifling a genuine claim, and impecuniosity caused by the defendant — are established considerations against making the order.

  • A treats the condition as determinative and omits the justice requirement.
  • B and E confine security to claimants resident outside the jurisdiction, which is only one of six conditions.
  • D is simply wrong; the company condition exists precisely for this case.

Q5 — B. Practice Direction 44: the court should make a summary assessment at the conclusion of a hearing lasting not more than one day, and a statement of costs (form N260) must be filed and served not less than 24 hours before the hearing.

  • A reverses the default for short hearings.
  • C describes one possible order, but the question asks about assessment of the amount, and “costs in the case” would not be the usual order where one party has clearly succeeded on the application.
  • D ignores the whole scheme of interim costs orders.
  • E invents an automatic indemnity basis; the standard basis applies unless the conduct takes the case out of the norm.
