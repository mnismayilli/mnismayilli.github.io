14  Trial, appeals and enforcement

Assessment objective 7: preparation of a case for a trial, the trial and any post trial steps.

14.1 Preparing for trial

14.1.1 Pre-trial checklists

Towards the end of the timetable the court sends the parties a pre-trial checklist (a listing questionnaire), to be filed by a specified date. It confirms whether the directions have been complied with, whether any further directions are needed, the witnesses to be called, whether expert evidence is to be given orally, the estimated length of trial, and the availability of witnesses, experts and advocates.

If no party files a checklist, the court will normally make an unless order — that unless a checklist is filed within seven days, the claim will be struck out. If one party files and another does not, the court will usually fix a hearing or give directions.

14.1.2 Pre-trial review

On the multi-track, and in longer or more complex cases, the court may list a pre-trial review, usually before the trial judge. Its purposes are to check that the directions have been complied with, to settle the trial timetable, to decide any outstanding applications, to determine the form and scope of the evidence, and to explore settlement one last time.

14.1.3 The trial bundle

CPR 39.5: unless the court orders otherwise, the claimant must file a trial bundle not more than 7 days and not less than 3 days before the start of the trial. Its contents are prescribed by Practice Direction 32 and typically include, in this order: the claim form and all statements of case; a case summary and chronology where useful; the court orders and directions; the witness statements to be relied on; any hearsay notices; the expert reports and joint statements; and the documents disclosed and relied on.

The bundle should be paginated and indexed, agreed with the other parties wherever possible, and accompanied by a case summary in more complex cases. The costs of preparing an unnecessarily bulky bundle may be disallowed.

14.1.4 Summoning witnesses

A witness summons (CPR 34) compels a person to attend court to give evidence or to produce documents. Key points:

  • It is issued by the court. The court’s permission is required where a party wishes to have a summons issued less than 7 days before the date of the trial, or where the witness is to attend on any date except the trial date, or at any hearing except the trial (CPR 34.3(2)).
  • It is binding if served at least seven days before the date the witness is required to attend; the court may permit shorter service.
  • The party serving it must offer or pay conduct money — a sum reasonably sufficient to cover the witness’s travelling expenses and compensation for loss of time.
  • Non-compliance is a contempt of court.

A summons should be considered even for a willing witness, since it protects an employed witness from their employer and guarantees attendance.

14.1.5 Other pre-trial steps

  • Skeleton arguments are required in the higher courts and are increasingly directed elsewhere.
  • Trial fee: the claimant must pay the hearing fee by the date specified, failing which the claim will be struck out.
  • The parties should keep settlement under review; a Part 36 offer may be made or accepted at any time before judgment, and the costs consequences of a late acceptance are severe.

14.2 The trial

14.2.1 Procedure

The judge may vary the order, but the standard sequence is:

  1. Preliminary matters — housekeeping, applications, the trial timetable.
  2. Claimant’s opening, often abbreviated where skeleton arguments have been read.
  3. Claimant’s evidence. Each witness is sworn; the witness statement stands as evidence in chief (CPR 32.5), sometimes with limited supplementary questions with permission; then cross-examination by the defendant; then re-examination by the claimant, confined to matters arising from cross-examination.
  4. Defendant’s evidence, in the same way.
  5. Expert evidence, either in the party’s case or concurrently if the court has directed hot-tubbing.
  6. Closing submissions — normally the defendant first, then the claimant.
  7. Judgment, delivered orally at the end of the hearing or reserved and handed down later.
  8. Consequential matters — costs, interest, permission to appeal, any stay of execution.
Exam trap

A party who wishes to challenge a witness’s evidence must put its case to that witness in cross-examination. A defendant who does not challenge a claimant’s account in cross-examination, and then invites the judge in closing to reject it as untrue, will normally be held to have accepted it. This is the rule in Browne v Dunn, and it explains why cross-examination is where the case is actually won or lost.

14.2.2 Judgment and its effect

  • A judgment or order takes effect from the day it is given or made, unless the court specifies a later date (CPR 40.7).
  • Unless it specifies otherwise, a party must comply with a judgment for the payment of money within 14 days (CPR 40.11).
  • Interest before judgment is discretionary: s.35A Senior Courts Act 1981 in the High Court and s.69 County Courts Act 1984 in the County Court permit simple interest on damages for all or part of the period between the cause of action arising and judgment. A claim for interest must be pleaded.
  • Interest after judgment runs on High Court judgments under s.17 Judgments Act 1838 at 8%, and on County Court judgments of £5,000 or more at the same rate.
  • A judgment creates a res judicata: the same cause of action cannot be litigated again between the same parties, and issues decided are binding on them (issue estoppel). Relitigating a point that could and should have been raised is an abuse of process under the rule in Henderson v Henderson.
  • Judgments for money are registered and, for judgments not satisfied within one month, entered on the Register of Judgments, Orders and Fines.

14.3 Appeals

14.3.1 Permission

Permission to appeal is required in almost every case (CPR 52.3). It may be sought from the lower court at the hearing at which the decision was made, or from the appeal court in the appellant’s notice. A refusal by the lower court is not an appealable decision; the appellant simply asks the appeal court.

The test (CPR 52.6): permission may be given only where:

  • the appeal would have a real prospect of success; or
  • there is some other compelling reason for the appeal to be heard.

Second appeals (CPR 52.7) — an appeal to the Court of Appeal from a decision of the County Court, the family court or the High Court which was itself made on appeal — face a higher threshold. Permission may be given only by the Court of Appeal, and only where it considers that the appeal would both have a real prospect of success and raise an important point of principle or practice, or that there is some other compelling reason for the Court of Appeal to hear it.

14.3.2 Time limits and procedure

The appellant’s notice must be filed within such period as the lower court directs, or, where it directs none, within 21 days of the date of the decision appealed against (CPR 52.12). It must be served on the respondents as soon as practicable and in any event within seven days of filing.

An appeal does not operate as a stay of the decision below unless the appeal court or the lower court orders otherwise (CPR 52.16). A successful appellant who has not obtained a stay may find the judgment has already been enforced.

14.3.3 Grounds and the nature of the appeal

CPR 52.21(1): every appeal is limited to a review of the decision of the lower court, unless a practice direction provides otherwise or the court considers that in the circumstances of the individual appeal it would be in the interests of justice to hold a re-hearing.

CPR 52.21(3): the appeal court will allow an appeal where the decision of the lower court was:

  1. wrong; or
  2. unjust because of a serious procedural or other irregularity in the proceedings in the lower court.

New evidence is admitted only in accordance with the principles in Ladd v Marshall [1954] 1 WLR 1489: the evidence could not have been obtained with reasonable diligence for use at trial; it would probably have had an important influence on the result; and it is apparently credible.

Exam trap

An appeal is a review, not a second trial. An appellate court will not interfere with a trial judge’s findings of primary fact based on oral evidence unless they are plainly wrong, because the trial judge saw the witnesses. Nor will it interfere with a discretionary case management decision merely because it would have exercised the discretion differently — the decision must be outside the range of reasonable decisions. Options offering an appeal because “the judge should have believed the claimant” are wrong.

14.3.4 Destination of appeals

Set by the Access to Justice Act 1999 (Destination of Appeals) Order 2016:

Decision of Appeal lies to
District judge of the County Court Circuit judge of the County Court
Circuit judge of the County Court High Court judge
Master or district judge of the High Court High Court judge
High Court judge Court of Appeal
A decision made on appeal in the County Court or High Court Court of Appeal (a second appeal)

A “leapfrog” transfer is possible: under CPR 52.23 the court may order an appeal to be transferred to the Court of Appeal where it would raise an important point of principle or practice or there is some other compelling reason. There is a separate statutory leapfrog from the High Court to the Supreme Court under the Administration of Justice Act 1969.

Exam trap

The 2016 Order simplified the destinations by reference to the judge who made the decision. The old rule sending final decisions in multi-track Part 7 claims straight to the Court of Appeal has gone. An option routing an appeal from a circuit judge’s final multi-track decision to the Court of Appeal is stating the pre-2016 position.

14.4 Enforcing a money judgment

A judgment is not self-executing. The judgment creditor must choose a method, and the choice depends on what the debtor has.

14.4.1 Finding out what the debtor has

Order to obtain information from a judgment debtor (CPR 71) — the “oral examination”. The creditor applies without notice; the order requires the debtor (or, for a company, an officer) to attend court, usually before a court officer, to be questioned on oath about their means and to produce documents. The order must be served personally at least 14 days before the hearing, and the debtor may request travelling expenses. Failure to attend or to answer may be referred to a judge, who may make a suspended committal order for contempt.

Other sources: Land Registry searches, Companies House filings, credit reference searches, and asset tracing agents.

14.4.2 The methods

Method Rule Reaches Best where
Writ or warrant of control CPR 83; Sch 12 Tribunals, Courts and Enforcement Act 2007 Goods, which enforcement agents may take control of and sell The debtor is a trading business with valuable goods
Third party debt order CPR 72 Money owed to the debtor by a third party — most often a bank account, but also trade debtors The creditor knows where the debtor banks and the account is in credit
Charging order CPR 73 The debtor’s beneficial interest in land, securities or certain funds The debtor owns property; slow, but secure
Attachment of earnings order CPR 89 A deduction from an employed debtor’s wages The debtor is employed — not available against the self-employed, and County Court only
Appointment of a receiver CPR 69 Income or assets not reachable by other means — rent, a partnership share Unusual assets
Insolvency Insolvency Act 1986 Everything Pressure, or where the debtor has assets but will not pay

Writs and warrants of control. Enforcement agents must give the debtor a notice of enforcement of at least seven clear days before taking control of goods. They may not take goods that are exempt — tools of the trade and basic domestic items up to prescribed values — or goods belonging to someone else.

Which court executes matters, and the High Court and County Courts Jurisdiction Order 1991, article 8, splits it three ways for a County Court money judgment enforced against goods:

Sum to be enforced Where
£5,000 or more High Court only — transfer up, and a writ of control executed by a High Court Enforcement Officer
£600 to under £5,000 Either court
Less than £600 County Court only — a warrant of control executed by a County Court enforcement agent

A judgment in proceedings arising out of an agreement regulated by the Consumer Credit Act 1974 must be enforced in the County Court, whatever the sum. High Court enforcement is generally faster, which is why creditors transfer up wherever the option exists.

Third party debt orders proceed in two stages: an interim order made without notice, which freezes the sum in the third party’s hands, followed by a hearing at which a final order is made requiring payment to the creditor. The court will not make an order that causes hardship to an individual debtor, and a joint account is generally beyond reach unless the judgment is against all the account holders.

Charging orders also proceed in two stages — interim then final — and a final charging order may be registered against the title. It secures the debt but does not produce money; the creditor must then apply for an order for sale, which is discretionary and, for a modest debt secured on a family home, frequently refused or postponed.

Exam trap

Match the method to the asset, because that is the discrimination the examiner is testing:

  • A debtor with a shop full of stock → writ or warrant of control.
  • A debtor with a healthy bank balance → third party debt order.
  • A debtor who owns a house but has no cash → charging order, then possibly an order for sale.
  • A debtor in employment with no assets → attachment of earnings.
  • A self-employed debtor → not attachment of earnings.

The creditor is not confined to one method and may pursue several, subject to the court’s control.

14.4.3 Enforcing abroad

Where the assets are in another country, the English judgment must be recognised there.

Route When it applies
Hague 2019 Judgments Convention Judgments in proceedings issued on or after 1 July 2025, as between the UK and other contracting states — the EU, Ukraine, Uruguay, Albania and Montenegro among them. Not confined to exclusive jurisdiction clauses
Hague 2005 Choice of Court Convention Where the judgment was given by a court designated in an exclusive choice of court agreement
Administration of Justice Act 1920 and Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933 Registration schemes for judgments of certain Commonwealth and other designated countries
Common law Otherwise: the creditor brings a fresh action on the English judgment in the foreign court, treating the judgment as a debt

The same framework operates in reverse for enforcing a foreign judgment in England and Wales.

Post-Brexit position — verify against your cut-off

The Brussels Recast Regulation, which provided near-automatic enforcement across the EU, no longer applies to proceedings commenced in England and Wales after the end of the transition period. Between 1 January 2021 and 1 July 2025 the position was patchy: Hague 2005 covered only exclusive jurisdiction clauses, and everything else fell back on the 1920 and 1933 Acts or the common law. The entry into force of Hague 2019 for the UK on 1 July 2025 substantially improved matters, but it applies only to proceedings issued on or after that date.

14.5 Check yourself

Q1. A trial is listed to begin on Monday 12 October. The claimant’s solicitor asks when the trial bundle must be filed.

Which of the following is correct?

A. At least 21 days before the start of the trial.

B. Not more than 7 days and not less than 3 days before the start of the trial.

C. On the first day of the trial.

D. At least 14 days before the start of the trial.

E. Whenever the parties agree.

Q2. A circuit judge sitting in the County Court gives a final judgment in a multi-track claim worth £300,000. The losing party wishes to appeal on the ground that the judge misapplied the law on remoteness of damage.

To which court does the appeal lie, and what must the appellant establish to obtain permission?

A. The Court of Appeal, because the claim was allocated to the multi-track and the decision was final.

B. A High Court judge, and permission may be given only where the appeal would have a real prospect of success or there is some other compelling reason for it to be heard.

C. The Supreme Court, because the point is one of law.

D. A different circuit judge, and no permission is required.

E. The Court of Appeal, and the appellant must show an important point of principle or practice.

Q3. A judgment creditor is owed £40,000 by an individual who is self-employed as a consultant, rents his home, has no significant goods, but is owed £60,000 by a single large client, payable next month.

Which method of enforcement is most likely to be effective?

A. An attachment of earnings order.

B. A charging order over his home.

C. A third party debt order against the client who owes him £60,000.

D. A warrant of control.

E. A receiver by way of equitable execution over his goods.

Q4. A defendant loses at trial. The judge found the claimant’s principal witness credible and the defendant’s witness unconvincing, having heard them both give evidence over two days. The defendant wishes to appeal on the basis that the judge should have preferred its witness.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The appeal will succeed if the appeal court, reading the transcripts, would have reached a different view.

B. Permission is likely to be refused: an appeal is a review, not a re-hearing, and an appellate court will not interfere with findings of primary fact based on oral evidence unless they are plainly wrong.

C. The appeal court will hear the witnesses again.

D. The appeal will succeed because a finding on credibility is a question of law.

E. Permission is not required where the appeal concerns findings of fact.

Q5. A claimant obtains judgment for £250,000 against a company incorporated in Uruguay, in proceedings issued in England in September 2025 under an English jurisdiction clause. The company’s assets are all in Uruguay.

What is the most likely route to enforcement?

A. The Brussels Recast Regulation.

B. There is no route; an English judgment cannot be enforced outside the United Kingdom.

C. The Hague 2019 Judgments Convention, which entered into force for the United Kingdom on 1 July 2025 and applies to judgments in proceedings issued on or after that date, Uruguay being a contracting state.

D. A fresh action on the judgment at common law in Uruguay, because no convention applies.

E. Registration under the Foreign Judgments (Reciprocal Enforcement) Act 1933.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. CPR 39.5(2): unless the court orders otherwise, the claimant must file the trial bundle not more than seven days and not less than three days before the start of the trial. The window is deliberately narrow so that the bundle is complete but the court has time to read it.

Q2 — B. Under the Access to Justice Act 1999 (Destination of Appeals) Order 2016, an appeal from a circuit judge in the County Court lies to a High Court judge. The permission test in CPR 52.6 is a real prospect of success or some other compelling reason.

  • A applies the pre-2016 rule sending final multi-track decisions to the Court of Appeal, which the 2016 Order abolished. Destination now turns on the judge who made the decision.
  • C ignores the appellate structure entirely.
  • D invents a horizontal appeal and dispenses with permission.
  • E applies the second appeal test in CPR 52.7, which is engaged only where the decision appealed from was itself made on appeal — and states it incompletely, since that test requires a real prospect of success as well as an important point of principle or practice, unless there is some other compelling reason.

Q3 — C. A third party debt order under CPR 72 attaches a debt owed to the judgment debtor by a third party. A single, large, identified debt falling due next month is the ideal target: the interim order freezes it in the client’s hands and the final order directs payment to the creditor.

  • A fails because attachment of earnings requires an employed debtor and is not available against the self-employed.
  • B fails because he rents; there is no beneficial interest in land to charge.
  • D and E fail because he has no significant goods.

Q4 — B. CPR 52.21(1): an appeal is limited to a review. An appellate court has not seen the witnesses and will not interfere with findings of primary fact drawn from oral evidence unless they are plainly wrong or unsupported by any evidence. A submission that the judge should have believed the other witness is the paradigm hopeless appeal.

  • A applies a re-hearing standard.
  • C misdescribes appellate procedure; new evidence is admitted only on the Ladd v Marshall principles, and witnesses are not recalled.
  • D mischaracterises a finding of fact as one of law.
  • E is wrong: permission is required whatever the ground.

Q5 — C. The Hague 2019 Judgments Convention entered into force for the United Kingdom on 1 July 2025 and applies to judgments given in proceedings issued on or after that date. Proceedings were issued in September 2025, and Uruguay is a contracting state, so the Convention provides the treaty route for recognition and enforcement.

  • A no longer applies to proceedings commenced in England and Wales.
  • B is simply wrong.
  • D is the fallback where no convention applies, which is not the case here.
  • E applies to designated countries under a reciprocal registration scheme, which is not the route for Uruguay.
