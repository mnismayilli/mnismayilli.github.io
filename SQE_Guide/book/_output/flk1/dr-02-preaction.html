10  Before proceedings

Assessment objective 3: pre-action considerations and steps.

10.1 Limitation

Limitation is the first thing a litigator checks and the last thing they can fix. It is examined by calculation, so learn the period, the trigger date, and the exceptions.

10.1.1 The periods

Claim Period Runs from Provision
Simple contract 6 years The date of breach s.5 Limitation Act 1980
Contract under deed (specialty) 12 years The date of breach s.8
Tort generally 6 years The date the cause of action accrued — for negligence, when damage is suffered s.2
Negligence causing latent damage (not personal injury) 6 years from accrual or 3 years from the starting date, whichever expires later — subject to a 15-year longstop Knowledge of the material facts about the damage ss.14A, 14B
Personal injury 3 years The later of the date of accrual and the claimant’s date of knowledge ss.11, 14
Fatal accidents 3 years The later of the date of death and the dependant’s date of knowledge s.12
Defective products (Consumer Protection Act 1987) 3 years, with a 10-year longstop from when the product was put into circulation Damage or knowledge s.11A
Contribution between wrongdoers 2 years The judgment or settlement fixing the liability to be contributed to s.10
Recovery of land 12 years Accrual s.15
Exam trap

For a contract claim, time runs from the breach — not from when the claimant discovered it, and not from when the loss was suffered. For a tort claim in negligence, time runs from when damage occurs, which may be much later than the negligent act. A question that gives you a negligently drafted document, a breach years later, and damage later still is testing exactly this: the contract claim may be statute-barred while the tortious claim is alive.

10.1.2 Knowledge, and the extensions

Date of knowledge (s.14) for a personal injury claim: the date on which the claimant first knew that the injury was significant, that it was attributable in whole or in part to the act or omission alleged to constitute negligence, and the identity of the defendant. Knowledge includes what the claimant might reasonably have been expected to acquire, including from expert advice it was reasonable to seek. Knowledge that, as a matter of law, the act amounted to negligence is irrelevant.

Section 14A applies the same idea to other negligence claims: the claimant gets the longer of six years from accrual, or three years from the date they had both the knowledge required to bring the action and the right to bring it — subject to the absolute 15-year longstop in s.14B, which runs from the negligent act or omission and applies even if the claimant never had knowledge.

Section 28 — disability. If the claimant was a child or lacked capacity when the cause of action accrued, time does not begin to run until the disability ends: a child’s period therefore runs from their 18th birthday, so a child injured at seven has until their 21st birthday to bring a personal injury claim. Supervening disability does not stop the clock.

Section 32 — fraud, concealment and mistake. Where the action is based on the defendant’s fraud, where a fact relevant to the claimant’s right of action has been deliberately concealed by the defendant, or where the action is for relief from the consequences of a mistake, time does not begin to run until the claimant has discovered the fraud, concealment or mistake, or could with reasonable diligence have discovered it.

Sections 29–31 — acknowledgment and part payment. In a claim for a debt or other liquidated sum, a written, signed acknowledgment of the debt, or a part payment, restarts the six-year clock — but cannot revive a claim already statute-barred.

Section 33 — the discretion. In personal injury and fatal accident claims only, the court has an equitable discretion to disapply the primary limitation period where it is equitable to do so, having regard to the prejudice to each party and to the s.33(3) factors: the length of and reasons for the delay, the effect of the delay on the cogency of the evidence, the defendant’s conduct after the cause of action arose, the duration of any disability, how promptly the claimant acted once they knew, and the steps the claimant took to obtain advice.

Exam trap

Section 33 is confined to personal injury and fatal accident claims. There is no general judicial discretion to extend limitation in a contract or property claim, however sympathetic the facts. An option offering the court a discretion to disapply the six-year contract period is always wrong.

10.1.3 How limitation operates

  • It is a procedural defence. It bars the remedy, not the right, and it must be pleaded in the defence — the court will not take the point of its own motion. (Conversion under s.3 and land under s.17 are the exceptions, where title is extinguished.)
  • The claim is brought when the claim form is received by the court office, not when it is sealed. Practice Direction 7A says so, and a claim form delivered on the last day is in time even if it is issued days later.
  • If the period is about to expire and the pre-action protocol has not been complied with, the claimant should issue to protect the position and then invite the court to stay the proceedings so that the protocol steps can be completed.
  • Parties may agree a standstill agreement to suspend time while they negotiate. Draft it carefully: an agreement that merely says the parties “will not take a limitation point” is a contractual promise, not an extension.

10.2 Practice Direction – Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols

Where no approved protocol covers the dispute, the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols applies. Its aims (para 3) are that the parties should:

  • understand each other’s position;
  • make decisions about how to proceed;
  • try to settle the issues without proceedings;
  • consider a form of ADR;
  • support the efficient management of proceedings; and
  • reduce the costs of resolving the dispute.

10.2.1 The steps

Paragraph 6 sets out what the parties should do where no protocol applies:

  1. The claimant writes to the defendant with concise details of the claim — the basis on which it is made, a summary of the facts, what the claimant wants from the defendant, and, if money, how the amount is calculated.
  2. The defendant responds within a reasonable time — 14 days in a straightforward case, and no more than three months in a very complex one — saying whether the claim is accepted and, if not, the reasons, together with an explanation of which facts and parts of the claim are disputed and whether the defendant is making a counterclaim.
  3. The parties disclose key documents relevant to the issues in dispute.
  4. The parties consider ADR.

Paragraph 4 imposes proportionality: the steps taken must be proportionate, and the Practice Direction must not be used as a tactical device to generate costs or to gain an unfair advantage.

10.2.2 ADR and the last resort principle

Litigation is a last resort (para 8). The parties should consider whether negotiation or another form of ADR might enable them to settle, and the court may require evidence that the parties considered it. Silence in response to an invitation to participate may itself be unreasonable and may lead to a costs sanction.

10.2.3 Non-compliance and its consequences

The court will look at whether the parties have complied in substance; minor or technical failures are not penalised. Where there has been a significant failure, the court may:

  • stay the proceedings while the missed steps are taken;
  • order the party at fault to pay costs, in whole or in part;
  • order those costs on the indemnity basis;
  • if the party at fault is a claimant who has been awarded a sum of money, deprive it of interest or award interest at a lower rate;
  • if the party at fault is a defendant ordered to pay a sum of money, award interest at a rate up to 10% above base rate.
Exam trap

Failure to comply with a protocol does not invalidate the claim, and it is not a ground for striking out. The sanctions are about costs and interest, and occasionally a stay. An option saying the claim will be struck out because the claimant did not send a letter before claim is wrong.

10.2.4 The approved protocols

Where an approved protocol applies, it displaces the Practice Direction’s generic steps. Those most likely to appear:

Protocol Distinctive features
Personal Injury Letter of claim in two copies (one for insurers); defendant has 21 days to acknowledge and 3 months thereafter to investigate and state whether liability is admitted; agreed expert procedure with the claimant nominating names
Debt Claims Applies where a business claims from an individual. Letter of claim with an information sheet, reply form and financial statement; the debtor has 30 days to respond; a further 30 days if documents are requested; the creditor must give 14 days’ notice before issuing
Professional Negligence Preliminary notice; letter of claim; the professional has 21 days to acknowledge and 3 months to investigate and provide a letter of response and/or settlement
Construction and Engineering Letter of claim, response, and a pre-action meeting
Clinical Disputes Request for records; letter of claim; 4 months to respond

Each shares a common shape — letter of claim, acknowledgment, substantive response, disclosure of key documents, consideration of ADR — and each attaches its own timetable. The structure is examinable; the individual timetables less so, except for the debt claims protocol, which is distinctive because it protects consumers.

10.3 Applicable law

Which country’s law governs the dispute is decided by assimilated versions of the Rome I and Rome II Regulations, which continue to apply in England and Wales after Brexit.

10.3.1 Contract: Rome I

Article 3 — party choice. The contract is governed by the law chosen by the parties. The choice must be express or clearly demonstrated by the terms or the circumstances. Parties may choose the law for the whole or part of the contract, and may change it.

Article 4 — no choice. A cascade:

  1. Specific rules by contract type:

    Contract Governing law
    Sale of goods The seller’s habitual residence
    Provision of services The service provider’s habitual residence
    A right in rem in, or a tenancy of, immovable property Where the property is situated
    Franchise The franchisee’s habitual residence
    Distribution The distributor’s habitual residence
    Sale of goods by auction Where the auction takes place

  2. Otherwise, or where the contract falls into more than one category: the law of the country where the party required to effect the characteristic performance has their habitual residence.

  3. Escape clause (Art 4(3)): if it is clear from all the circumstances that the contract is manifestly more closely connected with another country, that country’s law applies.

  4. Failing all of that (Art 4(4)): the law of the country with which it is most closely connected.

Protective rules. Article 6 (consumer contracts) and Article 8 (individual employment contracts) provide that a choice of law cannot deprive the consumer of the protection of the mandatory rules of their habitual residence, or the employee of the mandatory rules of the country where they habitually work. Article 9 preserves the overriding mandatory provisions of the forum, and Article 21 allows the court to refuse to apply a foreign rule that is manifestly incompatible with public policy.

For a company, “habitual residence” means its place of central administration; for a person acting in the course of business, their principal place of business.

10.3.2 Tort: Rome II

Article 4(1) — the general rule. The applicable law is that of the country in which the damage occurs, irrespective of the country in which the event giving rise to the damage occurred, and irrespective of the countries in which the indirect consequences of that event occur.

Article 4(2). If, at the time the damage occurs, both the person claimed to be liable and the person sustaining damage have their habitual residence in the same country, the law of that country applies instead.

Article 4(3) — escape clause. If the tort is manifestly more closely connected with another country, that country’s law applies. A pre-existing relationship between the parties, such as a contract, is expressly identified as capable of establishing that connection.

Article 14 — choice. The parties may choose the applicable law: after the event giving rise to the damage, or before it if all the parties are pursuing a commercial activity and the agreement is freely negotiated.

Special regimes: product liability (Art 5, a cascade), unfair competition (Art 6), environmental damage (Art 7, where the claimant may elect for the law of the country in which the event occurred), and intellectual property (Art 8, the law of the country for which protection is claimed, with no freedom to choose).

Exam trap

Article 4(1) is counter-intuitive and reliably tested. The law is that of the place where the damage occurs, not where the wrongful act was committed. A defective component made negligently in France and fitted to a machine that injures a worker in England produces English law under Art 4(1) — unless Art 4(2) or Art 4(3) displaces it. Note also that Rome II excludes defamation and privacy, for which the common law rules apply.

10.4 Jurisdiction

Since the end of the transition period the Brussels Recast Regulation no longer applies to proceedings commenced in England and Wales. Three regimes now matter.

10.4.1 Hague 2005 — exclusive choice of court agreements

Where a contract contains an exclusive jurisdiction clause in favour of the courts of a contracting state, the 2005 Hague Convention on Choice of Court Agreements requires the chosen court to hear the case and other contracting states to decline. The contracting parties include the EU, the UK, Mexico, Singapore and Montenegro. The Convention does not cover asymmetric (one-sided) clauses on the prevailing view, and does not cover non-exclusive clauses.

10.4.2 Hague 2019 — recognition and enforcement

The 2019 Hague Judgments Convention entered into force for the United Kingdom on 1 July 2025, applying to judgments arising out of proceedings issued on or after that date. It provides a treaty basis for the recognition and enforcement of judgments between contracting states — the EU, Ukraine, Uruguay, Albania and Montenegro among them — and is significantly wider than Hague 2005 because it is not confined to exclusive jurisdiction clauses.

10.4.3 The common law rules

Where neither Convention applies, the English court’s jurisdiction depends on service.

Defendant within the jurisdiction. Service of the claim form on a defendant present in England and Wales founds jurisdiction as of right. The defendant’s remedy is to apply for a stay on the ground of forum non conveniens.

Spiliada Maritime Corp v Cansulex Ltd [1987] AC 460 supplies the test: the court will stay proceedings if the defendant shows that there is another available forum which is clearly or distinctly more appropriate — the “natural forum”, with which the action has the most real and substantial connection. The burden then shifts to the claimant to show that justice nevertheless requires the trial to take place in England.

Defendant outside the jurisdiction. The claimant needs the court’s permission to serve out (CPR 6.36), unless one of the exceptions in CPR 6.32–6.33 applies — most importantly where the contract contains a term to the effect that the English court shall have jurisdiction, or where jurisdiction arises under Hague 2005.

To obtain permission, the claimant must establish three things:

  1. A good arguable case that the claim falls within one of the gateways in Practice Direction 6B, paragraph 3.1;
  2. A serious issue to be tried on the merits — a real prospect of success; and
  3. That England is the proper place in which to bring the claim (forum conveniens).

The gateways most often examined:

Category Gateway
General The defendant is domiciled within the jurisdiction; a claim for an injunction ordering the defendant to do or refrain from doing an act within the jurisdiction; the defendant is a necessary or proper party to a claim against another defendant
Contract The contract was made within the jurisdiction; made by or through an agent within the jurisdiction; is governed by English law; contains a term conferring English jurisdiction; or the breach was committed within the jurisdiction
Tort Damage was or will be sustained within the jurisdiction; or the damage results from an act committed, or likely to be committed, within the jurisdiction

FS Cairo (Nile Plaza) LLC v Brownlie [2021] UKSC 45 confirmed that “damage” in the tort gateway bears its ordinary meaning and covers any significant damage sustained in England — including continuing pain and financial loss suffered here after an accident abroad. The gateway is broad; the real filter is the third requirement, forum conveniens.

Exam trap

Keep the three limbs separate. Satisfying a gateway is not enough: the claimant must also show a serious issue to be tried and that England is the proper place. And keep jurisdiction separate from applicable law — an English court can and often does apply foreign law, so the fact that French law governs a contract does not deprive the English court of jurisdiction.

10.5 Where to start proceedings

10.5.1 High Court or County Court

Practice Direction 7A allocates business between them:

Claim Where it may be started
Money claim (not personal injury) of £100,000 or less County Court only
Money claim of more than £100,000 County Court or High Court
Personal injury claim worth less than £50,000 County Court only
Personal injury claim worth £50,000 or more County Court or High Court

A claimant issuing in the High Court must state in the claim form that it expects to recover more than £100,000 (or, for personal injury, £50,000 or more), or that an enactment requires the claim to be started in the High Court, or that the claim is to proceed in a specialist High Court list.

Beyond value, the factors pointing to the High Court are the financial value and amount in dispute, the complexity of the facts, legal issues, remedies or procedures, and the importance of the outcome to the public in general.

A case started in the wrong court is not a nullity: the court may transfer it (ss.40–42 County Courts Act 1984), and may penalise the claimant in costs for starting in the High Court unnecessarily.

10.5.2 The divisions and the specialist courts

The High Court has three divisions: the King’s Bench Division (contract and tort, including most commercial disputes and personal injury), the Chancery Division (business, property, trusts, land, intellectual property, companies and insolvency), and the Family Division.

The Business and Property Courts are an umbrella for the specialist lists:

Court or list Business
Commercial Court (KBD) National and international commercial disputes — sale of goods, insurance, banking, shipping
Circuit Commercial Courts Commercial claims of lower value, outside London
Technology and Construction Court (KBD) Building and engineering disputes, professional negligence against construction professionals, IT disputes
Admiralty Court (KBD) Shipping, collisions, salvage
Financial List Financial markets claims of £50 million or more, or requiring market expertise
Intellectual Property Enterprise Court (Chancery) Lower-value IP claims, with a £500,000 damages cap and recoverable costs capped at £60,000 on liability and £30,000 on quantum
Companies, Insolvency, Competition, Property, Trusts, Revenue lists As their names suggest

10.6 The solicitor’s position

Advising on limitation. A missed limitation period is the archetypal negligence claim against a litigation solicitor. The date must be calculated and diarised at the outset, and where it is close the right course is to issue protectively rather than to complete the protocol.

Not taking unfair advantage. A solicitor must not take unfair advantage of a third party, which constrains the tone and content of a letter before claim to a litigant in person: threatening consequences that cannot follow, or asserting a claim known to be bad, breaches the Code of Conduct. The SRA warning notice on SLAPPs — strategic lawsuits against public participation — is expressly flagged in the assessment specification, and identifies aggressive pre-action correspondence designed to silence rather than to resolve as a conduct risk.

Confidentiality in pre-action correspondence. A letter marked “without prejudice” is protected only if it is a genuine attempt to settle a dispute; the label alone does not create the privilege, and labelling an open assertion of claim “without prejudice” does not make it so.

Undertakings. Any undertaking given in pre-action correspondence — to preserve documents, to hold funds, not to issue before a date — is personally binding on the solicitor and enforceable by the court. Give none that is not within your control.

10.7 Check yourself

Q1. A surveyor negligently valued a property on 1 March 2018. The claimant relied on the valuation and bought the property on 1 June 2018, suffering an immediate loss. The claimant did not discover the negligence until 1 September 2024, when a second survey revealed it. The claimant consults a solicitor on 1 October 2026. There is no contract under deed.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Both the contract and the tort claims are statute-barred, and no extension is available.

B. The contract claim is statute-barred, but the tortious claim may be brought under s.14A of the Limitation Act 1980, which gives the claimant three years from the date of knowledge, expiring on 1 September 2027, subject to the 15-year longstop in s.14B.

C. Both claims remain in time, because time did not begin to run until the negligence was discovered.

D. The court may exercise its discretion under s.33 to disapply the limitation period.

E. The tortious claim is statute-barred, but the contract claim remains in time because a contract claim runs from the date of discovery.

Q2. An English company sells machinery to a Spanish company. The contract contains no choice of law clause and no jurisdiction clause. A dispute arises about whether the machinery conformed to the contract.

Which law governs the contract?

A. Spanish law, because the buyer is Spanish and the goods were delivered there.

B. English law, because the seller has its habitual residence in England and the contract is one for the sale of goods.

C. The law of the country with which the contract is most closely connected, which will always be that of the place of delivery.

D. English law, because proceedings would be issued in England.

E. There is no applicable law until the parties agree one.

Q3. A claimant is injured in a road accident in Portugal caused by the negligent driving of another English resident, who was also on holiday there. Both are habitually resident in England. The claimant issues proceedings in England.

Which law applies to the tort?

A. Portuguese law, under Article 4(1) of Rome II, because that is where the damage occurred.

B. English law, under Article 4(2) of Rome II, because both parties were habitually resident in the same country when the damage occurred.

C. English law, because the claim was issued in England and the forum applies its own law.

D. Portuguese law, because the event giving rise to the damage occurred there and Article 4(1) looks to the place of the event.

E. The claimant may choose which law applies.

Q4. A claimant issues a claim for £85,000 for breach of a commercial supply contract, with no personal injury element, in the High Court.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The claim is properly issued, because a claim for more than £50,000 may be issued in the High Court.

B. The claim should have been started in the County Court, because a money claim of £100,000 or less may only be started there; the court may transfer it and may penalise the claimant in costs.

C. The claim is a nullity and must be reissued.

D. The claim is properly issued, because commercial claims may always be issued in the High Court.

E. The claim should have been issued in the Commercial Court.

Q5. A defendant fails to respond at all to a letter of claim sent under the Practice Direction on Pre-Action Conduct and Protocols, and ignores an invitation to mediate. Proceedings are issued and the defendant ultimately succeeds at trial.

Which of the following is the most likely consequence of the defendant’s pre-action conduct?

A. The claim will be struck out for failure to comply with the Practice Direction.

B. There is no consequence, because the defendant won.

C. The court may take the non-compliance into account in exercising its discretion as to costs, and may reduce or disallow part of the successful defendant’s costs or order costs on a different basis.

D. The defendant will be ordered to pay interest at up to 10% above base rate.

E. The court must order a stay of the proceedings.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. The contract claim accrued on breach — the negligent valuation on 1 March 2018 — so the six-year period under s.5 expired on 1 March 2024. The tortious claim accrued when damage was suffered on 1 June 2018, so the primary six-year period under s.2 expired on 1 June 2024. But s.14A gives the claimant the longer of six years from accrual and three years from the date of knowledge. Knowledge came on 1 September 2024, so the claim may be brought until 1 September 2027 — comfortably covering a consultation in October 2026. The s.14B 15-year longstop runs from the negligent act (1 March 2018) and does not expire until 2033.

  • A overlooks s.14A entirely.
  • C applies a discoverability rule to the contract claim, where none exists.
  • D applies s.33, which is confined to personal injury and fatal accident claims.
  • E reverses the position: it is the tort claim that benefits from the knowledge extension, not the contract claim.

Q2 — B. Rome I, Article 4(1)(a): in the absence of choice, a contract for the sale of goods is governed by the law of the country where the seller has their habitual residence. The seller is English, so English law applies. (For a company, habitual residence means its place of central administration.)

  • A applies the buyer’s law, which is the opposite of the rule.
  • C skips the specific rules in Art 4(1) and goes straight to a closest-connection test, and then states it inflexibly. Article 4(3) is an escape clause requiring a manifestly closer connection.
  • D confuses jurisdiction with applicable law. An English court frequently applies foreign law.
  • E is simply wrong: Rome I supplies the answer where the parties have not.

Q3 — B. Rome II, Article 4(2): where both the person claimed to be liable and the person sustaining damage are habitually resident in the same country when the damage occurs, the law of that country applies instead of the Art 4(1) rule. Both are habitually resident in England, so English law governs — including on quantification of damages.

  • A and D apply Art 4(1) without checking Art 4(2), and D additionally misstates Art 4(1), which looks to where the damage occurs, not the event.
  • C assumes the forum applies its own law, which is what the Rome Regulations exist to prevent.
  • E offers a unilateral choice that exists only under Art 7 (environmental damage). Article 14 requires agreement.

Q4 — B. Practice Direction 7A: proceedings may only be started in the High Court if the value of the claim exceeds £100,000 (or, for personal injury, is £50,000 or more). £85,000 is below the threshold, so the claim belongs in the County Court.

  • A applies the personal injury threshold to a non-personal injury claim.
  • C overstates the consequence: starting in the wrong court is an irregularity, not a nullity. The court may transfer under s.40 County Courts Act 1984.
  • D invents a rule; the Commercial Court has its own criteria and the value here is far below what it would ordinarily take.
  • E compounds the error.

Q5 — C. Non-compliance with the Practice Direction is taken into account in the exercise of the court’s costs discretion (CPR 44.2(4)(a) — conduct, including conduct before proceedings). The court may reduce or disallow part of the successful party’s costs, or award costs on the indemnity basis against the party at fault. Ignoring an invitation to mediate compounds the point: silence in response to such an invitation may itself be unreasonable.

  • A overstates the sanction; failure to comply with a protocol does not invalidate or defeat a claim.
  • B ignores the court’s power to deprive even a successful party of costs for its conduct.
  • D applies a sanction available against a defendant ordered to pay a sum of money — this defendant won and pays nothing.
  • E describes a discretionary remedy, and one directed at incomplete protocol steps before trial, not at a defendant who has already succeeded.
