4  Stakeholders: rights, powers and protection

Assessment objective 3: the interests, rights, obligations and powers of stakeholders in a business.

4.1 Who the stakeholders are, and what they can actually do

A business has several constituencies, and the law gives each of them a different kind of leverage. The single most useful thing to hold in your head is that the strength of a stakeholder’s position depends on the mechanism available to them, not on the strength of their grievance.

Stakeholder Principal leverage
Shareholders Votes; the s.994 petition; the derivative claim; the winding-up petition
Directors Control of day-to-day management; their service contracts
Creditors Contract and security; insolvency procedures; the creditor duty as enforced by an office-holder
Employees Employment law; a “have regard” factor under s.172; preferential status in an insolvency
Partners / LLP members The agreement; the PA 1890 or LLP default terms; dissolution or resignation

4.2 Shareholders

4.2.1 What a share is

A share is not a slice of the company’s assets. Borland’s Trustee v Steel Brothers & Co Ltd [1901] 1 Ch 279 describes it as “the interest of a shareholder in the company measured by a sum of money, for the purpose of liability in the first place, and of interest in the second, but also consisting of a series of mutual covenants entered into by all the shareholders”. It is an item of personal property (s.541 CA 2006), transferable in accordance with the articles (s.544).

The rights attached to a share come from the articles, subject to the Act. A single ordinary share in a company with unamended Model Articles carries:

  • the right to vote — one vote on a show of hands (per member), one vote per share on a poll;
  • the right to a dividend if one is lawfully declared;
  • the right to a return of capital on a winding up, after creditors have been paid;
  • the right to transfer the share, subject to any restriction in the articles;
  • the right to receive notice of general meetings, to attend, and to appoint a proxy;
  • the right to receive the annual accounts;
  • the right to inspect the register of members and the minutes of general meetings.

None of these is a right to be involved in management, to be employed by the company, or to be paid a dividend. Those are the three expectations that generate unfair prejudice litigation.

4.2.2 Becoming and ceasing to be a member

Section 112: the subscribers to the memorandum are members on registration; every other person who agrees to become a member and whose name is entered in the register of members is a member. Entry on the register is what makes you a member — not the share certificate, and not the transfer form.

A person ceases to be a member on transfer and registration of the transferee, on forfeiture or surrender, on a valid buyback, or on death or bankruptcy followed by registration of the personal representative or trustee.

Exam trap

A shareholder cannot be expelled. There is no company law equivalent of an expulsion clause in a partnership agreement, unless the articles contain a compulsory transfer provision — which good drafting supplies as “leaver” provisions. If the question tells you the majority “voted to remove her as a shareholder”, the answer is that they cannot: they can remove her from the board under s.168, but her shares are her property.

4.2.3 The percentage thresholds

More marks turn on this table than on any other single page of the subject. Learn it in both directions: what a given percentage lets you do, and what it lets you block.

Holding What it enables
100% Unanimous informal assent (Duomatic); introduce entrenchment (s.22)
90% (majority in number holding 90% in nominal value) Consent to short notice of a general meeting of a private company (95% for a public company)
75% Pass a special resolution: amend the articles, change the name, disapply pre-emption rights, reduce capital, approve a buyback out of capital, wind up voluntarily, direct the board under MA 4
More than 50% Pass an ordinary resolution: appoint and remove directors, approve SPTs, loans, payments for loss of office, service contracts; declare a final dividend; grant s.551 authority to allot
More than 25% Block a special resolution
15% of the issued shares of a class Apply to court to have a variation of class rights cancelled (s.633)
10% Demand a poll (s.321); require an audit where the company would otherwise be exempt (s.476)
5% Require the directors to call a general meeting (s.303); require circulation of a written resolution (s.292) or of a statement (s.314)
Any single share Petition for unfair prejudice (s.994); apply for permission to bring a derivative claim (s.260); petition for a just and equitable winding up (s.122(1)(g) IA 1986)
Worked example

A company has 100 shares: A holds 74, B holds 26.

A cannot amend the articles, because she needs 75 and B’s 26 blocks her. A can remove B from the board, appoint herself sole director, and approve any transaction requiring an ordinary resolution. B cannot pass anything, but he can: block every special resolution; requisition a general meeting; require an audit; demand a poll; petition under s.994; and seek permission for a derivative claim.

Now give A 75 and B 25. A can do everything by resolution. B’s blocking power has gone — but every one of his individual rights survives, and it is those, not his votes, that the exam is usually about.

4.3 Minority protection

4.3.1 The problem, and the starting point

The default rule is majority rule, and the courts do not run companies. Foss v Harbottle (1843) 2 Hare 461 supplies the two principles that everything else is an exception to:

  • The proper claimant rule — where a wrong is done to the company, the company is the proper claimant.
  • The internal management rule — the court will not interfere in a matter that the company in general meeting can itself ratify.

Three statutory or equitable routes cut through this: the unfair prejudice petition, the derivative claim, and the just and equitable winding up.

4.3.2 Unfair prejudice: s.994 CA 2006

A member may petition on the ground that the company’s affairs are being, or have been, conducted in a manner that is unfairly prejudicial to the interests of members generally or of some part of the members (including at least himself), or that an actual or proposed act or omission is or would be so prejudicial.

Both limbs are needed, and they are separate. Conduct can be prejudicial without being unfair (the company genuinely needs to retain profits) and unfair without being prejudicial (a technical breach causing no harm).

Re Saul D Harrison & Sons plc [1995] 1 BCLC 14 — Hoffmann LJ: fairness is judged in the context of a commercial relationship whose terms are contained in the articles and any collateral agreements. A member’s expectations are ordinarily those set out in the constitution.

O’Neill v Phillips [1999] 1 WLR 1092, the only House of Lords decision on the section, supplies the test:

  • Unfairness ordinarily requires either a breach of the terms on which it was agreed the company’s affairs would be conducted, or the use of rules “in a manner which equity would regard as contrary to good faith”.
  • Equitable considerations of that kind arise most readily in a quasi-partnership: a company formed on the basis of a personal relationship involving mutual confidence, with an understanding that all or some of the members will participate in management, and with restrictions on the transfer of shares.
  • There is no “no-fault divorce”. A member who simply wants out, because trust has broken down and there is no market for his shares, has no remedy under s.994 absent some unfair conduct.
  • An offer to buy the petitioner’s shares at a fair value determined by an independent expert, with equality of access to information and provision for costs, will usually make it unfair for the petition to continue.

Conduct commonly held to be unfairly prejudicial:

  • Exclusion from management in a quasi-partnership, where there was an understanding of participation.
  • Diversion of the company’s business or opportunities to a competing entity controlled by the majority.
  • Payment of excessive remuneration to the controllers, coupled with a persistent refusal to pay dividends — the classic way of appropriating profit while starving the minority.
  • Serious mismanagement causing loss, though mere commercial misjudgment is not enough.
  • Improper allotment of shares to dilute the minority.
  • Breaches of the articles or of a shareholders’ agreement, where more than trivial.

Conduct usually held not to be:

  • A single or trivial breach causing no prejudice.
  • A genuine commercial decision the minority dislikes.
  • Non-payment of dividends where there is a proper commercial reason to retain profits.
  • Loss of trust and confidence without more, in a company that is not a quasi-partnership.

Remedies (s.996). The court may make “such order as it thinks fit”, and s.996(2) gives a non-exhaustive list: regulating the conduct of the company’s affairs in future; requiring the company to do or refrain from doing an act; authorising civil proceedings in the company’s name; restraining alterations to the articles without leave; and — overwhelmingly the usual order — requiring the purchase of the petitioner’s shares by other members or by the company itself, with a corresponding reduction of capital.

Valuation is where these cases are actually fought:

  • In a quasi-partnership where the petitioner has been unfairly excluded, the shares are normally valued pro rata, without a minority discount (Re Bird Precision Bellows Ltd [1986] Ch 658).
  • Where the company is not a quasi-partnership, or the petitioner acquired the shares as an investment, a discount for a minority holding may be applied.
  • The valuation date is at the court’s discretion, and is commonly the date of the order or the date of the unfair conduct, whichever better reflects the injustice.
Exam trap

Two errors recur. First, candidates treat every falling-out as unfair prejudice; O’Neill v Phillips says otherwise, and the discriminating fact is usually whether there was an agreed understanding (express or equitable) that has been broken. Second, candidates forget that a reasonable buy-out offer defeats the petition — if the majority has offered fair value determined by an independent expert, the right answer is usually that the petition should not proceed.

4.3.3 Derivative claims: ss.260–264 CA 2006

A derivative claim is brought by a member, on behalf of the company, in respect of a cause of action vested in the company, seeking relief on the company’s behalf. Any recovery goes to the company, not the claimant.

Scope (s.260(3)). The cause of action must arise from an actual or proposed act or omission involving negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust by a director. It may be brought against the director or another person, or both. It is available:

  • whether the cause of action arose before or after the claimant became a member (s.260(4)); and
  • against a former director, a shadow director, and (by s.260(5)) is not confined to breaches from which the director personally benefited — negligence alone suffices, which is a significant widening of the common law.

Permission is required, and there are two stages (s.261):

  1. Stage one, on the papers. The member applies for permission, with evidence. If the application and the evidence do not disclose a prima facie case, the court must dismiss it and may make any consequential order. No notice need be given to the company at this stage; the company takes no part.
  2. Stage two, at a hearing. If a prima facie case is shown, the court directs the company to provide evidence, and decides whether to give permission.

Mandatory refusal (s.263(2)). The court must refuse permission if satisfied that:

  • a person acting in accordance with s.172 (the duty to promote the success of the company) would not seek to continue the claim; or
  • the act or omission has been authorised by the company before it occurred; or
  • the act or omission has been ratified by the company since it occurred.

Discretionary factors (s.263(3)). Otherwise the court must take into account, in particular:

  • whether the member is acting in good faith;
  • the importance a person acting under s.172 would attach to continuing the claim;
  • where the act has not yet occurred, whether it would be likely to be authorised or ratified;
  • where it has occurred, whether it would be likely to be ratified;
  • whether the company has decided not to pursue the claim; and
  • whether the act gives rise to a cause of action the member could pursue in his own right.

Section 263(4): the court must have particular regard to the views of members who have no personal interest, direct or indirect, in the matter.

Exam trap

Choosing between the two remedies is the discrimination the examiner is usually after:

Derivative claim Unfair prejudice petition
Wrong done to The company The member, in their capacity as such
Who recovers The company Usually the petitioner, by a buy-out
Permission needed Yes, two stages No
Typical facts Director diverted an opportunity, or was negligent Exclusion from management; excessive pay; no dividends

If the client wants out at a fair price, the answer is s.994. If the client wants the company to recover what a director took, and to stay in the company, the answer is a derivative claim. And note that a s.994 petition may itself result in an order authorising proceedings in the company’s name under s.996(2)(c) — a route that avoids the s.261 permission filter.

4.3.4 Reflective loss

A shareholder cannot sue in their own name for a loss which is merely the reflection of a loss suffered by the company — a fall in the value of their shares, or in the dividends they would have received — where the company has its own claim.

  • Prudential Assurance Co Ltd v Newman Industries Ltd (No 2) [1982] Ch 204 established the rule.
  • Johnson v Gore Wood & Co [2002] 2 AC 1 confirmed and explained it.
  • Sevilleja v Marex Financial Ltd [2020] UKSC 31 narrowed it decisively: the rule is a bright line confined to claims by a shareholder, in that capacity, for loss reflective of the company’s loss. It does not bar claims by creditors, nor claims by a shareholder for a loss that is separate and distinct from the company’s.

4.3.5 Just and equitable winding up

Section 122(1)(g) IA 1986: a company may be wound up by the court if the court is of the opinion that it is just and equitable to do so. A contributory (a member) may petition, but must generally have held the shares for at least six of the last eighteen months (s.124(2)).

Ebrahimi v Westbourne Galleries Ltd [1973] AC 360 is the leading case, and the source of the quasi-partnership concept. Ebrahimi and Nazar traded as partners, incorporated the business, and later brought in Nazar’s son; the two of them then used their majority to remove Ebrahimi from the board under what is now s.168. The removal was lawful. The House of Lords nevertheless wound the company up: the statutory powers are subject to equitable considerations “which may make it unjust or inequitable to insist on legal rights”. Lord Wilberforce identified the typical elements — an association formed on a personal relationship of mutual confidence, an understanding that all or some members will participate in management, and restrictions on transfer so that a member cannot simply take his stake out and go.

Other established grounds: the substratum of the company has failed (its whole purpose has gone); the company was formed for a fraudulent purpose; there is complete deadlock; or there has been a justifiable loss of confidence in the management arising from a lack of probity.

Exam trap

Winding up is a remedy of last resort and destroys the value it is supposed to protect. Section 125(2) makes this explicit: where the petitioners are entitled to relief either by winding up or by some other remedy, the court must not make a winding-up order if it is of the opinion that some other remedy is available and that they are acting unreasonably in seeking winding up rather than pursuing it. Where the majority has offered to buy the minority out at a fair value, a winding-up petition will normally fail. In an exam, if s.994 would give the client what they want, s.994 is the answer.

4.3.6 Check yourself: minority protection

Q1. Three friends incorporated a company fifteen years ago. Each holds one third of the shares, and each has always been a director drawing an equal salary; there has never been a dividend, and the articles restrict transfers. Following a personal falling-out, the other two use their combined votes to remove the third from the board under s.168 and to stop her salary. She remains a shareholder. The removal complied in every respect with the statutory procedure.

Which of the following is the best advice?

A. She has no remedy, because the removal complied with s.168 and the majority is entitled to exercise its votes as it wishes.

B. She should bring a derivative claim, because the removal was a breach of the directors’ duties owed to the company.

C. She should petition under s.994: this has the hallmarks of a quasi-partnership with an understanding of participation in management, and her exclusion is likely to be unfairly prejudicial; the usual remedy is an order that her shares be bought at a value with no minority discount.

D. She should petition for a just and equitable winding up under s.122(1)(g), which is the primary remedy for exclusion from management.

E. She should sue the other two shareholders personally for the fall in the value of her shares.

Q2. A director of a company diverted a contract worth £900,000 to a business he controls. The board comprises that director and two others who are his appointees and have declined to take any action. A member holding 4% of the shares wishes to see the company recover the profit, and wishes to remain a shareholder.

Which of the following is the most appropriate course?

A. An unfair prejudice petition, because the member has been prejudiced by the loss in value of her shares.

B. A personal claim in the member’s own name for the diminution in the value of her shares.

C. An application for permission to bring a derivative claim under ss.260–264, since the cause of action is vested in the company and arises from a breach of duty by a director.

D. A petition for a just and equitable winding up.

E. Nothing, because a member holding less than 5% has no standing to bring proceedings.

Q3. A member petitions under s.994, complaining of exclusion from management in a quasi-partnership. Before the hearing, the majority shareholders offer in writing to purchase her shares at a price to be determined by an independent accountant as an expert, on a pro rata basis with no minority discount, with both sides having equal access to the company’s information, and offering to pay her costs to date.

What is the likely effect of the offer?

A. None; an offer to purchase cannot affect a petition once presented.

B. The court is likely to hold that it is no longer unfair for the petitioner to be excluded, and to strike out or dismiss the petition, because a fair offer of the remedy she would obtain has been made.

C. The offer is irrelevant unless it is accepted.

D. The offer converts the petition into a derivative claim.

E. The offer will be disregarded because the price is not fixed at the date of the offer.

Q4. A member applies for permission to continue a derivative claim. The company’s independent members, who have no personal interest in the matter, have resolved that pursuing the claim would harm the company’s commercial relationships and that it should not be brought. The alleged breach has not been ratified.

Which of the following best describes the court’s approach?

A. The court must grant permission, because the breach has not been ratified.

B. The court must refuse permission, because the members have decided not to pursue the claim.

C. The court must refuse permission if satisfied that a person acting in accordance with s.172 would not seek to continue the claim; otherwise it will weigh the discretionary factors in s.263(3), giving particular regard under s.263(4) to the views of members with no personal interest.

D. The court will grant permission only if the member holds at least 5% of the shares.

E. The court will grant permission automatically once a prima facie case is shown at the first stage.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. Every hallmark of a quasi-partnership identified in Ebrahimi is present: a company formed on a personal relationship, an understanding that all members participate in management, and restrictions on transfer. Removal that is lawful under s.168 can still be unfairly prejudicial, because equitable considerations may make it unjust to insist on strict legal rights (O’Neill v Phillips). The standard remedy under s.996 is a buy-out order, and in a quasi-partnership exclusion case the shares are normally valued pro rata without a minority discount (Re Bird Precision Bellows).

  • A states the Foss v Harbottle starting point and ignores the statutory exception.
  • B misidentifies the wrong: the harm is to her as a member, not to the company, so a derivative claim is the wrong vehicle.
  • D is available in principle (Ebrahimi itself was a winding-up case) but is a remedy of last resort; s.125(2) IA 1986 means a petitioner who could obtain a buy-out under s.994 and unreasonably insists on winding up will be refused.
  • E runs into the reflective loss principle: a fall in share value mirroring the company’s loss is not separately recoverable by a member (Prudential, Johnson v Gore Wood, Sevilleja v Marex).

Q2 — C. The cause of action — breach of the s.175 duty to avoid conflicts, and the exploitation of a corporate opportunity — is vested in the company. The client wants the company to recover and wants to stay in. That is the derivative claim, and s.260(3) covers it exactly. There is no minimum shareholding.

  • A would give her a buy-out, which is not what she wants, and the loss she has suffered is the company’s loss reflected in her shares.
  • B is barred by the reflective loss principle.
  • D destroys the company for no gain.
  • E invents a standing threshold. Any member may apply under s.260.

Q3 — B. O’Neill v Phillips. Where the majority offers to buy the petitioner’s shares at a fair value determined by a competent independent expert, on a pro rata basis in a quasi-partnership, with equality of information and provision for costs, it will usually cease to be unfair to exclude the petitioner from the company, and the petition should not proceed. The offer here has each of those features.

  • A and C deny the effect of a fair offer, which is the practical core of O’Neill.
  • D confuses the two remedies.
  • E misstates the requirement: a fair offer does not need a fixed price, and independent expert determination is precisely what makes it fair.

Q4 — C. Section 263(2)(a) requires mandatory refusal only where the court is satisfied that a person acting under s.172 would not seek to continue the claim. A decision by the company not to pursue is a discretionary factor under s.263(3)(e), and the views of disinterested members attract particular regard under s.263(4) — they are weighty, but not determinative.

  • A treats the absence of ratification as sufficient. It removes one mandatory bar, not the discretion.
  • B elevates a discretionary factor to a mandatory bar.
  • D invents a shareholding requirement.
  • E ignores the second stage: a prima facie case gets the applicant past s.261(2), no further.

4.4 Directors as stakeholders

Directors are stakeholders in their own right, and their interests can conflict with the company’s in ways the Act tries to manage.

Remuneration. Directors have no automatic right to be paid for acting as directors; the entitlement comes from the articles or a service contract. Model Article 19 permits the directors to undertake any services for the company and to be paid such remuneration as the directors determine — which is why a company controlled by its directors can pay them what it likes, and why excessive remuneration combined with a refusal to declare dividends is such a common s.994 complaint.

Service contracts. See Chapter 3: s.188 approval for terms over two years, s.228 inspection rights for members, and the preservation of contractual claims on removal by s.168(5).

The tension with s.172. A director appointed by, and beholden to, a controlling shareholder still owes the duty to promote the success of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole — including the minority — and must exercise independent judgment under s.173. A nominee who simply votes as instructed is in breach of both. The point recurs in group scenarios: a director of a subsidiary owes duties to the subsidiary, not to the parent, and a decision that benefits the group at the subsidiary’s expense is a breach.

Directors as employees. An executive director is usually also an employee, which gives them the protections of employment law and preferential status in an insolvency for limited amounts — but a non-executive director is not, and a director who is dismissed as an employee is not thereby removed from the board.

4.5 Creditors

Creditors have no rights under the constitution and no vote. Their leverage is contractual, and then statutory.

Contract and security. A creditor’s real protection is taken in advance: security over assets (Chapter 5), personal guarantees from the directors, retention of title, and covenants restricting what the borrower may do. A trade creditor with none of these is at the bottom of the priority order.

The creditor duty. As explained in Chapter 3, BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA [2022] UKSC 25 holds that when a company is insolvent or bordering on insolvency, or an insolvent liquidation or administration is probable, the directors’ s.172 duty is modified so that they must consider the interests of creditors, with increasing weight as insolvency approaches. Three points:

  • The duty is owed to the company, not to individual creditors. It is enforced by a liquidator or administrator after the event, not by a creditor suing directly.
  • It is not triggered by a mere “real risk” of insolvency at some future date.
  • Because it is a modification of s.172, breach is a breach of duty, with the ordinary consequences — and a director’s conduct on this front will also feature in any wrongful trading or disqualification claim.

Statutory protection. The clawback provisions (ss.238, 239, 245, 423 IA 1986), wrongful and fraudulent trading, and the priority rules are the creditors’ real remedies. They are dealt with in Chapter 7.

4.6 Employees

Employees are the stakeholder the Act mentions most and protects least, at least within company law:

  • Section 172(1)(b) requires directors to have regard to employees’ interests. It creates no right of action for employees.
  • Section 247 permits a company to make provision for employees on the cessation or transfer of its business — an express reversal of the old rule that such payments were beyond the company’s objects — exercisable by the directors if the articles allow, or by ordinary resolution.
  • On an insolvency, employees are ordinary preferential creditors for limited amounts: unpaid wages for the four months before the relevant date, capped per employee, accrued holiday pay, and certain pension contributions. Claims above the cap rank as unsecured. See Chapter 7.

4.7 Partners and LLP members

The stakeholder analysis for unincorporated and hybrid vehicles is different in kind, because the owners are the managers.

Rights. Under the PA 1890 defaults: to take part in management (s.24(5)); to an equal share of capital and profits (s.24(1)); to be indemnified for proper payments (s.24(2)); to 5% interest on advances beyond agreed capital (s.24(3)); to inspect the books (s.24(9)); to true accounts and full information (s.28); and to have partners account for private profits (s.29) and competing business (s.30).

No expulsion. Section 25 means a majority cannot expel a partner unless the agreement confers the power — the direct analogue of the rule that a shareholder cannot be removed. The same is true of an LLP member under the default regulations.

Exit. A partner in a partnership at will may dissolve it by notice (s.26); an LLP member may resign on reasonable notice but cannot dissolve the LLP.

On dissolution. Section 44 PA 1890 sets the order in which assets are applied: first, losses, including losses of capital, are paid out of profits, then out of capital, and then by the partners individually in the proportion in which they share profits. The assets are then applied: (1) in paying the debts and liabilities to non-partners; (2) in repaying to each partner rateably what is due for advances; (3) in repaying rateably what is due in respect of capital; and (4) the residue is divided in the profit-sharing ratio.

Minority protection. Section 994 CA 2006 is applied to LLPs by regulation, but the members may exclude it by unanimous written agreement — an option not available to company shareholders. For a general partnership there is no s.994 equivalent: the remedies are the agreement, dissolution, and a court order under s.35 PA 1890 (permanent incapacity, conduct prejudicial to the business, wilful or persistent breach, carrying on only at a loss, or just and equitable).

Exam trap

Section 44 is the provision that produces the counter-intuitive result. Where a partnership dissolves with a capital deficiency, the loss of capital is a loss like any other, borne in the profit-sharing ratio — so a partner who contributed nothing to capital may still have to contribute to making good the loss of a partner who did. Candidates assume that a partner who put in no capital cannot lose any.

4.8 The solicitor’s position

Acting for the company when instructed by the majority. A solicitor instructed by the company must remember that the company is the client, and that the majority shareholder is not. Advice on how to exclude the minority, or on how to structure a transaction that benefits the majority at the company’s expense, is advice to the majority — and if the solicitor is also acting for the company there is a conflict.

Acting for two shareholders in dispute. Where two clients’ interests conflict, or there is a significant risk that they will, the solicitor must not act for both. The narrow exceptions — substantially common interest, or competing for the same objective — require informed written consent, effective safeguards and a reasonable belief that acting is in both clients’ best interests. A falling-out between shareholders is very rarely a substantially common interest.

Confidentiality across a group. Information obtained while acting for one company in a group is confidential to that company. Passing it to the parent without authority breaches the duty of confidentiality, even where the parent owns 100% of the shares.

4.9 Chapter review

Q1. A shareholder holds 26% of the shares in a private company. The other 74% is held by the managing director.

Which of the following can the minority shareholder do without the majority’s cooperation?

A. Remove the managing director from the board.

B. Amend the articles to introduce a pre-emption right on transfer.

C. Block a special resolution, require the directors to call a general meeting, require an audit, and petition under s.994.

D. Compel the payment of a dividend.

E. Compel the company to buy back her shares.

Q2. In a partnership dissolution, after all outside creditors have been paid, the remaining assets are £120,000. Partner X is owed £30,000 in respect of an advance made to the firm; X’s capital account stands at £150,000 and Y’s at £50,000. X and Y share profits equally.

Applying s.44 PA 1890, which of the following is correct?

A. The £120,000 is divided equally between X and Y.

B. X is repaid the £30,000 advance first; the remaining £90,000 is applied towards capital of £200,000, leaving a capital deficiency of £110,000, which is a loss borne equally, so each partner bears £55,000.

C. The £120,000 is applied first to capital in proportion to the capital accounts, and X’s advance ranks last.

D. X is repaid the £30,000 and the balance of £90,000 is paid to X in part satisfaction of capital, because X’s capital contribution was larger.

E. The £120,000 is retained by the firm until the partners agree its distribution.

Q3. A shareholder in a company that is not a quasi-partnership is dissatisfied with the board’s strategy, which she considers commercially misguided. The company is profitable, dividends are paid, and there is no breach of the articles or of any agreement. She wishes to exit but there is no market for her shares.

Which of the following is the best advice?

A. She may petition under s.994, because the absence of a market for her shares is itself unfairly prejudicial.

B. She may petition for a just and equitable winding up, because there has been an irretrievable breakdown in her relationship with the board.

C. She has no remedy on these facts: there is no “no-fault divorce” under s.994, disagreement with a genuine commercial decision is not unfair prejudice, and winding up is a remedy of last resort.

D. She may bring a derivative claim in respect of the board’s strategic decisions.

E. She may require the company to purchase her shares under s.994 as of right.

Q4. A creditor of a company believes that the directors continued to trade and paid a large dividend at a time when insolvent administration was probable. The company is now in administration.

Which of the following is correct?

A. The creditor may sue the directors directly for breach of the duty owed to creditors under s.172(3).

B. The creditor has no remedy of any kind, because directors’ duties are owed only to the company.

C. The creditor cannot sue on the duty directly, because it is a modification of the duty owed to the company; it is for the administrator to pursue the breach, and the administrator may also consider wrongful trading and the clawback provisions.

D. The creditor may petition under s.994.

E. The creditor may bring a derivative claim under s.260.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — C. A 26% holding blocks a special resolution (more than 25% is needed), exceeds the 5% threshold for requiring a general meeting under s.303, exceeds the 10% threshold for requiring an audit under s.476, and — like any single share — supports a s.994 petition.

  • A requires an ordinary resolution, which she cannot pass.
  • B requires a special resolution, which she cannot pass.
  • D is not within any shareholder’s power: a final dividend is recommended by the directors and declared by ordinary resolution, and cannot exceed the recommendation.
  • E is not a right; a buy-out follows only from an order under s.996 or an agreement.

Q2 — B. Section 44(b) sets the order: outside creditors first, then advances, then capital, then the residue in the profit-sharing ratio. X’s £30,000 advance is repaid before any capital. The remaining £90,000 is insufficient to repay £200,000 of capital, leaving a deficiency of £110,000. Under s.44(a) a loss of capital is a loss, borne by the partners in the proportions in which they share profits — equally here, so £55,000 each.

  • A ignores both the priority of advances and the capital accounts.
  • C inverts the order of advances and capital.
  • D invents a preference for the larger contributor.
  • E describes no rule.

Q3 — C. O’Neill v Phillips: there is no no-fault divorce under s.994, and unfairness ordinarily requires a breach of the agreed terms or use of the rules contrary to good faith. Disagreement with a genuine commercial decision, in a company that is not a quasi-partnership and is complying with its constitution, is neither unfair nor a ground for winding up; and s.125(2) IA 1986 makes winding up a last resort.

  • A is precisely the argument O’Neill rejected.
  • B applies quasi-partnership reasoning where the facts exclude it.
  • D requires a cause of action vested in the company arising from a director’s breach of duty; a strategic decision within the board’s powers is not one.
  • E treats a s.994 remedy as an entitlement rather than a discretionary order following proof of unfair prejudice.

Q4 — C. BTI 2014 LLC v Sequana SA. The creditor duty is not owed to creditors individually; it is a modification of the s.172 duty owed to the company, enforced by the office-holder. The administrator may pursue breach of duty, and should also consider wrongful trading under s.214 IA 1986 and the clawback provisions in ss.238, 239 and 423.

  • A is the recurring structural error.
  • B over-corrects: the creditor’s interests are protected through the office-holder’s claims and the insolvency regime.
  • D is not open to a creditor; s.994 is a member’s remedy.
  • E is not open to a creditor either; s.260 requires the claimant to be a member.
