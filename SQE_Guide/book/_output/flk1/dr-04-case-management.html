12  Running the claim

Assessment objective 5: case management and any interim applications relevant to a claim.

12.1 The overriding objective

CPR 1.1(1): the Rules are a new procedural code with the overriding objective of enabling the court to deal with cases justly and at proportionate cost.

CPR 1.1(2): dealing with a case justly and at proportionate cost includes, so far as is practicable:

  1. ensuring that the parties are on an equal footing and can participate fully in proceedings, and that parties and witnesses can give their best evidence;
  2. saving expense;
  3. dealing with the case in ways which are proportionate to the amount of money involved, the importance of the case, the complexity of the issues, and the financial position of each party;
  4. ensuring that it is dealt with expeditiously and fairly;
  5. allotting to it an appropriate share of the court’s resources, while taking into account the need to allot resources to other cases;
  6. promoting or using alternative dispute resolution; and
  7. enforcing compliance with rules, practice directions and orders.

CPR 1.2 requires the court to give effect to the overriding objective when it exercises any power or interprets any rule. CPR 1.3 imposes a duty on the parties to help the court further it. CPR 1.4 requires the court to further the objective by actively managing cases, which includes encouraging co-operation, identifying the issues at an early stage, deciding promptly which issues need full investigation, fixing timetables, and — under CPR 1.4(2)(e) — “ordering or encouraging the parties to use, and facilitating the use of, alternative dispute resolution”.

The ADR amendments of 1 October 2024

Sub-paragraph 1.1(2)(f) and the words “ordering or” in 1.4(2)(e) were inserted with effect from 1 October 2024, following Churchill v Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council [2023] EWCA Civ 1416. They confirm that the court may order parties to engage in a non-court dispute resolution process, not merely encourage them. If your examinable law cut-off falls before 1 October 2024, the older wording applies — but for any current sitting these are the operative provisions.

12.2 Allocation to a track

12.2.1 The directions questionnaire

Once a defence is filed, the court provisionally allocates the claim and sends the parties a directions questionnaire (form N180 for the small claims track, N181 for the others). It must be filed by the date specified. The questionnaire asks about settlement and ADR, the track the party considers appropriate, disclosure, witnesses, experts, trial length and availability.

The court may order a stay of up to one month to allow for settlement (CPR 26.5), and in most small claims a referral to the Small Claims Mediation Service now follows automatically (CPR 26.6).

12.2.2 The four tracks

CPR 26.9 sets the scope of each track.

Track Value Key features
Small claims Not more than £10,000 — but personal injury only where general damages for pain, suffering and loss of amenity are not more than £1,000, and RTA whiplash claims where those damages are not more than £5,000 Informal hearing; strictly limited costs (CPR 27.14); compulsory mediation for most money claims
Fast track More than £10,000 but not more than £25,000 Trial of one day or less; oral expert evidence limited to one expert per party per field, in no more than two fields; complexity bands 1–4; fixed recoverable costs
Intermediate track More than £25,000 but not more than £100,000 Trial of not more than three days; oral expert evidence likely limited to two experts per party; ordinarily no more than three parties; complexity bands 1–4; fixed recoverable costs
Multi-track More than £100,000, or otherwise unsuitable for a lower track Case management conference; costs budgeting; assessed rather than fixed costs

The intermediate track was introduced on 1 October 2023 alongside the extension of fixed recoverable costs. Both the fast and intermediate tracks are further assigned to a complexity band (1 to 4), which determines the level of fixed costs — band 1 being the least complex.

12.2.3 The factors

CPR 26.13(1) lists the matters the court considers when allocating:

  • the financial value of the claim;
  • the nature of the remedy sought;
  • the likely complexity of the facts, law or evidence;
  • the number of parties;
  • the value of any counterclaim and its complexity;
  • the amount of oral evidence likely to be required;
  • the importance of the claim to persons who are not parties;
  • the views expressed by the parties; and
  • the circumstances of the parties.
Exam trap

Value is the starting point, not the answer. A £15,000 claim raising a novel point of law, involving five parties and requiring three days of evidence will be allocated to the multi-track despite its value. Conversely the parties’ agreement does not bind the court: CPR 26.13 lists their views as one factor among nine.

Note also how personal injury value is measured on the small claims track. It is the general damages for pain, suffering and loss of amenity that must not exceed £1,000 (or £5,000 for RTA whiplash) — not the total claim. A claim worth £8,000 in total but £2,000 in general damages is not a small claim.

12.3 Directions and case management conferences

12.3.1 Fast and intermediate tracks

The court gives standard directions without a hearing wherever possible. A typical fast track timetable, running from allocation, is:

Step Typical timing
Disclosure by list 4 weeks
Inspection 5 weeks
Exchange of witness statements 10 weeks
Exchange of expert reports 14 weeks
Sending of pre-trial checklists 20 weeks
Filing of pre-trial checklists 22 weeks
Trial 30 weeks

The intermediate track has its own standard directions with a similar shape, extended to reflect a trial of up to three days.

12.3.2 Multi-track

The court will either give directions and fix a timetable, or fix a case management conference (CMC) or, where costs budgets are to be considered, a costs and case management conference (CCMC). At a CMC the court will review the statements of case, identify the issues, consider whether the claim can be resolved without trial, decide on disclosure, witness and expert evidence, and fix a trial window.

12.3.3 Costs budgets

CPR 3.12 applies costs management to all Part 7 multi-track claims, with exceptions — notably where the claim is valued at £10 million or more and certain claims involving children.

CPR 3.13: every party except a litigant in person must file and exchange a budget in Precedent H:

  • where the stated value of the claim is less than £50,000, with the directions questionnaire;
  • in any other case, not later than 21 days before the first case management conference.

Budget discussion reports must be filed not later than 7 days before the CCMC.

CPR 3.14 — the sanction. A party that fails to file a budget when required will be treated as having filed a budget comprising only the applicable court fees.

The court then makes a costs management order recording the extent to which budgets are agreed or, where not agreed, the figures it approves. Once made, the court will not depart from an approved or agreed budget on assessment unless satisfied that there is good reason to do so — which is what makes budgeting so consequential.

Exam trap

CPR 3.14 is draconian and is examined for that reason. The defaulting party is not merely criticised; it is limited to court fees, and recovering anything more requires relief from sanctions under CPR 3.9 on the Denton test. The rule applies to a failure to file a budget at all, or to file it in time; a budget that is filed late but before the CCMC still engages it, and relief must be sought.

12.4 Sanctions and relief from sanctions

CPR 3.8: where a party has failed to comply with a rule, practice direction or court order, any sanction imposed for that failure has effect unless the party in default applies for and obtains relief.

Sanctions may be built into the rules (as with CPR 3.14), or imposed by order — most commonly an unless order, which provides that unless a step is taken by a stated date, a specified consequence follows automatically.

CPR 3.9(1): on an application for relief, the court will consider all the circumstances of the case, so as to enable it to deal justly with the application, including the need:

  1. for litigation to be conducted efficiently and at proportionate cost; and
  2. to enforce compliance with rules, practice directions and orders.

12.4.1 The Denton test

Denton v TH White Ltd [2014] EWCA Civ 906 supplies a three-stage approach, and it should be recited in that order:

  1. Identify and assess the seriousness and significance of the breach. If the breach is neither serious nor significant, relief will usually be granted and the court need not spend much time on stages two and three.
  2. Consider why the default occurred. Is there a good reason for it? Overlooking a deadline, pressure of work, and a solicitor’s oversight are not good reasons; a sudden illness or a debilitating event may be.
  3. Evaluate all the circumstances of the case, so as to deal justly with the application, giving particular weight to the two factors in CPR 3.9(1)(a) and (b).

Denton corrected the over-strict application that followed Mitchell v News Group Newspapers Ltd [2013] EWCA Civ 1537, and the Court of Appeal took the opportunity to warn that opportunistic opposition to reasonable relief applications would itself be penalised in costs.

Exam trap

The two factors in CPR 3.9(1) are given particular weight at stage three, but they are not paramount and they are not a trump. An answer that says relief must be refused because the need to enforce compliance is decisive misstates Denton. Equally, an answer that treats stage three as a free-ranging discretion in which all factors weigh equally misstates it in the other direction.

12.5 Striking out

CPR 3.4(2) — the court may strike out a statement of case if it appears:

  1. that the statement of case discloses no reasonable grounds for bringing or defending the claim;
  2. that it is an abuse of the court’s process or is otherwise likely to obstruct the just disposal of the proceedings;
  3. that there has been a failure to comply with a rule, practice direction or court order; or
  4. that the claim is a SLAPP claim and the claimant has failed to show that it is more likely than not that the claim would succeed at trial.
The SLAPP strike-out — verify against your cut-off

Ground (d) implements ss.194–195 of the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023, which came into force on 18 June 2025, together with a corresponding amendment to the court’s costs discretion in CPR 44.2. A claim is a SLAPP for these purposes where it restrains, or is intended to restrain, freedom of speech; the speech concerns information relating to economic crime; the publication is for a purpose connected with the public interest in combating economic crime; and the claimant’s conduct is intended to cause harassment, alarm, distress, expense or other harm beyond that ordinarily encountered in properly conducted litigation.

The SRA’s warning notice on SLAPPs is named expressly in the assessment specification, so the conduct dimension — a solicitor must not pursue litigation whose purpose is to silence rather than to vindicate a legal right — is as likely to be examined as the rule itself.

Striking out is a draconian remedy and the court will consider whether a lesser sanction, or an opportunity to amend, would meet the case.

12.6 Interim applications

12.6.1 The procedure: Part 23

  • The application is made on form N244, stating what order is sought and why (CPR 23.6), and is supported by evidence — normally a witness statement, though the application notice itself may be verified by a statement of truth and stand as evidence.
  • It must be served as soon as practicable after issue and, unless a rule provides otherwise, at least three clear days before the hearing (CPR 23.7).
  • Without notice applications are permitted where there is exceptional urgency, where the overriding objective is best furthered by doing so, by consent, with the court’s permission, where a rule permits it, or where giving notice would defeat the purpose of the application — the freezing injunction being the obvious case. An applicant proceeding without notice owes a duty of full and frank disclosure of everything material, including matters adverse to their case; the respondent may apply to set aside within seven days of service of the order.
  • The court may deal with an application without a hearing if the parties agree, or if the court does not consider a hearing appropriate.
  • Costs of an interim application are normally summarily assessed at the end of the hearing.

12.6.2 Summary judgment: Part 24

The test (CPR 24.3). The court may give summary judgment against a claimant or a defendant on the whole of a claim or on a particular issue if:

  • the party has no real prospect of succeeding on the claim, defence or issue; and
  • there is no other compelling reason why the case or issue should be disposed of at a trial.

Both limbs are required. A “compelling reason” might be that the claim raises a point of general importance, that further evidence is expected to become available, or that a related claim is to be tried at the same time.

Swain v Hillman [2001] 1 All ER 91: “real” means realistic as opposed to fanciful. The court must not conduct a mini-trial on the documents. Easyair Ltd v Opal Telecom Ltd [2009] EWHC 339 (Ch) collects the principles: the court considers the evidence that can reasonably be expected to be available at trial, and should hesitate to decide a short point of law only where the facts are uncertain or the area of law is developing.

Procedure. A claimant may not apply until the defendant has filed an acknowledgment of service or a defence, unless the court gives permission (CPR 24.4). The respondent must have at least 14 days’ notice of the hearing and of the issues to be decided; the respondent’s evidence must be filed and served at least 7 days before the hearing; and any evidence in reply at least 3 days before.

The possible orders: judgment on the claim; striking out or dismissal of the claim; dismissal of the application; or a conditional order — permitting a party to continue only if it pays money into court or takes a specified step, appropriate where the case is improbable but not fanciful.

Exam trap

Distinguish the three ways of disposing of a case early:

Application Question asked
Strike out (CPR 3.4(2)(a)) Taking the pleaded facts at their highest and assuming they are true, do they disclose a legally recognisable claim?
Summary judgment (CPR 24.3) Looking at the evidence, does the party have a real prospect of success?
Default judgment (Part 12) Has the defendant simply failed to respond in time?

A defective pleading points to strike out; a hopeless case on the evidence points to summary judgment; silence points to default judgment. Applications are commonly made in the alternative.

12.6.3 Interim payments

CPR 25.23 sets out the conditions. The court may order an interim payment only where:

  • the defendant has admitted liability to pay damages or some other sum of money to the claimant;
  • the claimant has obtained judgment against the defendant for damages to be assessed or for a sum of money to be assessed;
  • the court is satisfied that, if the claim went to trial, the claimant would obtain judgment for a substantial amount of money against the defendant from whom the payment is sought;
  • in a possession claim, the defendant would be held liable to pay a sum for the defendant’s occupation and use of the land; or
  • there are two or more defendants and the court is satisfied that, if the claim went to trial, the claimant would obtain judgment for a substantial amount of money against at least one of them — subject to conditions about insurance and public funding.

The restriction (CPR 25.20). The court must not order an interim payment of more than a reasonable proportion of the likely amount of the final judgment, and must take into account contributory negligence and any relevant set-off or counterclaim.

The application may not be made before the end of the period for filing an acknowledgment of service, and the fact that an interim payment has been made is not to be disclosed to the trial judge until all questions of liability and quantum have been decided.

12.6.4 Interim injunctions

An interim injunction is a discretionary equitable remedy granted to hold the position until trial. The governing framework is American Cyanamid Co v Ethicon Ltd [1975] AC 396:

  1. Is there a serious question to be tried? This is a low threshold — the claim must not be frivolous or vexatious. The court does not weigh the relative strength of the cases at this stage.
  2. Would damages be an adequate remedy for the claimant if the injunction were refused and the claimant succeeded at trial? If so, no injunction.
  3. If not, would the cross-undertaking in damages adequately compensate the defendant if the injunction were granted and the claimant lost? If so, an injunction is more likely.
  4. If damages would not adequately compensate either side, where does the balance of convenience lie?
  5. If the balance is even, take the course that preserves the status quo.

The applicant must almost always give a cross-undertaking in damages — an undertaking to the court to compensate the respondent for loss caused by the injunction if it turns out to have been wrongly granted. The court will want evidence of the applicant’s ability to honour it.

Modifications to American Cyanamid arise where:

  • the injunction would in practice dispose of the whole action (for example a restrictive covenant with a short remaining term), where the court will look at the merits more closely;
  • the application is for a mandatory injunction, requiring a higher degree of assurance that the claimant will succeed; or
  • the application affects freedom of expression, where s.12(3) Human Rights Act 1998 requires the claimant to establish that it is likely to succeed at trial.

Freezing injunctions (CPR 25.12–25.14) are a distinct species. The applicant must show a good arguable case on the merits, that the respondent has assets, and a real risk of dissipation of those assets — not merely that the respondent may become unable to pay. They are almost always sought without notice, engaging the duty of full and frank disclosure, and the order includes an exception for ordinary living and legal expenses. A freezing order does not give the applicant any security or priority over the frozen assets.

Search orders (CPR 25.15–25.19) permit entry to premises to search for and preserve evidence. They require an extremely strong prima facie case, very serious potential or actual damage, clear evidence that the respondent has incriminating material, and a real possibility that it will be destroyed. A supervising solicitor, independent of the applicant’s firm, must be appointed to serve and oversee execution of the order.

12.7 Check yourself

Q1. A claimant brings a claim for £22,000 for breach of a building contract. Liability is disputed on straightforward facts, the trial is expected to take one day, and each party wants to call one expert surveyor. There are two parties.

To which track will the claim be allocated?

A. The small claims track, because the dispute is straightforward.

B. The fast track, because the value is more than £10,000 but not more than £25,000, the trial will last one day, and expert evidence is within the permitted limits.

C. The intermediate track, because expert evidence is required.

D. The multi-track, because the claim involves expert evidence.

E. The fast track, but only if both parties agree.

Q2. A defendant in a multi-track claim worth £600,000 fails to file a costs budget before the CCMC. Its solicitor had diarised the wrong date.

What is the consequence, and what should the defendant do?

A. There is no consequence; the court will simply require the budget to be filed late.

B. The claim will be struck out under CPR 3.4(2)(c).

C. The defendant is treated as having filed a budget comprising only the applicable court fees (CPR 3.14), and must apply for relief from sanctions under CPR 3.9, on which the court will apply the three-stage test in Denton v TH White Ltd.

D. The court will disallow the defendant’s costs entirely and no relief is available.

E. The consequence applies only if the defendant loses at trial.

Q3. A defendant applies for summary judgment. The claimant’s case depends on an oral agreement which the defendant denies was ever made. The claimant has produced a witness statement from a person who says he was present when the agreement was made; the defendant has produced three statements to the contrary and contemporaneous documents that are inconsistent with the claimant’s account.

Which of the following is the most likely outcome?

A. Summary judgment will be granted, because the weight of the evidence favours the defendant.

B. Summary judgment will be refused, because there is a conflict of factual evidence which can only be resolved by hearing the witnesses; the court must not conduct a mini-trial, and the claimant’s case is realistic rather than fanciful.

C. Summary judgment will be granted, because an oral agreement can never be proved.

D. The court will strike out the claim under CPR 3.4(2)(a).

E. The court will enter default judgment for the defendant.

Q4. A claimant seeks an interim injunction to restrain a former employee from working for a competitor, in breach of a restrictive covenant with eleven months left to run. Trial is unlikely to take place within that period.

Which of the following best describes the court’s approach?

A. The court will apply American Cyanamid without modification, and will not consider the merits.

B. The court will grant the injunction automatically, because a covenant is a contractual right.

C. Because the injunction would in practice dispose of the whole action — trial will come too late to matter — the court will look more closely at the merits than the American Cyanamid threshold would normally require; the claimant will also be required to give a cross-undertaking in damages.

D. Interim injunctions are not available in employment cases.

E. The court will refuse the injunction because damages are always an adequate remedy for breach of a restrictive covenant.

Q5. A party misses a deadline for exchanging witness statements by two days because its solicitor was hospitalised unexpectedly. The other party has suffered no prejudice, and the trial date is not affected. The other party opposes the application for relief on the ground that the need to enforce compliance is paramount.

Which of the following is correct?

A. Relief must be refused, because CPR 3.9(1)(b) requires the court to enforce compliance.

B. Relief will be granted: applying Denton, the breach is neither serious nor significant, there is a good reason for it, and all the circumstances favour relief. The opposing party risks a costs sanction for taking an opportunistic point.

C. Relief may be granted only if the party pays the other side’s costs of the entire action.

D. Relief is unavailable because CPR 3.8 provides that sanctions have effect.

E. The court has no discretion where a rule imposes an automatic sanction.

Answers and explanations

Q1 — B. CPR 26.9: the fast track covers claims of more than £10,000 but not more than £25,000, where the trial is likely to last no longer than one day and oral expert evidence is limited to one expert per party in each of no more than two fields. Every element is satisfied.

  • A applies the small claims limit of £10,000.
  • C and D treat expert evidence as pushing the claim up a track. It does not; the fast track expressly contemplates one expert per party.
  • E makes allocation depend on the parties’ agreement. Under CPR 26.13 their views are one of nine factors, and the decision is the court’s.

Q2 — C. CPR 3.14 provides that a party that fails to file a budget when required will be treated as having filed a budget comprising only the applicable court fees. The remedy is an application for relief from sanctions under CPR 3.9, decided on the three-stage Denton test. A diary error is unlikely to be a good reason at stage two, so the defendant’s prospects turn on stages one and three.

  • A ignores the automatic sanction.
  • B applies the wrong rule; strike out is not the sanction for a budget failure.
  • D overstates the position: relief is available, however difficult.
  • E invents a condition; the sanction bites on assessment whatever the outcome.

Q3 — B. Swain v Hillman: “real” prospect means realistic, not fanciful, and the court must not conduct a mini-trial. A genuine conflict of oral evidence, supported on the claimant’s side by a witness who says he was present, is quintessentially a matter for trial, however unpromising the documents look.

  • A invites the court to weigh evidence, which is precisely what Part 24 does not permit.
  • C is legally wrong: oral contracts are enforceable and provable.
  • D applies the wrong test. Strike out under CPR 3.4(2)(a) asks whether the pleaded case discloses reasonable grounds, assuming the facts to be true — and here it does.
  • E misuses default judgment, which is about failure to respond in time.

Q4 — C. Where the practical effect of granting or refusing interim relief is to determine the action — because the covenant will have expired before trial — the court departs from the strict American Cyanamid approach and takes a view on the merits, since the “serious question to be tried” threshold would otherwise decide the case without any assessment of it. A cross-undertaking in damages will be required as a matter of course.

  • A applies American Cyanamid mechanically and ignores the recognised exception.
  • B and D state rules that do not exist.
  • E is too absolute. Damages are frequently an inadequate remedy for breach of a restrictive covenant, because the loss of confidential information or client connection is hard to quantify.

Q5 — B. Denton v TH White Ltd. Stage one: a two-day delay causing no prejudice and not imperilling the trial date is neither serious nor significant, and where that is so relief will usually be granted without much need for stages two and three. Stage two: a solicitor’s sudden hospitalisation is capable of being a good reason. Stage three: all the circumstances plainly favour relief. The Court of Appeal in Denton expressly warned that parties who unreasonably oppose relief applications of this kind should expect to be penalised in costs.

  • A treats CPR 3.9(1)(b) as paramount. It is given particular weight at stage three, not decisive effect.
  • C invents a condition out of all proportion to the breach.
  • D misreads CPR 3.8, which provides that a sanction has effect unless relief is obtained — the rule that makes relief applications necessary, not one that makes them impossible.
  • E denies the existence of CPR 3.9.
