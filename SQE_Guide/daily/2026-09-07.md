## Questions

**1.** A circuit judge sitting in the county court is hearing a contract claim. Counsel cites a
2019 decision of a High Court judge sitting at first instance, and a 2023 decision of the
Divisional Court of the King's Bench Division. The two decisions conflict.

Which of the following best describes the circuit judge's position?

- **A** — She is bound by the High Court decision but not the Divisional Court decision.
- **B** — She is bound by the Divisional Court decision and should follow it.
- **C** — She is bound by neither, as county court judges are bound only by the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court.
- **D** — She is bound by both and must refer the conflict to the Court of Appeal.
- **E** — She is bound by the later decision in time, whichever court gave it.

**2.** A solicitor holding a current practising certificate, but no higher rights of audience
qualification, wishes to represent a client personally at a trial.

In which of the following can she exercise a right of audience?

- **A** — A trial in the Crown Court following committal for sentence.
- **B** — A trial in the King's Bench Division of the High Court.
- **C** — A summary trial in the magistrates' court.
- **D** — An appeal in the Court of Appeal, Civil Division.
- **E** — A trial in the Crown Court where the client is unrepresented and consents.

**3.** The Court of Appeal (Civil Division) is hearing an appeal. It is faced with one of its own
decisions from 2015 which it considers was reached without the court having been referred to a
relevant statutory provision that was in force at the time.

May the Court of Appeal decline to follow its 2015 decision?

- **A** — No. The Court of Appeal is absolutely bound by its own previous decisions.
- **B** — No, unless the Supreme Court has since overruled the 2015 decision.
- **C** — Yes, because the decision was given per incuriam.
- **D** — Yes, but only if the appellant's liberty is at stake.
- **E** — Yes, because the Court of Appeal has an unfettered discretion to depart from its own decisions.

**4.** In the course of giving judgment, a Supreme Court Justice states that had the claimant been
a minor rather than an adult, the outcome would have been different. The claimant in the case
before the court was an adult.

What is the status of that statement in a later case involving a minor?

- **A** — It forms part of the ratio decidendi and binds all lower courts.
- **B** — It is obiter dictum and is persuasive only.
- **C** — It is obiter dictum and must be disregarded entirely.
- **D** — It binds the Court of Appeal but not the High Court.
- **E** — It becomes binding only if a later Supreme Court decision adopts it.

**5.** A High Court judge sitting at first instance is considering a point of law decided by
another High Court judge at first instance three years earlier. The judge considers the earlier
reasoning to be clearly wrong.

What should the judge do?

- **A** — Follow the earlier decision; High Court judges bind each other at first instance.
- **B** — Decline to follow it; the earlier decision is persuasive but not binding.
- **C** — Refer the point to the Court of Appeal before proceeding.
- **D** — Follow the earlier decision unless it was given per incuriam.
- **E** — Decline to follow it only if the parties consent.

**6.** Which of the following best describes the effect of the 1966 Practice Statement?

- **A** — It permits the Court of Appeal to depart from its own previous decisions where justice requires.
- **B** — It permits the House of Lords, and now the Supreme Court, to depart from its own previous decisions when it appears right to do so.
- **C** — It abolished the doctrine of binding precedent in civil cases.
- **D** — It permits any appellate court to depart from precedent where the decision was given per incuriam.
- **E** — It requires the Supreme Court to follow decisions of the European Court of Human Rights.

**7.** A district judge in the county court gives a final decision in a Part 7 claim allocated to
the fast track. The losing party wishes to appeal.

To which court or judge does the appeal lie?

- **A** — A circuit judge in the county court.
- **B** — A High Court judge.
- **C** — The Court of Appeal, Civil Division.
- **D** — The Divisional Court of the King's Bench Division.
- **E** — Directly to the Supreme Court under the leapfrog procedure.

**8.** A solicitor is instructed in a matter that turns on a complex point of pensions law. She has
no experience in the area and no colleague in the firm practises in it. The client is anxious for
the firm to act and offers to pay a substantially increased fee.

What should the solicitor do?

- **A** — Accept the instructions, as the client has given informed consent to her lack of experience.
- **B** — Accept the instructions and instruct counsel, charging the client for both.
- **C** — Decline to act, or take steps to ensure the service is competent, because she must provide a competent service.
- **D** — Accept the instructions, as a solicitor must act in the best interests of each client and the client wishes her to act.
- **E** — Accept the instructions provided she notifies her professional indemnity insurers.

**9.** A judge is invited to distinguish a binding Court of Appeal authority.

Which of the following would properly justify distinguishing it?

- **A** — The judge considers the earlier decision to have been wrongly reasoned.
- **B** — The material facts of the present case differ from those of the earlier case in a legally relevant way.
- **C** — The earlier decision is more than twenty years old.
- **D** — Academic commentary has criticised the earlier decision.
- **E** — The earlier decision was reached by a majority rather than unanimously.

**10.** Counsel for the defendant in a Crown Court trial realises mid-hearing that the authority

Which of the following best reflects her obligation?

- **A** — She must say nothing, as her duty is to act in the best interests of her client.
- **B** — She must correct the position, because she must not mislead the court and her duty to the court prevails.
- **C** — She should seek the client's instructions and act on them.
- **D** — She may remain silent provided she does not repeat the citation.
- **E** — She must withdraw from the case immediately.

## Answers and explanations

**1 — B.** Divisional Court decisions bind judges of the High Court sitting at first instance and
bind the county court. A decision of a single High Court judge at first instance binds no one; it
is persuasive only. **A** inverts the hierarchy. **C** is wrong: the county court is bound by the
High Court and Divisional Court as well as the appellate courts. **D** invents a referral
procedure that does not exist. **E** is a plausible-sounding rule that is not the law — seniority
of court governs, not chronology.

**2 — C.** Solicitors with a practising certificate have automatic rights of audience in the
magistrates' court and the county court. Rights of audience are a reserved legal activity under
the Legal Services Act 2007. **A**, **B**, **D** and **E** all require a higher rights of audience
qualification; the client's consent in **E** is irrelevant, as the restriction protects the
administration of justice, not just the client.

**3 — C.** *Young v Bristol Aeroplane Co Ltd* [1944] KB 718 gives the Civil Division three
exceptions: conflicting previous Court of Appeal decisions; a previous decision inconsistent with
a later House of Lords or Supreme Court decision; and a decision given *per incuriam* — reached in
ignorance of a relevant statute or binding authority. That is exactly this case. **A** overstates
the rule. **B** describes only the second exception. **D** describes the *additional* latitude the
Criminal Division has, not the Civil Division. **E** is far too wide.

**4 — B.** The statement is not necessary to the decision on the facts before the court, so it is
obiter dictum. Obiter from the Supreme Court is highly persuasive and often followed, but it does
not bind. **A** misidentifies the ratio. **C** is the trap: persuasive is not the same as
worthless. **D** invents a split-status rule. **E** is wrong — its persuasive force exists now.

**5 — B.** High Court judges at first instance do not bind one another. Judicial comity means the
later judge will usually follow the earlier decision for consistency, but where she is convinced
it is wrong she may decline to follow it. **A** states the opposite. **D** imports the *per
incuriam* exception, which is about courts bound by their own decisions — irrelevant here.

**6 — B.** The Practice Statement (Judicial Precedent) [1966] 1 WLR 1234 allowed the House of
Lords to depart from its own previous decisions "when it appears right to do so"; the power passed
to the Supreme Court. **A** attributes it to the wrong court. **C** is a substantial overstatement.
**D** confuses it with *Young*. **E** is unrelated — that is s.2 HRA 1998, which requires courts to
*take into account* Strasbourg jurisprudence, not follow it.

**7 — A.** ⚠ verify against the current Destination of Appeals Order and CPR 52. The general rule
is that an appeal from a district judge in the county court lies to a circuit judge. **B** applies
to appeals from a circuit judge. **C** applies to final decisions in Part 7 **multi-track** claims —
note the question specifies the fast track, which is the discriminator. **E** describes the
leapfrog procedure under the Administration of Justice Act 1969, which runs from the High Court.

**8 — C.** Paragraph 3.2 of the SRA Code of Conduct for Solicitors requires a service that is
competent and delivered in a timely manner. Competence is not something a client can waive, so
**A** fails. **D** misapplies Principle 7: acting in the client's best interests cannot mean acting
incompetently. **B** may be a legitimate route to competence, but stated flatly it is not the
governing obligation and the fee point is a distractor. **E** confuses insurance with competence.

**9 — B.** Distinguishing operates on material facts. If the facts of the present case differ in a
legally relevant respect, the earlier ratio does not apply. **A** is the classic wrong answer —
disagreeing with reasoning is not a ground to distinguish, and a lower court may not simply
decline to follow binding authority. **C**, **D** and **E** are all irrelevant to whether an
authority binds.

**10 — B.** Paragraph 1.4 of the Code of Conduct prohibits misleading the court, and Principle 1
(upholding the rule of law and the proper administration of justice) takes precedence over the
duty to the client where they conflict. Allowing an overruled authority to stand uncorrected
misleads the court. **A** and **C** invert the order of priority — the client cannot instruct a
solicitor to mislead the court. **D** is the subtle wrong answer: silence perpetuates the earlier
misleading statement. **E** is disproportionate; correction, not withdrawal, is the required step.
