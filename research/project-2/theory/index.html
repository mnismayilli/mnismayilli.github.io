Strategic delay: the model in detail

A two-period durable-goods model in which the good has a finite economic life and production gets cheaper over the product cycle. When does a monopolist introduce a product later than a social planner would — and which consumers pay for it?

Companion to the working paper Strategic delay of product introductions, with Subir Bose and Piercarlo Zanchettin. This page follows the structure of the seminar presentation, which you can read in full below; the paper contains the full proofs.

Two forces pulling against each other

A durable-good monopolist deciding when to introduce a product faces two effects that point in opposite directions.

  • Finite economic life. A unit bought late delivers fewer periods of service than the same unit bought early. Willingness to pay for the same physical good falls mechanically over the product cycle.
  • Declining marginal cost. Production technology improves within the product's life, so the monopolist can supply that same good more cheaply later than it could at launch.

Late service is worth less to the buyer, but late production is cheaper for the seller. Who should be served, and when? The question this paper asks is how the monopolist's answer departs from the planner's, under two different assumptions about what the firm can promise.

Over-delay — the object of the whole analysis. A type's consumption is over-delayed if postponing it to period 2 is optimal for the monopolist but sub-optimal for the planner.

The model

  • Two periods, \(t=\{1,2\}\). The good is perfectly durable, and its economic life ends after period 2.
  • Technology improves within the product cycle: \(c_{1} > c_{2} \ge 0\).
  • Two consumer types, \(V_{H} > V_{L}\), with relative masses \(1-\mu\) and \(\mu\). Consumers have unit demand, know their own type, and are forward-looking.
  • Technology and the distribution of types are common knowledge; the monopolist cannot observe an individual's type.

Timing of purchase is what makes the problem non-trivial. A type-\(J\) consumer compares

\[ \underbrace{2V_{J}-p_{1}}_{\text{buy at launch: two periods of service}} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \underbrace{V_{J}-p_{2}}_{\text{buy late: one period of service}} \]

so willingness to pay is \(2V_{J}\) in period 1 and only \(V_{J}\) in period 2. The decline is mechanical, not a taste for novelty.

The first-best benchmark

A benevolent planner with full information over types chooses, for each type \(J\), among three production–consumption paths.

PathDescriptionNet surplus
\(J_{12}\)serve at launch, two periods of service\(2V_{J}-c_{1}\)
\(J_{2}\)serve late, one period of service, cheaper\(V_{J}-c_{2}\)
\(J_{0}\)never serve\(0\)

Delay buys a cost saving \(c_{1}-c_{2}\) at the price of one period of service worth \(V_{J}\). So \(J_{2}\) beats \(J_{12}\) exactly when \(V_{J} < c_{1}-c_{2}\), and \(J_{2}\) beats \(J_{0}\) exactly when \(V_{J} > c_{2}\).

Condition (A) — significant cost saving

Delay is ever efficient only if \(c_{2} < V_{J} < c_{1}-c_{2}\), which is possible only when \[ c_{2} < c_{1}-c_{2}. \] If the cost saving is small, the \(J_{2}\) region vanishes: the planner serves immediately or never, and neither distortion below can arise.

Applying this to both types under (A) gives the two benchmarks the analysis uses:

ConditionFirst best
(A1)\(c_{2}<c_{1}-c_{2}<V_{L}<V_{H}\)both types served at launch
(A2)\(c_{2}<V_{L}<c_{1}-c_{2}<V_{H}\)H at launch, L late

Commitment: the low type is over-delayed

The monopolist announces \(\{p_{1},p_{2}\}\) at the start of period 1. Commitment here has two distinct parts:

  • it cannot renege on an announced second-period price — a promised discount is honoured; and
  • it commits not to offer any new contract in period 2 — a promise not to discount is also credible.

The second part is what makes the pattern \((H_{12},L_{0})\) — serve the high type at full price, never serve the low type — implementable at all. Without it, the high type anticipates a period-2 discount to the low type and refuses to pay \(2V_{H}\). This is the hinge on which the whole non-commitment section turns.

Proposition 2 — the high type is never over-delayed

Under commitment, over-delay of the high type's consumption cannot characterise the monopoly equilibrium. In particular the monopolist never delays the high type when \(V_{H} > c_{1}-c_{2} > c_{2}\).

The intuition is clean. Delaying the high type costs the monopolist that type's second period of service, worth \(V_{H}\) in revenue, and buys the cost saving \(c_{1}-c_{2}\) — which is exactly the planner's trade-off. The monopolist extracts the high type's full surplus either way, so private and social incentives coincide at the margin. Screening gives no extra reason to delay the top type, because nobody sits above it whose incentive constraint delay could relax.

Proposition 3 — intertemporal price discrimination

Over-delay of the low type characterises the monopoly equilibrium if and only if \[ V_{H} > V_{L} > c_{1}-c_{2} > c_{2}, \tag{A1} \] \[ \frac{V_{H}-V_{L}}{V_{H}-c_{2}} \;<\; \mu \;<\; \frac{V_{H}-V_{L}}{V_{H}-(c_{1}-c_{2})}. \tag{C1} \]

The condition on \(\mu\) is an interior interval, and the two ways of leaving it are instructive: the distortion is a knife-edge between two different corner solutions.

What does the distortion cost? Under (A1) the first best is \((H_{12},L_{12})\) while the monopolist implements \((H_{12},L_{2})\), so \[ \Delta W = \mu\bigl(V_{L}-(c_{1}-c_{2})\bigr) > 0. \]

Delay is the moderate distortion. The alternative — outright exclusion \((H_{12},L_{0})\) — costs \(\mu(2V_{L}-c_{1})\), which is strictly larger under (A1). The monopolist's screening instrument destroys less surplus than exclusion would.

Without commitment: the high type is over-delayed

Now let the monopolist offer a new contract in period 2. The first best is unchanged; only the firm's feasible set moves — and it does not grow. It shrinks by exactly one element.

\((H_{12},L_{0})\) is no longer implementable

Once the high type has bought at \(p_{1}=2V_{H}\), the monopolist strictly wants to offer \(p_{2}=V_{L}\) to the remaining low types whenever \(V_{L}>c_{2}\). The high type anticipates this and refuses to pay \(2V_{H}\).

That single deletion is what unlocks the second distortion. The comparison behind Proposition 2 is no longer available, so \((H_{2},L_{0})\) becomes a live candidate — and it is the only remaining way to serve the high type without a subsequent discount to the low type.

Proposition 4 — over-delay of the high type

Over-delay of the high type's consumption by one period characterises the monopoly equilibrium if and only if \[ V_{H} > c_{1}-c_{2} > V_{L} > c_{2}, \tag{A2} \] \[ \mu < \frac{(c_{1}-c_{2})-V_{L}}{c_{1}-2c_{2}}. \tag{C2} \] The equilibrium pattern is \((H_{2},L_{0})\): nobody is served at launch, and only the high type is served late.

(A2) is the case where the planner serves the high type at launch and the low type late. (C2) says the low type's mass is small; since \(c_{1}>2c_{2}\) and \(c_{1}-c_{2}>V_{L}\) under (A2), the bound is strictly positive.

Why waiting buys credibility

With commitment the monopolist would pick \((H_{12},L_{0})\): extract \(2V_{H}\) from the high type and promise never to discount. Without commitment that promise is empty as long as a period 2 exists in which the low type can be served. By waiting until period 2, the monopolist reaches a date after which no further contract is possible — the promise becomes credible by construction. The finite economic life is doing the work.

The firm weighs

\[ \underbrace{V_{H}-V_{L}}_{\text{rent saved on the high type}} \quad\text{against}\quad \underbrace{V_{H}}_{\text{one period of service lost}} -\underbrace{(c_{1}-c_{2})}_{\text{cost saved}} \]

so waiting pays only when the cost saving is large, and only when there are few low types to sell to in the meantime.

Welfare now falls short on two margins at once. Under (A2) the first best is \((H_{12},L_{2})\) and the monopolist implements \((H_{2},L_{0})\): \[ \Delta W=\underbrace{(1-\mu)\bigl(V_{H}-(c_{1}-c_{2})\bigr)}_{\text{high type served one period late}} \;+\;\underbrace{\mu(V_{L}-c_{2})}_{\text{low type excluded entirely}}\;>\;0 \] Both terms are strictly positive under (A2) — two distortions, against the single moderate one of the commitment regime.

Both distortions are phenomena of fast-improving production technology. A slow cost decline restores immediate-or-never service and neither result survives.

Where this sits in the literature

On the commitment side, the classic result is that intertemporal price discrimination is sub-optimal for a durable-goods monopolist (Coase, 1972; Stokey, 1979, 1981; Gul, Sonnenschein and Wilson, 1986) unless the firm can commit to its price path — via capacity (Bulow, 1982; McAfee and Wiseman, 2008), R&D obsolescence (Lee and Lee, 1998), endogenous quality (Moorthy and Png, 1992), or an inflow of new consumers (Besbes and Lobel, 2015). Here it arises in equilibrium instead.

Read as a damaged-goods problem, the second-period good is a perishable version of the first. The discrete-demand damaged-goods literature (Deneckere and McAfee, 1996; McAfee, 2007; Anderson and Dana, 2009) assumes the damaged good's marginal cost need not be lower, and so requires an increasing-percentage-difference condition on valuations. Significant future cost savings mean no such restriction is needed here.

Closest antecedent. Ortner (2017) obtains over-delay of the high type with stochastic marginal costs and an infinite horizon — the firm waits for high-cost states. The over-delay here does not require future costs to be high. It arises precisely because they will be low, and because the product dies: finite economic life substitutes for persistence in costs.

Throughout, the driver is the size of the future cost saving relative to valuations, not the shape of the valuation distribution — no increasing percentage differences, no log-supermodularity, and no violation of Spence–Mirrlees.
